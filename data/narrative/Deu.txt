=== intro
At the end of Numbers, Israel is camped on the plains of Moab, on the eastern side of the Jordan River, looking across at Jericho and the hills of Canaan beyond. Nearly forty years have passed since the exodus. The generation that left Egypt and refused to enter the land at Kadesh has died in the wilderness, all except Caleb and Joshua. Miriam and Aaron are dead. The kings Sihon and Og have been defeated, the tribes of Reuben and Gad and half of Manasseh have been given land east of the river, and Joshua has been commissioned to lead the people over. One thing remains before they cross. Moses, who will not go with them, has something to say.

Deuteronomy is that farewell. Almost the whole book is in Moses' own voice, a series of speeches delivered to the assembled nation in the eleventh month of the fortieth year, in the last weeks of his life. The book's Hebrew name comes from its opening line and means simply words: these are the words that Moses spoke to all Israel. The English title comes from the old Greek translation and means second law, a phrase drawn from a later chapter. It suits the book well, because Deuteronomy is not a new law so much as the law of Sinai spoken again, explained and pressed home to a new generation on the edge of a new life.

The speaker is old now, a hundred and twenty years of age, though the book's last chapter says his eyes were still clear. He has led this people out of Egypt, through the sea and the wilderness, and interceded for them again and again when God's anger burned against them. He is also a man under a sentence. Because of what happened at the waters of Meribah, he will see the land only from a mountaintop. Much of the force of the book comes from that situation: a leader who will not cross the river, speaking to a people who will.

Jewish and Christian tradition has long regarded Moses as the author of the book, as of the rest of the Torah, and the book itself says that Moses wrote down this law and gave it to the priests. The account of his death at the end was plainly added by another hand. Many modern scholars believe the book reached its final form centuries later, and they often connect it with the scroll of the law found in the temple in the days of King Josiah, a story told in Second Kings. These questions are debated, and the book itself presents its words as those of Moses on the plains of Moab.

The book is built around Moses' speeches. In the first, he looks back over the forty years, from Mount Sinai, which Deuteronomy usually calls Horeb, to the plains of Moab. He reminds the people of the failure at Kadesh and the victories east of the Jordan, and calls them to faithfulness. The second and longest speech begins with the Ten Commandments and with the great call to love the Lord with all the heart, soul and strength. It goes on to set out the laws that will shape Israel's life in the land: its worship at the one place God will choose, its festivals, its courts, its kings and prophets, its care for the poor and the stranger, its life in family and field. Then come blessings and curses, life and death set before the people, and a ceremony of covenant renewal.

The shape of the whole has often been compared with treaties made between kings and their subjects in the ancient Near East, which recalled the history between the parties, set out obligations, and closed with blessings and curses. Here the great king is the Lord himself, and the covenant is renewed with every generation that hears it.

The final chapters turn from speeches to farewell. Joshua is publicly charged to take Moses' place, the law is written down and entrusted to the priests, and Moses teaches the people a song and pronounces a blessing on each tribe. Then he climbs Mount Nebo, the Lord shows him the whole land, and he dies there in Moab.

Deuteronomy shaped the rest of the Old Testament more than almost any other book. The histories from Joshua to Kings measure Israel's kings by its standards, and the prophets echo its warnings. It also runs deep in the New Testament. When Jesus was tempted in the wilderness, each of his answers came from this book. The book of Joshua will take up the story where Deuteronomy ends, at the bank of the Jordan.

=== 1
The opening lines set the scene with care. These are the words Moses spoke to all Israel in the wilderness east of the Jordan. It is the first day of the eleventh month of the fortieth year, and Sihon and Og have already been defeated. A quiet note adds that it is only an eleven-day journey from Horeb to Kadesh-barnea. That journey has taken forty years.

Then Moses begins to speak, and he starts with history. He takes his listeners back to Horeb, when the Lord told them they had stayed at the mountain long enough and should go and take the land. He recalls how he appointed leaders and judges from every tribe, because the people had grown too many for him to carry alone, and charged them to judge fairly and fear no one.

Most of the chapter retells the story of Kadesh-barnea from Numbers, but now as Moses tells it, to the children of those who were there. It is a story of fear, refusal and loss, told by the one man who remembers every part of it.

=== 2
Moses' review of the past moves on from Kadesh, past the long years of wandering around the hill country of Seir, to the moment when the Lord told Israel to turn north. It covers ground already told in Numbers.

As the people travel up the east side of the Dead Sea, they pass the lands of three related peoples: Edom, descended from Jacob's brother Esau, and Moab and Ammon, descended from Abraham's nephew Lot. Each time the Lord forbids Israel to provoke them, because he has given these peoples their lands, just as he is giving Israel its own. Brief notes, almost like an ancient historian's asides, recall the giant peoples who lived in these regions before them.

At the Brook of Zered, the boundary of Moab, Moses marks a solemn milestone. Thirty-eight years have passed since Kadesh, and every man of fighting age from that generation has died. Only then does Israel's first battle on the road to the promised land begin. It is against Sihon, the Amorite king of Heshbon, who refuses them passage, and the victory is total.

=== 3
Moses continues the story of the victories east of the Jordan. After Sihon comes Og, king of Bashan, the fertile highland north of the Yarmuk valley, with its sixty fortified cities. Og is remembered as the last of the Rephaim, a people of great stature, and an aside mentions his enormous iron bed, still to be seen in the Ammonite city of Rabbah.

Moses then recalls how this conquered land, from the Arnon valley to Mount Hermon, was given to Reuben, Gad and half of Manasseh, on condition that their fighting men cross over and fight beside their brothers. He reminds Joshua that what the Lord did to these two kings he will do to every kingdom across the river.

The chapter closes on a deeply personal note. Moses tells the people that he begged the Lord to let him cross over and see the good land. The Lord refused and told him not to speak of it again, but to climb Pisgah and see it with his eyes, and to strengthen Joshua, who would go in his place. The last line places them exactly: in the valley opposite Beth-peor.

=== 4
Moses has finished his review of the journey from Horeb to the valley opposite Beth-peor. Now the history turns into an appeal, and this chapter brings his first speech to its close. Hear now, O Israel, he begins, and the tone changes from recollection to urgent instruction.

His appeal rests on what the people have seen with their own eyes, from the judgement at Baal-peor back to the day at Horeb when the mountain blazed with fire and the Lord spoke out of it. They heard a voice but saw no form, and from that he draws the heart of his warning: they must never make an idol in any shape at all. He looks far ahead, to a time when their descendants have lived in the land for generations, turned to idols and been scattered among the nations, and he promises that even there, if they seek the Lord with all their heart, they will find him.

After the speech, a short narrative note records Moses setting apart three cities of refuge east of the Jordan, as Numbers had required. Then the book introduces the second and longest of his speeches, the law itself, with a fresh description of where they are standing.

=== 5
Moses summons all Israel, and his second address begins. Its opening words are Hear, O Israel, and its first great subject is the covenant made at Horeb.

He makes a bold claim. The Lord did not make this covenant only with their fathers, he says, but with us, all of us who are alive here today. Most of his listeners were children or not yet born when the mountain burned, yet Moses speaks as though every one of them had stood there.

Then he repeats the Ten Commandments, first given in Exodus, the words God spoke from the fire and wrote on two tablets of stone. They are almost the same as before, with small differences. The most noticeable is the reason for the Sabbath. In Exodus it recalls God resting after creation. Here the people are told to remember that they were slaves in Egypt, and to let their own servants rest as they do.

The chapter ends by recalling the fear of the people at Horeb, when they begged Moses to go near and listen to God for them. The Lord approved their request, and that is why Moses now stands before them with the commandments he was given to teach.

=== 6
Moses has repeated the Ten Commandments and explained how he came to be the one who teaches them. Now he gives the words that have become the most treasured confession of the Jewish faith. It is called the Shema, after its first Hebrew word, meaning hear: Hear, O Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is one. It continues with the command to love the Lord with all the heart, soul and strength. Centuries later, when Jesus was asked which commandment was the greatest, this was his answer.

These words are to be on the people's hearts, taught to their children, spoken of at home and on the road, bound on hand and forehead and written on doorposts. Jewish practice came to take these instructions literally, with small boxes containing Scripture worn in prayer and fixed beside the door.

Moses also looks ahead to the danger of prosperity, when Israel lives in houses it did not build and eats from vineyards it did not plant, and forgets the Lord who brought it out of slavery. And he imagines a scene in a future home, a son asking his father what all these laws mean. The answer begins with a story: we were slaves of Pharaoh in Egypt.

=== 7
Moses has called Israel to love the Lord with all its heart. Now he turns to what that will mean when they cross the Jordan and meet the peoples already living there. He names seven nations of Canaan, among them the Hittites, Amorites, Canaanites and Jebusites, and describes them as larger and stronger than Israel.

His instructions are uncompromising. Israel is to make no treaty with them, not to intermarry, and to tear down their altars, smash their sacred pillars and burn their idols. The command to devote these peoples to destruction is one of the hardest passages in the Old Testament for modern readers,. Moses' own concern, repeated through the chapter, is that their gods will draw Israel away from the Lord.

At the centre of the chapter is a passage about why Israel was chosen at all. It was not because they were many, Moses says, for they were the fewest of peoples. It was because the Lord loved them and kept the oath he swore to their fathers. The chapter ends with encouragement for a people afraid of stronger enemies: remember what the Lord did to Pharaoh, and know that he will drive these nations out little by little.

=== 8
Moses turns from the enemies ahead to a quieter danger, and he does it by looking back over the forty years in the wilderness. Those years, he says, were a time of testing, when the Lord humbled his people to find out what was in their hearts. He let them go hungry and then fed them with manna, so that they would learn that man does not live on bread alone, but on every word that comes from the mouth of the Lord. Through all those years their clothes did not wear out and their feet did not swell.

When Jesus was tempted in the wilderness after forty days without food, this was the saying he quoted.

Then Moses describes the land they are about to enter, and the picture is full of water and harvest after the dry years: brooks and springs, wheat and barley, vines, figs and pomegranates, olive oil and honey, hills with iron and copper. The danger he sees is not hunger but plenty. When they have built fine houses and their flocks and silver have multiplied, they may come to believe that their own strength made them rich, and forget the God who led them through the wilderness.

=== 9
Hear, O Israel, Moses says again. The day is coming when they will cross the Jordan and face the Anakim, the people whose size frightened their parents at Kadesh. The Lord will go ahead of them like a consuming fire. But when the victory comes, Moses warns, they must not imagine it was their own righteousness that won it.

To make the point, he reminds them that they have been rebellious from the day they left Egypt, and he tells again the story of the golden calf, from his own point of view. While he was on the mountain for forty days and nights without food, receiving the tablets of the covenant written by the finger of God, the people below made a calf of molten metal. He describes coming down, seeing what they had done, and breaking the tablets before their eyes.

Moses reveals more of his own intercession than Exodus records: forty days and nights face down before the Lord, praying for the people and also for Aaron. He adds the names of other places where they provoked God, Taberah, Massah, Kibroth-hattaavah and Kadesh-barnea, and the chapter ends with the words of his prayer.

=== 10
Moses' memory of the golden calf continues to its resolution. After his prayer, the Lord told him to cut two new tablets of stone and make an ark of wood. God wrote the Ten Commandments again, and Moses placed the tablets in the ark, where, he says, they remain.

A brief note in the narrator's voice interrupts the speech with a few stages of Israel's travels and the death of Aaron, whose son Eleazar became priest in his place. It recalls that the Lord then set apart the tribe of Levi to carry the ark and to bless in his name, which is why Levi has no territory of its own.

Then Moses gathers up everything he has said in a single question: what does the Lord your God ask of you? His answer is to fear the Lord, walk in his ways, love him, and serve him with heart and soul. The God who owns the highest heavens chose Israel's fathers out of love, and he defends the fatherless and the widow and loves the foreigner. So Israel too must love the foreigner, since they were once foreigners in Egypt. The chapter closes with a contrast: seventy went down to Egypt, and now they are as many as the stars.

=== 11
This chapter brings the opening part of Moses' second speech to a close. Before he sets out the detailed laws, he presses once more on the people's memory and their choice.

He speaks directly to the adults in front of him, who, unlike their children, saw the plagues on Egypt, the Egyptian chariots swallowed by the sea, the years in the wilderness, and the earth opening beneath Dathan and Abiram. Then he paints a picture of the land they are entering, and he contrasts it with Egypt. In Egypt the fields were watered from the Nile by human labour, irrigated on foot like a vegetable garden. Canaan is a land of hills and valleys that drinks rain from heaven. Its harvests will depend on the autumn and spring rains, and the rains on the Lord, whose eyes are on that land all year round.

The chapter ends with a choice set out in the plainest terms, a blessing and a curse. Moses gives the people a task for when they enter the land: the blessing is to be proclaimed on Mount Gerizim and the curse on Mount Ebal, two mountains facing each other in the heart of Canaan, near Shechem, where Abraham built his first altar in the land.

=== 12
The long introduction is over, and Moses now begins the detailed laws that form the core of the book. They run from here to chapter twenty-six. These are the statutes and ordinances, he says, that you must follow all the days you live in the land.

The first subject is worship. The Canaanites worship their gods on hilltops and under green trees, at many local shrines, and Israel is to destroy them all. Instead, they are to bring their sacrifices, tithes and offerings to one place only, the place the Lord will choose from among their tribes to put his name. The chapter does not name the place. In time the tabernacle would stand at Shiloh, and centuries later the temple in Jerusalem would be understood as that chosen place.

Moses foresees practical questions. If the chosen place is far away, ordinary meat may be slaughtered and eaten at home, so long as the blood is poured out on the ground. But the holy offerings must be brought to the sanctuary, where families, servants and Levites will eat together and rejoice before the Lord. The chapter ends with a warning not to imitate the worship of the nations, which even includes the burning of children.

=== 13
Moses has insisted that Israel worship the Lord alone, in the place he will choose. Now he addresses the most dangerous threat to that loyalty: someone inside Israel who urges the people to follow other gods.

He considers three cases. The first is a prophet or dreamer of dreams who performs a sign or wonder that actually comes true, and then says, let us follow other gods. The miracle proves nothing, Moses says. The Lord is testing his people to see whether they love him with all their heart. The second is closer to home: a brother, a son or daughter, a beloved wife, or a closest friend who secretly entices someone to worship other gods. The third is a whole town that has been led astray.

In each case the penalty is death, and the language is severe. The chapter treats turning to other gods as treason against the King who had redeemed them from slavery, and it required careful investigation before any town was condemned. Loyalty to the Lord was to come before every other bond, even within the family.

=== 14
Having warned against anything that would draw Israel to other gods, Moses moves to the everyday practices that mark them out as a people belonging to the Lord. You are sons of the Lord your God, he begins, and you are a holy people.

First he forbids the mourning customs of their neighbours, cutting the body or shaving the forehead for the dead. Then comes a list of foods. Much of it repeats the laws of clean and unclean animals in Leviticus: land animals that both chew the cud and have a split hoof may be eaten, sea creatures with fins and scales, and birds other than those named. The pig, the camel and many birds of prey are forbidden. A short command not to cook a young goat in its mother's milk has puzzled interpreters for centuries.

The chapter closes with the tithe, a tenth of each year's harvest. It is to be eaten as a joyful feast at the place the Lord will choose, and if the journey is too far, it may be turned into money and spent there. Every third year, the tithe stays in the towns, to feed the Levite, the foreigner, the fatherless and the widow.

=== 15
The last chapter ended with the tithe that feeds the poor every third year. Now Moses turns to a larger rhythm in Israel's economic life, the cycle of seven years.

At the end of every seventh year, debts between Israelites are to be cancelled. Moses states the ideal, that there should be no poor among them if they obey the Lord, and a few lines later acknowledges that there will always be poor people in the land. The practical command is to open the hand to a brother in need, and not to refuse a loan because the year of release is close.

The same seven-year pattern applies to Hebrew servants, men or women who have sold themselves into service, usually because of debt. After six years they go free, and they are not to be sent away empty-handed but given generously from the flock, the threshing floor and the winepress. Again the reason is Israel's own history: remember that you were slaves in Egypt. A servant who chooses to stay may do so for life. The chapter closes with the firstborn of the herds and flocks, to be eaten before the Lord each year at the chosen place.

=== 16
Moses now sets out Israel's sacred year around three great festivals, each to be kept at the place the Lord will choose.

The first is Passover, in the month of Abib, the spring month in which the Lord brought Israel out of Egypt by night. It is followed by seven days of unleavened bread, the bread of affliction, eaten in memory of their hasty departure. The second is the Feast of Weeks, counted seven weeks from the beginning of the grain harvest. The third is the Feast of Tabernacles, kept for seven days after the harvest of grain and grapes has been gathered in. Three times a year, every man is to appear before the Lord, and no one is to come empty-handed.

What stands out in Deuteronomy's version of the feasts is the guest list. Again and again Moses names those who are to rejoice together: sons and daughters, servants, the Levite, the foreigner, the fatherless and the widow.

The chapter ends by beginning a new section of the laws, about the leaders who will govern Israel in the land. The first are judges and officials in every town, who must show no partiality and take no bribes. Justice, and justice alone, Moses says, they must pursue.

=== 17
Moses is setting out how Israel will be governed in the land, and after the local judges he turns to the hardest cases and the highest offices.

A charge of idolatry must be investigated thoroughly and can be decided only on the testimony of two or three witnesses, never just one. Cases too difficult for local judges are to be taken to the place the Lord will choose, to the Levitical priests and the judge serving at that time, whose verdict is final.

Then Moses looks ahead. One day, he says, Israel will want a king like all the nations around them. When that day comes, the king must be chosen by the Lord and must be an Israelite. He must not build up great numbers of horses, take many wives or pile up silver and gold. Instead he is to write out a copy of this law and read it all his life, so that he does not think himself above his brothers. Centuries later, Israel would ask for a king, and the reign of Solomon would show what happened when these limits were ignored. It is from the phrase a copy of this law, in the old Greek translation, that the book takes its English name.

=== 18
After judges and kings, Moses turns to the other figures who will speak and act for God in Israel: the priests and the prophets.

The priests and the whole tribe of Levi will have no territory of their own. The Lord is their inheritance. They live from their share of the sacrifices, the firstfruits and the first wool.

Then Moses warns against the ways the nations of Canaan sought guidance from their gods: divination, omens, sorcery, spells, mediums and consulting the dead, and the sacrifice of children in the fire. Israel is not to listen to such voices. Instead the Lord will raise up for them a prophet like Moses from among their own people, and they must listen to him. This, he says, is God's answer to their plea at Horeb not to hear his voice directly. Later Israel waited for such a prophet, and in the book of Acts the apostle Peter applies these words to Jesus. The chapter ends with a simple test for anyone who claims to speak in God's name: if what he says does not come true, the Lord has not spoken it.

=== 19
Moses has spoken of judges, kings, priests and prophets. Now the laws turn to the protection of life and of justice in the courts.

He begins with the cities of refuge. Moses himself has already set apart three east of the Jordan, and here he tells Israel to choose three more in the land itself once it is settled, with roads built so that anyone who has killed someone by accident can reach one quickly. His example comes from ordinary life: two men cutting timber in a forest, when an axe head flies off its handle. The avenger of blood, the relative whose duty was to pursue a killer, must not be allowed to kill someone who meant no harm. But a deliberate murderer who flees to such a city is to be handed back.

Two shorter laws follow. No one may move a neighbour's boundary stone, the marker of a family's inheritance. And no one may be convicted on the word of a single witness. A witness who lies is to suffer what he intended for the accused. The chapter closes with the principle of life for life, eye for eye, tooth for tooth, which in its setting limited punishment to match the offence and go no further.

=== 20
The laws now turn to war, a reality Israel will face as soon as it crosses the Jordan. Moses begins with fear. When the army sees horses and chariots and a force larger than its own, a priest is to remind them that the Lord who brought them out of Egypt goes with them.

Then the officers are to send certain men home: a man who has built a house and not yet dedicated it, one who has planted a vineyard and not yet enjoyed its fruit, one engaged to a woman he has not yet married, and finally anyone who is simply afraid, so that his fear does not spread. Israel's army is to rely on the Lord more than on its size.

The chapter distinguishes between distant cities, which are first to be offered peace, and the cities of the nations of Canaan, which are to be devoted to destruction, the hard command already given in chapter seven. It ends with an unusual law about sieges. Fruit trees around a besieged city must not be cut down. Are the trees of the field people, Moses asks, that you should besiege them?

=== 21
The laws move quickly from subject to subject here, but many of them share a concern for life, death and the guilt of innocent blood.

The chapter opens with an unsolved killing. A body is found in open country and no one knows who did it. The elders of the nearest town must take part in a solemn ceremony by a running stream, washing their hands over a heifer and declaring their innocence, so that the guilt of innocent blood does not rest on the land.

Then come laws about households. A woman taken captive in war who is to become a wife must be given a month to mourn her parents, and if she is later sent away she cannot be sold as a slave. A man with two wives may not take the rights of the firstborn from the son of the wife he loves less. Parents with a persistently rebellious son must bring him before the elders of the town.

The chapter ends with the body of an executed man hung on a tree, which must be buried the same day, because anyone hung on a tree is under God's curse. The apostle Paul would quote these words in his letter to the Galatians when writing of the death of Christ.

=== 22
The laws continue with a wide variety of everyday situations, and the opening examples show the spirit of many of them. If you see a neighbour's ox or sheep straying, or his donkey fallen on the road, you must not look away. Even a bird's nest by the road is covered: the young may be taken, but the mother bird is to go free.

A run of short commands follows. A new house must have a railing round its flat roof, where people in that world often worked and gathered, so that no one falls. Men and women must not wear each other's clothing. Certain things must not be mixed: two kinds of seed in a vineyard, an ox and a donkey under one yoke, wool and linen in one garment. Tassels are to be worn on the corners of the cloak, as Numbers also commanded.

The rest of the chapter deals with marriage and sexual conduct, including a husband who falsely accuses his new wife, adultery, and the violation of a young woman. Some of these laws are severe, but they repeatedly protect a woman from a false charge or from blame where she could not resist.

=== 23
The laws now turn to who may belong to the assembly of the Lord, the gathered people of Israel at worship. Certain people are excluded, and the reasons given for two of them reach back into Israel's recent history. Ammonites and Moabites may not enter, because they did not meet Israel with food and water on the journey, and because Moab hired Balaam to curse them. Yet Edomites are not to be despised, because they are Israel's brothers, descended from Esau, and Egyptians are not to be despised, because Israel once lived as foreigners in their land. The book of Ruth will later tell of a Moabite woman who joined herself to Israel's God and became an ancestor of King David.

Varied laws follow. The army's camp must be kept clean, because the Lord walks through it. A slave who escapes from a foreign master and takes refuge in Israel must not be handed back. Israelites are not to charge one another interest. Vows to the Lord must be kept promptly.

The chapter ends with a small law of neighbourliness. A traveller passing through a vineyard or grainfield may eat his fill, but not fill a basket or put a sickle to the grain.

=== 24
The laws continue, many of them concerned with protecting people who could easily be harmed. The chapter begins with divorce. It regulates divorce, requiring a written certificate, and forbidding a man to remarry a woman he has divorced once she has married another. Jesus would be questioned about this passage, and his answer is recorded in the Gospels.

A newly married man is to be free from military service for a year. A millstone may not be taken as security for a loan, because a family needs it to make bread every day. Kidnapping an Israelite is a crime punishable by death. Moses reminds the people of what happened to Miriam when he speaks of skin disease.

Then come laws for the poor and the vulnerable. A lender must not walk into a borrower's house to take security, and a poor man's cloak must be returned by nightfall, so that he can sleep in it. Wages must be paid before sunset. Children are not to be put to death for their parents' sins. At harvest time, a forgotten sheaf is to be left in the field, and olive trees and vines are not to be stripped bare, so that the foreigner, the fatherless and the widow can glean.

=== 25
Moses' collection of laws is drawing toward its end. This chapter begins in the law court. A guilty man may be flogged, but never more than forty lashes, so that he is not degraded in the eyes of his neighbours. A single line follows: do not muzzle an ox while it treads out the grain.

Then comes a family law that plays an important part in later stories. If a man dies without a son, his brother is to marry the widow, and her first son will carry on the dead man's name. A brother who refuses must face a public ceremony of shame at the town gate. The custom lies behind the story of Tamar in Genesis and the story of Ruth and Boaz.

The chapter also requires honest weights and measures, since a merchant with one weight for buying and another for selling is detestable to the Lord. It ends by recalling the Amalekites, who attacked the weary stragglers at the back of Israel's column on the way out of Egypt. Israel is not to forget it. The command will return in the time of King Saul.

=== 26
This chapter brings the long collection of laws to a close, and it ends not with a rule but with worship.

Moses describes a ceremony for the first harvest in the land. A farmer is to bring a basket of the first fruits of his soil to the place the Lord will choose, hand it to the priest and recite a short confession that tells Israel's whole story in a few lines. It begins: my father was a wandering Aramean. The father is Jacob, who lived for years in Aram and went down into Egypt with a small family. The confession goes on through slavery, the cry for help, the mighty hand that brought them out, and the gift of the land, and ends, here are the firstfruits you have given me.

A second declaration follows for the third year, when the tithe is given to the Levite, the foreigner, the fatherless and the widow. The worshipper declares that he has obeyed, and asks God to look down from heaven and bless his people and the land.

The chapter closes with a solemn exchange. Today, Moses says, you have declared that the Lord is your God, and today the Lord has declared that you are his treasured people. The covenant is being renewed on the plains of Moab.

=== 27
The laws have been given, and the covenant declared. Now the speech pauses, and the narrative steps in. Moses is joined first by the elders of Israel and then by the Levitical priests, and together they give instructions for a ceremony to be held once the people have crossed the Jordan.

It is to take place in the heart of Canaan, at the two mountains Moses mentioned earlier, Ebal and Gerizim, which face each other across the valley of Shechem. On Mount Ebal, Israel is to set up large stones coated with plaster and write on them all the words of this law. They are to build an altar of uncut stones and offer sacrifices there, eating and rejoicing before the Lord. The book of Joshua records the day it was done.

Six tribes are to stand on Gerizim for the blessing, and six on Ebal for the curse. Then the Levites are to call out a series of twelve curses, each answered by the whole people with a single word: Amen. Many of them name sins committed in secret, an idol hidden away, a boundary stone moved, a blind man led astray, a neighbour struck down unseen, and the last sums up all the rest.

=== 28
Moses resumes his speech, and sets out the consequences of the covenant in the starkest terms the Torah contains. Treaties in the ancient Near East commonly closed with blessings for loyalty and curses for betrayal, and this chapter has that shape.

It begins with blessing. If Israel obeys, they will be blessed in the city and in the country, in children, crops and flocks, in coming in and going out. The rain will fall in its season, enemies will flee, and Israel will lend to many nations and borrow from none.

Then the tone darkens, and the curses run on far longer than the blessings: disease, drought, defeat and humiliation. As the chapter goes on, it describes a nation from far away, speaking an unknown language, besieging Israel's cities until the people suffer the worst horrors of famine, and finally Israel scattered among the nations, serving gods of wood and stone. The last line imagines them going back to Egypt in ships, offering themselves as slaves, with no one willing to buy.

Later generations read these words with grief, because the histories and prophets record sieges and exile at the hands of Assyria and Babylon.

=== 29
The blessings and curses have been set out. Now a new heading marks what many readers regard as Moses' third address: these are the words of the covenant made with Israel in the land of Moab, in addition to the covenant made at Horeb.

Moses summons all Israel and begins, once more, with what they have seen: the signs in Egypt, the forty years in which their clothes and sandals did not wear out, the defeat of Sihon and Og. Yet, he says, to this day the Lord has not given them a mind to understand or eyes to see.

Then he describes the people standing before him: leaders, elders and officials, men, wives and children, even the foreigners who cut their wood and draw their water. All of them are entering the covenant today. And it is being made, he adds, not only with those present but also with those who are not here today, the generations still to come. He warns of the person who hears the covenant's curses and quietly assures himself that all will be well, and imagines future nations looking at a ruined land and asking why. The chapter ends with a single, often quoted sentence about the secret things and the things revealed.

=== 30
Moses has warned that Israel may one day be uprooted from the land and cast into another. Now he looks beyond that disaster. When all these things have happened, he says, and the people remember his words among the nations where they have been scattered, and return to the Lord with all their heart, the Lord will have compassion on them and gather them from the farthest horizon. He will circumcise their hearts, so that they love him and live.

Then he speaks to the people in front of him. The commandment, he says, is not too difficult or far away. It is not up in heaven or beyond the sea, so that someone must fetch it. The word is very near you, in your mouth and in your heart. The apostle Paul would take up these words in his letter to the Romans.

Then Moses brings the whole covenant to a point. He calls heaven and earth as witnesses. He has set before them life and death, blessing and curse. And he gives one of the most famous appeals in the Old Testament: choose life, so that you and your descendants may live, loving the Lord, obeying him and holding fast to him, for he is your life.

=== 31
The speeches are finished, and the book turns to a leader's farewell. Moses tells the people he is a hundred and twenty years old and that the Lord has told him he will not cross the Jordan. The Lord himself will go before them, and so will Joshua.

In front of all Israel, Moses calls Joshua forward and charges him with words that will echo through the opening of the next book: be strong and courageous. Moses writes down the law and entrusts it to the Levitical priests who carry the ark, and to the elders. Every seven years, at the Feast of Tabernacles, it is to be read aloud to the whole people, so that each new generation will hear it.

Then the Lord appears in the pillar of cloud at the Tent of Meeting, where Moses and Joshua stand together, and the mood darkens. The Lord tells Moses that after his death the people will turn to other gods and break the covenant. He gives Moses a song to teach them, which will stand as a witness against them in the troubles to come. Moses gives the Book of the Law to the Levites to place beside the ark, gathers the leaders, and begins to recite the song.

=== 32
Moses has gathered the elders and officers of the tribes, and now, with Joshua beside him, he recites the song the Lord gave him. It is a long poem, meant to be remembered and sung by generations who will need it.

It opens by calling heaven and earth to listen. Its central image is God as the Rock, faithful, just and unchanging, and the word returns again and again. The song tells Israel's story as a parable. The Lord found his people in a barren, howling wilderness, guarded them as the apple of his eye, and carried them like an eagle bearing its young on its wings. He brought them into a rich land. Then Jeshurun, a poetic name for Israel meaning the upright one, grew fat and kicked, and turned to other gods.

What follows is judgement, described in fierce poetry, and then a turn. When his people's strength is gone, the Lord will have compassion, and he will show that there is no god besides him.

After the song Moses urges the people to take its words to heart, because they are their life. That same day the Lord tells him to climb Mount Nebo, in the Abarim range, to see the land and die there.

=== 33
Moses has been told to climb Mount Nebo and die. Before he goes, he gives his last words to the people, and like Jacob on his deathbed in Genesis, he blesses the tribes of Israel one by one. The chapter introduces him with a title later given to prophets such as Samuel and Elijah: Moses, the man of God.

The blessing is framed by poetry about the Lord himself. It opens with God coming from Sinai, dawning from Seir and shining from Mount Paran, and it speaks of the Lord becoming king over his people when the tribes gathered.

Then each tribe receives its word, and set beside Jacob's blessings in Genesis there are striking differences. Levi, condemned by Jacob for violence, is now honoured as the tribe that keeps God's covenant, teaches his law and serves at his altar. Joseph receives the richest blessing of all, a land full of the dew of heaven, the yield of the seasons and the favour of the one who dwelt in the burning bush. Simeon is not mentioned at all.

The final lines are a song of praise to the God of Israel, and they contain one of the best-loved images in Scripture: the eternal God is your dwelling place, and underneath are the everlasting arms.

=== 34
His last words spoken, Moses leaves the plains of Moab for the last time and climbs Mount Nebo, to the top of Pisgah, the heights that look across the Jordan toward Jericho.

From there the Lord shows him the whole land. The description sweeps around it: north to Gilead and Dan, across the territories that will belong to Naphtali, Ephraim and Manasseh, west to Judah and the Mediterranean, south to the Negev, and down to the valley of Jericho, the city of palms. This is the land, the Lord says, that I swore to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. Moses sees it with his own eyes, but he will not cross over.

What follows is told by a later voice, looking back on Moses. It records his death in Moab, at a hundred and twenty, his eyes still clear and his strength undiminished, and his burial by the Lord himself in a grave whose place no one knows. Israel mourns him for thirty days. Joshua, on whom Moses laid his hands, is now filled with the spirit of wisdom, and the people follow him. The last words are a tribute. Never since has a prophet arisen in Israel like Moses, whom the Lord knew face to face.
