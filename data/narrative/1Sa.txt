=== intro
The book of Judges ended with a refrain that hung in the air like an unanswered question: in those days there was no king in Israel. The book of Ruth ended with a name, David, the great-grandson of a Moabite widow and a farmer from Bethlehem. First Samuel brings those two threads together. It tells how Israel moved from the loose federation of tribes we have seen under the judges to a kingdom with a crown, and how the crown passed, by a long and painful road, from the first king to the man God had chosen.

First and Second Samuel were originally one book in the Hebrew Bible. They were divided later, when the Scriptures were translated into Greek and the long scroll was split in two. The book is named after Samuel, its great figure in the early chapters, but Samuel cannot have written all of it, since his death is recorded halfway through. The author is unknown. Later Scripture mentions records kept by Samuel and by the prophets Nathan and Gad, and the book may draw on sources like these, but who gave it its final form, and when, we cannot say.

The events belong roughly to the eleventh century before Christ. Israel's tribes are settled in the hill country on both sides of the Jordan, but they are under growing pressure. The Philistines, whom we met in the days of Samson, now dominate the coastal plain from their five cities and push steadily into the hills. They are well organised, well armed, and they control the working of iron. Israel has no army beyond whatever the tribes can muster, and its leadership has grown weak. At Shiloh, where the tabernacle stands, the old priest Eli presides, but his sons, who serve as priests, are corrupt.

Three lives dominate the book. The first is Samuel himself. He is born in answer to a mother's prayer, given to the Lord as a small child, and raised at Shiloh. He becomes a prophet, a priest-like leader at Israel's altars, and the last of the judges. Samuel is a bridge between two eras. He is the one who warns Israel what a king will cost them, and he is the one who anoints, first, Saul, and then David.

The second is Saul, a tall and impressive young man from the tribe of Benjamin, from the town of Gibeah. His early days as king are full of promise. But Saul is a troubled figure, impatient, insecure and unwilling to obey, and the book traces his decline with something close to tragedy: his rejection by God, his loss of the Spirit, his jealous rage, and finally his despair.

The third is David, the youngest son of Jesse of Bethlehem, a shepherd boy anointed in secret while Saul still reigns. He enters the story as a musician to soothe the king, steps into the open as the champion who faces the Philistine giant Goliath, and becomes Saul's most successful commander, the close friend of Saul's son Jonathan and the husband of his daughter Michal. Then Saul turns against him, and for the second half of the book David lives as a fugitive, hiding in the wilderness of Judah, gathering a band of outcasts, even taking refuge among the Philistines. Twice he has the chance to kill Saul, and twice he refuses to lift his hand against the Lord's anointed.

Behind these lives the book raises the question of what kind of king Israel should have. The people's demand for a king, so that they can be like the other nations, is treated as a rejection of God's own rule. Yet God gives them a king, and kingship itself becomes part of his purpose. The difference lies in the heart of the man who holds the throne. That is the contrast the book draws between Saul and David, though it is honest about David's failings too, and his gravest ones still lie ahead.

The book also has its moments of tenderness and surprise: Hannah's song of praise, the boy Samuel hearing his name called in the night, the ark of God captured by the Philistines and sent back, the friendship of David and Jonathan, and the wise intervention of a woman named Abigail. Its final scenes are dark. Samuel dies. Saul, abandoned and afraid before a last battle with the Philistines, seeks out a medium at Endor. The book ends on the slopes of Mount Gilboa, with the king and his sons dead and the Philistines victorious. Second Samuel will open with David hearing the news.

=== 1
The book begins, as several great stories in the Bible begin, with a woman who cannot have children. Her name is Hannah, and she is one of the two wives of a man named Elkanah, from Ramathaim-zophim, usually shortened to Ramah, in the hill country of Ephraim. His other wife, Peninnah, has children, and she uses that to torment Hannah year after year.

Every year the family travels to Shiloh, a day or so's walk to the north, to worship and offer sacrifice at the tabernacle. This is still the time of the judges, and Shiloh is still Israel's central sanctuary. The old priest there is Eli, and his two sons, Hophni and Phinehas, also serve as priests. The listener is told their names in the opening verses, because they will matter.

Elkanah loves Hannah, and he cannot understand why he is not enough for her. One year at Shiloh, after the sacrificial meal, Hannah goes to the sanctuary to pray, weeping, while Eli sits watching by the doorpost. Her prayer includes a vow that echoes the story of Samson: if God gives her a son, no razor will touch his head, and the boy will belong to the Lord for life. Eli's first reaction to her is a misunderstanding, but his last word to her is a blessing.

=== 2
Hannah has kept her vow. Once her son Samuel was weaned, probably at around three years old, she brought him to Shiloh and gave him to the Lord's service, under the care of Eli. Now she prays again, but this time with joy.

Her prayer is a song, and it reaches far beyond her own story. It celebrates a God who turns the world upside down, who breaks the bows of the mighty, feeds the hungry, lifts the poor from the dust and gives children to the barren. It ends with a word that is surprising in a book that opens without a king: the Lord will give strength to his king and exalt the horn of his anointed. Many centuries later Mary's song in the Gospel of Luke would echo Hannah's.

Then the chapter alternates between two pictures. On one side are Eli's sons, Hophni and Phinehas, who treat the sacrifices with contempt, take the best meat by force, and sleep with the women who serve at the entrance to the tabernacle. On the other is the boy Samuel, ministering before the Lord in a small linen ephod, with a new robe each year from his mother. Eli rebukes his sons, but too mildly and too late. The chapter ends with an unnamed man of God bringing a solemn message of judgement against Eli's house.

=== 3
The boy Samuel is growing up at Shiloh, serving before the Lord under the old priest Eli. The chapter opens with a sentence that describes the whole era: in those days the word of the Lord was rare, and visions were few. Eli is nearly blind now. It is night, and Samuel is lying down in the sanctuary, where the ark of God is kept, before the lamp of God has gone out.

Then the Lord calls the boy by name. Samuel does not yet know the Lord's voice, because the Lord has not yet spoken to him, and the scene that follows is told with gentle repetition. Each time he runs to Eli. It is the old man, so dull in many ways, who finally understands what is happening and tells the boy how to answer.

The first message Samuel receives is a hard one, a confirmation of the judgement already spoken against Eli's household. In the morning Samuel is afraid to tell him. Eli's response, when the boy finally tells him everything, is one of quiet resignation. The chapter ends with Samuel recognised throughout Israel, from Dan to Beersheba, as a prophet of the Lord. The silence of heaven has been broken.

=== 4
Samuel's word is now heard throughout Israel, but the next scene leaves him offstage entirely. War breaks out with the Philistines. Israel camps at a place called Ebenezer, and the Philistines at Aphek, on the edge of the coastal plain, near the route that led up into the hill country. The first battle goes badly, and Israel loses about four thousand men.

The elders' response reveals how far Israel has drifted. Rather than seeking the Lord, they send to Shiloh for the ark of the covenant, as though it were a charm that could guarantee victory. They remember that the ark once went before Israel across the Jordan and around Jericho, but they forget the God whose presence it represented. Eli's two sons, Hophni and Phinehas, come with it into the camp, and the great shout that greets it terrifies the Philistines, who have heard of the God who struck Egypt.

The judgement spoken against Eli's house is about to fall. The second half of the chapter moves back to Shiloh, where Eli, ninety-eight years old and blind, sits by the road, trembling not for his sons but for the ark, waiting for news. A runner from the tribe of Benjamin arrives with dust on his head.
