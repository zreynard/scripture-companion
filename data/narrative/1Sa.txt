=== intro
The book of Judges ended with a refrain that hung in the air like an unanswered question: in those days there was no king in Israel. The book of Ruth ended with a name, David, the great-grandson of a Moabite widow and a farmer from Bethlehem. First Samuel brings those two threads together. It tells how Israel moved from the loose federation of tribes we have seen under the judges to a kingdom with a crown, and how the crown passed, by a long and painful road, from the first king to the man God had chosen.

First and Second Samuel were originally one book in the Hebrew Bible. They were divided later, when the Scriptures were translated into Greek and the long scroll was split in two. The book is named after Samuel, its great figure in the early chapters, and his death is recorded well before the story of First Samuel is over. Later Scripture says that the acts of David were written in the records of Samuel the seer, Nathan the prophet and Gad the seer, and Jewish tradition holds that these three men gave us this book.

The events belong to the eleventh century before Christ. Israel's tribes are settled in the hill country on both sides of the Jordan, but they are under growing pressure. The Philistines, whom we met in the days of Samson, now dominate the coastal plain from their five cities and push steadily into the hills. They are well organised, well armed, and they control the working of iron. Israel has no army beyond whatever the tribes can muster, and its leadership has grown weak. At Shiloh, where the tabernacle stands, the old priest Eli presides, but his sons, who serve as priests, are corrupt.

Three lives dominate the book. The first is Samuel himself. He is born in answer to a mother's prayer, given to the Lord as a small child, and raised at Shiloh. He becomes a prophet, offers sacrifice at Israel's altars, and is the last of the judges. Samuel is a bridge between two eras. He is the one who warns Israel what a king will cost them, and he is the one who anoints, first, Saul, and then David.

The second is Saul, a tall and impressive young man from the tribe of Benjamin, from the town of Gibeah. His early days as king are full of promise. But Saul is a troubled figure, impatient, insecure and unwilling to obey, and the book traces his decline with something close to tragedy: his rejection by God, his loss of the Spirit, his jealous rage, and finally his despair.

The third is David, the youngest son of Jesse of Bethlehem, a shepherd boy anointed in his father's house while Saul still reigns. He enters Saul's court as a musician to soothe the king, steps into the open as the champion who faces the Philistine giant Goliath, and becomes Saul's most successful commander, the close friend of Saul's son Jonathan and the husband of his daughter Michal. Then Saul turns against him, and for the second half of the book David lives as a fugitive, hiding in the wilderness of Judah, gathering a band of outcasts, even taking refuge among the Philistines. Twice he has the chance to kill Saul, and twice he refuses to lift his hand against the Lord's anointed.

Behind these lives the book raises the question of what kind of king Israel should have. The people's demand for a king, so that they can be like the other nations, is treated as a rejection of God's own rule. Yet God gives them a king, and kingship itself becomes part of his purpose. The difference lies in the heart of the man who holds the throne. That is the contrast the book draws between Saul and David, though it is honest about David's failings too, and his gravest ones still lie ahead.

The book also has its moments of tenderness and surprise: Hannah's song of praise, the boy Samuel hearing his name called in the night, the ark of God captured by the Philistines and sent back, the friendship of David and Jonathan, and the wise intervention of a woman named Abigail. Its final scenes are dark. Samuel dies. Saul, abandoned and afraid before a last battle with the Philistines, seeks out a medium at Endor. The book ends on the slopes of Mount Gilboa, with the king and his sons dead and the Philistines victorious. Second Samuel will open with David hearing the news.

=== 1
The book begins, as several great stories in the Bible begin, with a woman who cannot have children. Her name is Hannah, and she is one of the two wives of a man named Elkanah, from Ramathaim-zophim, usually shortened to Ramah, in the hill country of Ephraim. His other wife, Peninnah, has children, and she uses that to torment Hannah year after year.

Every year the family goes up to Shiloh to worship and offer sacrifice at the tabernacle. This is still the time of the judges, and Shiloh is still Israel's central sanctuary. The old priest there is Eli, and his two sons, Hophni and Phinehas, also serve as priests. The listener is told their names in the opening verses, because they will matter.

Elkanah loves Hannah and gives her a double portion, and he asks her gently whether he is not better to her than ten sons. One year at Shiloh, after the sacrificial meal, Hannah goes to the sanctuary to pray, weeping, while Eli sits watching by the doorpost. Her prayer includes a vow that echoes the story of Samson: if God gives her a son, no razor will touch his head, and the boy will belong to the Lord for life. Eli's first reaction to her is a misunderstanding, but his last word to her is a blessing.

=== 2
Hannah has kept her vow. Once her son Samuel was weaned, she brought him to Shiloh and gave him to the Lord's service, under the care of Eli. Now she prays again, but this time with joy.

Her prayer is a song that reaches far beyond her own story. It celebrates a God who turns the world upside down, who breaks the bows of the mighty, feeds the hungry, lifts the poor from the dust and gives children to the barren. It ends with a word that is surprising in a book that opens without a king: the Lord will give strength to his king and exalt the horn of his anointed. Many centuries later Mary's song in the Gospel of Luke would echo Hannah's.

The chapter then alternates between two pictures. On one side are Eli's sons, Hophni and Phinehas, who treat the sacrifices with contempt, take the best meat by force, and sleep with the women who serve at the entrance to the tabernacle. On the other is the boy Samuel, ministering before the Lord in a small linen ephod, with a new robe each year from his mother. Eli rebukes his sons, but too mildly and too late. Then an unnamed man of God arrives with a solemn message of judgement against Eli's house.

=== 3
The boy Samuel is growing up at Shiloh, serving before the Lord under the old priest Eli. The chapter opens with a sentence that describes the whole era: in those days the word of the Lord was rare, and visions were few. Eli's eyes have grown so dim that he can no longer see. It is night, and Samuel is lying down in the sanctuary, where the ark of God is kept, before the lamp of God has gone out.

Then the Lord calls the boy by name. Samuel does not yet know the Lord's voice, because the Lord has not yet spoken to him, and the scene that follows is told with gentle repetition. Each time he runs to Eli. At the third call it is the old priest who understands what is happening and tells the boy how to answer.

The first message Samuel receives is a hard one, a confirmation of the judgement already spoken against Eli's household. In the morning Samuel is afraid to tell him. Eli's response, when the boy finally tells him everything, is one of quiet resignation. By the close, Samuel is recognised throughout Israel, from Dan to Beersheba, as a prophet of the Lord. The silence of heaven has been broken.

=== 4
Samuel's word is now heard throughout Israel, but the next scene leaves him offstage entirely. War breaks out with the Philistines. Israel camps at a place called Ebenezer, and the Philistines at Aphek, on the edge of the coastal plain, near the route that led up into the hill country. The first battle goes badly, and Israel loses about four thousand men.

The elders' response reveals how far Israel has drifted. Rather than seeking the Lord, they send to Shiloh for the ark of the covenant, as though it were a charm that could guarantee victory. The ark had once gone before Israel across the Jordan and around Jericho, but now it is sent for like a weapon, with no word from the God whose presence it represented. Eli's two sons, Hophni and Phinehas, come with it into the camp, and the great shout that greets it terrifies the Philistines, who have heard of the God who struck Egypt.

The judgement spoken against Eli's house is about to fall. The second half of the chapter moves back to Shiloh, where Eli, ninety-eight years old and blind, sits by the road watching, his heart trembling for the ark, waiting for news. A runner from the tribe of Benjamin arrives with dust on his head.

=== 5
The ark of the covenant has been captured. Eli and both his sons are dead, and Phinehas's widow has died giving birth to a son she named Ichabod, meaning the glory has departed. In Israel it seems that God himself has been defeated.

The Philistines certainly think so. They carry the ark from the battlefield to Ashdod, one of their five chief cities, near the coast, and set it in the temple of their god Dagon, beside his statue. In the ancient world this was what victors did with the gods of a conquered people: they placed them as trophies before their own god, as a sign of who was stronger.

But this chapter is about the God of Israel acting alone, without priests, without an army, and in enemy territory. The story is told with grim humour. Dagon's statue is found face down before the ark, as if bowing. Then a plague breaks out among the people of Ashdod. The Philistine rulers send the ark on to Gath, and then on to Ekron, and the same thing happens in each city. The capture that seemed to prove Israel's God powerless has become a disaster for his captors.

=== 6
The ark has been in Philistine territory for seven months, passed from city to city, leaving plague behind it. The Philistine leaders now want only to be rid of it, and they ask their priests and diviners how to send it home safely.

The advice they receive is careful and, in its own way, respectful. The ark must not go back empty. It must be sent with a guilt offering, gold images of the tumours and of the rats that have ravaged the land, one for each of the five Philistine cities. The priests even recall what happened to Pharaoh when he hardened his heart. And they devise a test. Two milk cows that have never been yoked will pull the cart, while their calves are penned up at home. If the cows leave their calves and go straight up the road toward Israel, the disaster came from Israel's God. If not, it was chance.

The road leads east into the Sorek Valley, the same valley where Samson once met Delilah, toward Beth-shemesh, an Israelite town where the people are harvesting wheat. Their joy at the ark's return is great, but the chapter ends on a sombre note, with the people of Beth-shemesh asking who can stand before this holy God, and sending word to Kiriath-jearim to come and take the ark away.

=== 7
The ark has come to rest at Kiriath-jearim, a hill town on the border between Judah and Benjamin, in the house of a man named Abinadab, whose son Eleazar is set apart to guard it. It will remain there for a long time. Twenty years pass, and Israel mourns and longs for the Lord.

Samuel has been absent from the story since the battle at Aphek. Now he steps forward again, as a grown man and Israel's leader. His message is direct: if you are truly returning to the Lord, put away the foreign gods and the Ashtoreths, and serve him only. The people listen, and Samuel calls all Israel to gather at Mizpah, in the hill country of Benjamin, to fast and confess their sins.

The Philistines hear of the assembly and see a threat. As they march up, Samuel is offering a sacrifice and crying out to the Lord. The outcome is the reverse of the disaster at Aphek, and Samuel marks the victory with a stone, which he names Ebenezer, the stone of help. A brief summary then sketches Samuel's long years of leadership as a judge, travelling on a yearly circuit from Bethel to Gilgal to Mizpah, and home to Ramah.

=== 8
Years have passed. Samuel is old, and he has made his sons, Joel and Abijah, judges in Beersheba, in the far south. But they take bribes and pervert justice, just as Eli's sons once abused the priesthood. Israel's leadership is failing again, from within.

So the elders of Israel come to Samuel at Ramah with a request that changes the course of their history. Appoint a king to judge us, they say, like all the other nations. Their reasons are understandable. Samuel is ageing, his sons are corrupt, and as later chapters make clear, enemies are pressing on every side. The law of Moses had even anticipated that one day Israel would ask for a king.

Samuel is deeply displeased, and he takes the matter to the Lord. God's answer is that the people have not rejected Samuel but God himself, as their king. Yet he tells Samuel to give them what they ask, and to warn them first. What follows is Samuel's description of the ways of a king, built around a single, repeated verb: he will take. The people listen, and they insist.

=== 9
The people have asked for a king, and the Lord has told Samuel to give them one. Now the story moves, without warning, to a different family and a very ordinary errand. Kish, a man of standing in the tribe of Benjamin, has lost some donkeys, and he sends his son Saul, with a servant, to find them.

Saul is introduced as choice and handsome, a head taller than anyone else in Israel. He looks every inch a king. The search takes them through the hill country of Ephraim and the land of Benjamin for three days, until they reach the district of Zuph and Saul is ready to give up. It is the servant who suggests asking the man of God in the town, and who has the silver to pay him.

The listener knows more than Saul does. The day before, the Lord told Samuel that a man from Benjamin would come to him, and that he should anoint him ruler over Israel to save them from the Philistines. When Saul and Samuel meet at the town gate, Saul has no idea who he is talking to. By evening he has been given the place of honour at a sacrificial meal and spoken with privately on Samuel's roof. At dawn, on the edge of town, Samuel asks him to wait while his servant goes on ahead.

=== 10
At the edge of the town, with the servant out of earshot, Samuel takes a flask of oil and pours it on Saul's head. Anointing with oil set a person apart for a sacred task, as Israel's priests had been set apart in the wilderness. Now it marks Israel's first king.

To confirm that this is truly from God, Samuel gives Saul three signs that will happen on his way home: news of the donkeys at Rachel's tomb, a meeting with three men going up to worship at Bethel, and an encounter with a band of prophets near Gibeah, where the Spirit of the Lord will come upon him. Samuel also gives him an instruction that will matter later: go down to Gilgal and wait seven days for me there.

The second half of the chapter makes the private anointing public. Samuel gathers the tribes at Mizpah and, after rebuking them once more for rejecting God as their king, has lots cast before the Lord, tribe by tribe and clan by clan, to reveal the man God has chosen. But when Saul's name comes up, he cannot be found. He has hidden among the baggage. Not everyone is impressed by the new king, and the chapter ends with Saul keeping silent in the face of contempt.

=== 11
Saul has been proclaimed king at Mizpah, but he has returned home to Gibeah and gone back to farming. Israel has a king in name, but he has not yet done anything. Then a crisis comes from across the Jordan.

Nahash, king of the Ammonites, lays siege to Jabesh-gilead, an Israelite town in the hills of Gilead east of the river. The listener may remember Jabesh-gilead from the end of Judges, when its people were punished for failing to join the war against Benjamin, and four hundred of its young women were given as wives to the surviving Benjamites. There are old ties between this town and Saul's tribe.

Nahash's terms of surrender are brutal: he will gouge out the right eye of every man, as a humiliation to all Israel. The elders of Jabesh ask for seven days to seek help. When the messengers reach Gibeah, Saul is coming in from the field behind his oxen. What happens next is the moment Saul becomes a king in fact as well as name. His response echoes, in a striking way, the summons the Levite sent to the tribes in the days of the judges, and it rouses the whole nation.

=== 12
Saul has rescued Jabesh-gilead, and at Gilgal the people have confirmed him as king amid great rejoicing. Now Samuel addresses the assembly. It is a kind of farewell, not to life, for Samuel will live on for some years yet, but to the role he has held as Israel's judge. The leadership now passes to the king.

Samuel begins by asking the people to testify to his own integrity. Has he taken anyone's ox or donkey, cheated anyone, or accepted a bribe? It is a pointed question after his warning that a king would take. Then he reviews Israel's history since Egypt, the pattern familiar from Judges, of forgetting the Lord, oppression, crying out and deliverance. He reminds them that when Nahash threatened them, they demanded a king, even though the Lord was their king.

To underline his words, Samuel calls on the Lord to send thunder and rain. It is the wheat harvest, early in the summer, a season when rain in that land is rare. The people are terrified, and they ask Samuel to pray for them. His answer combines warning with reassurance, and with a promise that he will not stop praying for them.

=== 13
Saul is king, and the war with the Philistines that has shadowed the whole story now begins in earnest. He sends most of the people home and keeps three thousand men under arms, two thousand with himself and a thousand with his son.

That son is a new character, introduced without explanation: Jonathan, already old enough to command a thousand men. The setting is the hill country of Benjamin a few miles north of Jerusalem, where Geba and Michmash face each other across a deep ravine. Jonathan attacks a Philistine outpost at Geba, and the Philistines respond in overwhelming force, with chariots, horsemen and troops like the sand on the seashore. Many Israelites hide in caves and cisterns, and some flee across the Jordan.

Saul waits at Gilgal, as Samuel told him, for seven days. His army is melting away, and Samuel has not come. What Saul does next seems understandable, even prudent, and it costs him dearly. Samuel's verdict introduces, for the first time, the idea of another man, a man after God's own heart. A striking detail closes the chapter: the Philistines control the work of the blacksmith, so that only Saul and Jonathan have swords or spears.

=== 14
The Philistines are camped at Michmash, and Saul sits under a pomegranate tree on the edge of Gibeah with about six hundred men. With him is a priest named Ahijah, a great-grandson of Eli, wearing the ephod used to seek God's guidance.

Jonathan, Saul's son, does not wait. Without telling his father, he and his armour-bearer set out to cross the ravine between Geba and Michmash, a steep gorge with rocky cliffs on either side, to the Philistine outpost on the far side. His words to the young man reveal his faith: nothing can stop the Lord from saving, whether by many or by few. The two of them agree on a sign that will tell them whether to climb.

The daring attack that follows sets off panic throughout the Philistine camp, and the earth itself shakes. Saul's watchmen see the enemy scattering, and Israel joins the pursuit. But the victory is clouded by Saul's own command. He has bound his army with a rash oath, forbidding anyone to eat until evening, and Jonathan, who did not hear it, tastes some wild honey. The rest of the day turns on that oath and its consequences. The chapter closes with a summary of Saul's wars and family, including his daughters Merab and Michal and his cousin Abner, commander of his army.

=== 15
Saul has fought on every side, but this chapter brings a single mission that will decide his future. Samuel comes to him with a command from the Lord. The Amalekites, a nomadic people of the southern deserts, were the first to attack Israel after the exodus, falling on the weak and weary at the rear of the column. God had promised then that he would one day blot out their memory. Now Saul is to carry out that judgement, and to devote everything to destruction, keeping nothing.

Saul gathers a large army and wins the battle, after warning the Kenites, who showed kindness to Israel in the wilderness, to move away first. But he spares Agag, the Amalekite king, and the army keeps the best of the livestock.

Then the word of the Lord comes to Samuel, with a sentence that grieves him so much that he cries out to the Lord all night: I regret that I have made Saul king. The confrontation that follows at Gilgal is a turning point in the book. Saul's explanations, Samuel's famous reply about obedience and sacrifice, and a torn robe all point to one conclusion. The chapter ends with the two men parting, Samuel to Ramah and Saul to Gibeah. Samuel never again goes to see Saul, but he mourns for him.

=== 16
Samuel is grieving for Saul, but the Lord tells him it is time to move on. He is to fill his horn with oil and go to Bethlehem, to a man named Jesse. Listeners who remember the book of Ruth will know the name. Jesse is the grandson of Boaz and Ruth. One of his sons is to be king.

The mission is dangerous. Saul is still on the throne, and anointing a rival is treason. So Samuel goes under cover of a sacrifice, and the elders of Bethlehem tremble when the great prophet arrives unannounced.

Jesse's sons pass before Samuel one by one, beginning with the eldest, Eliab, who looks the part, as Saul once did. Each is passed over. The Lord's words to Samuel here, that man looks at the outward appearance but the Lord looks at the heart, explain the choice. The youngest, out with the sheep, has not even been invited.

The second half of the chapter moves to Saul's court. The Spirit of the Lord has left Saul, and a tormenting spirit troubles him. His servants suggest music, and one of them knows of a son of Jesse who plays the harp. So, without knowing it, Saul brings into his own household the man who has been anointed to replace him.

=== 17
David has been anointed at Bethlehem and taken into Saul's service as a musician. Now the scene shifts to the battlefield. The Philistines have advanced into the territory of Judah, to the Valley of Elah, a broad valley in the western foothills that leads from the coastal plain up toward Bethlehem and the hill country. The two armies face each other from opposite slopes, with the valley between them.

From the Philistine ranks comes a champion named Goliath, from Gath, enormously tall and armed in bronze, with a spear whose shaft is like a weaver's beam. He proposes that the war be settled by single combat, and for forty days, morning and evening, he repeats his challenge. No one in Israel will face him, not even Saul, who stands a head taller than any man in Israel.

The story then reintroduces David as if for the first time, the youngest of Jesse's eight sons. His three eldest brothers are with the army, and Jesse sends him with food for them. He arrives just as Goliath steps out again, and what David says, to the soldiers, to his brother Eliab and to King Saul, shows what the Lord saw in his heart. At the end of the chapter Saul asks whose son this young man is, though David has already served at his court.

=== 18
Goliath is dead, and David, still holding the giant's head, has been brought before Saul. From this moment the young shepherd from Bethlehem moves into the centre of Israel's life, and this chapter traces how different people respond to him.

Jonathan, Saul's son and heir, loves David as his own soul. He makes a covenant with him and gives him his robe, his tunic, his sword, his bow and his belt, as if handing over what would have been his own. The people love David. The army's officers approve of him. Saul's daughter Michal loves him.

But Saul does not. When the women come out from the towns of Israel to celebrate the victory, dancing and singing, their song gives David ten times the credit given to the king. Saul hears it and draws his own conclusion. From that day on he watches David with suspicion, and fear begins to turn into hatred. The rest of the chapter shows Saul trying to get rid of David, first openly with a spear, and then by sending him into danger with the promise of a royal marriage. Each attempt only makes David more successful.

=== 19
Saul has given his daughter Michal to David in marriage, after setting a bride price he hoped would get David killed by the Philistines. They have not, and Saul no longer hides his intentions. He tells Jonathan and all his servants that David must be killed.

The chapter shows two of Saul's own children taking David's side. First Jonathan speaks up for his friend, reminding his father of everything David has done for Israel, and for a time Saul relents. But when war breaks out again and David wins another victory, the old torment returns, and once more a spear flies across the room as David plays the harp.

That night David's wife Michal helps him escape through a window, and she uses a household idol and some goat's hair to buy him time. David flees north to Ramah, to Samuel, the old prophet who anointed him, and the two go to a place called Naioth. Saul sends men to seize him, and then goes himself, but something unexpected happens to each of them on the way. The chapter ends by explaining, a second time, the old saying, is Saul also among the prophets?

=== 20
David has escaped from Naioth, and he goes in search of Jonathan. His question is raw: what have I done, that your father wants to kill me? Jonathan cannot believe it. His father, he says, tells him everything.

The two young men devise a test. The next day is the New Moon, the start of a new month, marked with a religious feast at which David, as a member of the king's household, would be expected at Saul's table. David will stay away and hide in the fields, and Jonathan will give an excuse for his absence and watch how his father reacts. If the news is bad, Jonathan will send a signal using arrows shot near a stone where David is hiding.

In the middle of these plans, Jonathan asks David for something. He prays that the Lord will be with David as he has been with Saul, and he asks David to swear that he will show kindness to Jonathan's family forever, even when the Lord has cut off all David's enemies. The promise will matter long after this chapter, in Second Samuel.

At the feast David's seat stays empty, one day and then a second, and on the second day the king's patience runs out. By the next morning Jonathan is walking out to the field with a small boy and his bow.

=== 21
David is now a fugitive, alone and without food or weapons. His first stop is Nob, a town just north of Jerusalem where the priests of the Lord now serve. The chief priest is Ahimelech, another descendant of Eli. He is alarmed to see David arrive without an escort, and David answers with a story about a secret mission for the king.

Ahimelech has no ordinary bread to give him, only the consecrated bread, the Bread of the Presence, which had been set out before the Lord and was meant for the priests. Centuries later Jesus would refer to this very episode when he was criticised for allowing his disciples to pick grain on the Sabbath. The priest also has a sword to offer: the sword of Goliath, kept wrapped in a cloth behind the ephod.

One detail should not be missed. Among those present that day is Doeg, an Edomite, Saul's chief shepherd. Then David does something surprising. He flees to Gath, Goliath's home city, and to its Philistine king Achish. He is recognised at once. A psalm, Psalm thirty-four, is linked by its heading to this moment.

=== 22
David has escaped from Gath by pretending to be mad, and now he takes refuge in the cave of Adullam, in the foothills of Judah, south-west of Bethlehem. His family come to join him, and so do others: the distressed, those in debt, the discontented. Soon there are about four hundred men, the beginnings of David's own fighting force.

For safety David takes his parents to the king of Moab, in the land his great-grandmother Ruth came from. A prophet named Gad, who will remain at David's side for many years, tells him to return to the land of Judah.

Meanwhile the story moves to Gibeah, where Saul sits under a tamarisk tree with his spear in his hand, surrounded by his officials from Benjamin. He accuses them all of conspiring against him, and his self-pity is painful to hear. Then Doeg the Edomite, who was at Nob in the previous chapter, steps forward with what he saw. What follows is one of the darkest acts of Saul's reign. When Saul's own servants refuse to obey his order, a foreigner carries it out. Only one survivor escapes to David, a priest named Abiathar, son of Ahimelech.

=== 23
David now has a priest with him, Abiathar, who has brought the ephod, the sacred garment through which God's guidance could be sought. From now on David asks the Lord before he acts, and again and again the answers come.

The first news is of a Philistine raid on Keilah, a town of Judah in the foothills, where the threshing floors, piled with the harvest, are being looted. David's men are reluctant, but he inquires of the Lord twice, and goes. He saves the town. But a walled town is also a trap, and Saul hears where David is. Once again David asks the Lord, and the answer reveals that the people he has just rescued would hand him over.

From there David moves into the Wilderness of Ziph and then of Maon, the barren country of hills, ravines and caves that falls away eastward from the hill country of Judah toward the Dead Sea. Saul hunts him there day after day. In the middle of the pursuit, Jonathan comes to find David, and their meeting at Horesh is the last time we will see them together. At the last, Saul is closing in on one side of a mountain while David's men flee along the other.

=== 24
Saul broke off his pursuit of David to deal with a Philistine raid, but now he is back. David and his men have moved to the strongholds of En-gedi, an oasis on the western shore of the Dead Sea, where a spring flows down through steep cliffs and caves, in country where wild goats still climb the rocks. Saul comes after him with three thousand chosen men.

What happens next turns on a cave. Saul goes into one alone, to relieve himself, not knowing that David and his men are hiding in its depths. David's men see the hand of God in it: here is your enemy, delivered into your hand. David creeps forward in the darkness. What he does, and what he refuses to do, show the kind of man he is.

The second half of the chapter is a conversation across a distance, once Saul has left the cave. David calls after the king, bows to the ground, and speaks to him with great respect, calling him my father, and holding up the evidence of what could have happened. Saul's response is startling. He weeps, and for a moment the old relationship between the two men seems restored. Saul even admits openly what he has long feared, and asks David for a promise in return.

=== 25
The chapter opens with a death, recorded in a single verse. Samuel, the prophet who anointed both Saul and David, has died, and all Israel gathers to mourn him at his home in Ramah. The last great judge is gone.

Then the story returns to David in the wilderness of southern Judah. His band of six hundred men have been living alongside the shepherds of a wealthy landowner named Nabal, from Maon, whose flocks graze near the town of Carmel, a village in the hill country of Judah, not the famous mountain in the north. David's men have protected those flocks from raiders. Now it is sheep-shearing time, a season of feasting and generosity, and David sends messengers to ask for a share of the provisions.

Nabal, whose name means fool, refuses with contempt, and David straps on his sword. The listener is told at the outset that Nabal has a wife named Abigail, who is intelligent and beautiful, and it is she who steps into the gap. Her speech to David in the mountain ravine is one of the longest by a woman in the Old Testament, full of wisdom and insight into David's future. A closing note records David's wives, and the news that Saul has given David's first wife, Michal, to another man.

=== 26
Once again the men of Ziph come to Saul at Gibeah to tell him where David is hiding, on the hill of Hachilah, and once again Saul sets out with three thousand chosen men. The scene closely resembles the encounter at En-gedi. Yet this is a second occasion, and the differences between them matter.

This time David goes looking for Saul, slipping into the royal camp by night with one companion. He is Abishai, son of David's sister Zeruiah, and brother of Joab, who will later command David's army. Saul lies asleep in the middle of the camp, his spear stuck in the ground beside his head, with Abner, his commander, and the troops around him. A deep sleep from the Lord has fallen on them all.

Abishai offers to finish the king with a single thrust. David's answer is again a refusal, and with it he states his conviction more fully than before, that the Lord himself will deal with Saul in his own time. Then, from a hilltop at a safe distance, he calls across the valley, first mocking Abner for failing to protect the king, and then speaking to Saul. It is the last conversation the two men will ever have.

=== 27
Saul has gone home promising never to harm David again, but David does not believe him. The chapter opens with David talking to himself, and his conclusion is bleak: sooner or later Saul will kill him, and the only safe place is among the Philistines. It is a strange decision for the man who killed Goliath, and the narrative records no word from the Lord guiding it.

So David returns to Achish, king of Gath, the same king before whom he once pretended to be mad. This time he comes not as a lone fugitive but as the leader of six hundred fighting men with their families, and Achish takes them in. David asks for a town of his own, and Achish gives him Ziklag, on the southern edge of the land. David will spend a year and four months in Philistine territory.

What David does from Ziklag is a dangerous double game. He raids the desert peoples to the south, old enemies of Israel such as the Amalekites, and leaves no survivors to tell the tale. Then he reports to Achish that he has been raiding the south of Judah. Achish believes him, and thinks David has made himself so hated by his own people that he will serve Gath forever.

=== 28
The Philistines are gathering for a major war against Israel, and Achish tells David that he and his men will march with him. David's answer is carefully ambiguous.

The narrative then turns to Saul, and the listener is reminded of two things: Samuel is dead, and Saul has expelled the mediums and spiritists from the land, as the law of Moses required. The Philistine army is camped at Shunem, on the north side of the Valley of Jezreel, and Saul's forces are on the slopes of Mount Gilboa to the south. The sight of the enemy fills Saul with terror. He seeks the Lord, but there is no answer, not by dreams, nor by the sacred lots, nor by prophets.

In his desperation Saul does what he himself had forbidden. He asks his servants to find a woman who can call up the dead, and they tell him of one at Endor, a village on the far side of the valley, beyond the Philistine camp. Saul disguises himself and goes by night. The scene that follows is one of the strangest in the Old Testament, and the text does not explain how it happened. What matters is the message Saul receives, and the meal the woman sets before him in the night before the battle.

=== 29
The story steps back a little in time, to the days before the battle, when the Philistine forces are still mustering at Aphek, far to the south-west on the edge of the coastal plain, before marching north to the Valley of Jezreel. As the Philistine lords parade their troops by hundreds and thousands, David and his six hundred men march at the rear with Achish.

The other Philistine commanders are not as trusting as the king of Gath. They see a company of Hebrews in their ranks and demand to know what they are doing there. Achish vouches for David, but the commanders remember the song the Israelite women sang, about Saul and his thousands and David and his tens of thousands. What better way, they ask, for David to win back his master's favour than by turning on them in the middle of the battle?

So David is sent back. He protests, though the text leaves his true intentions unspoken. But the outcome spares him from an impossible choice: fighting against his own people, or betraying the king who sheltered him. At dawn David and his men set off south, and the Philistines go north to Jezreel.

=== 30
Dismissed by the Philistine commanders, David and his men march for three days back to Ziklag. They arrive to find the town burned and empty. While they were away, the Amalekites have raided the south and carried off everyone in Ziklag, including all the women and children, among them David's two wives, Ahinoam and Abigail.

This is the lowest moment of David's years as a fugitive. His men weep until they have no strength left, and in their bitterness they talk of stoning him. The narrator then gives one short sentence that marks a turning point: David strengthened himself in the Lord his God. Then he calls for Abiathar and the ephod, and for the first time since he went over to the Philistines, David asks the Lord what to do.

The pursuit that follows takes them south across the Brook of Besor into the desert, guided by a sick Egyptian slave the Amalekites had left behind. The chapter ends with two decisions that show David's character as a leader. One concerns how the spoil is shared with the men who were too exhausted to fight. The other concerns gifts sent to the elders of the towns of Judah, the people who will soon be asked to make him their king.

=== 31
While David has been rescuing the captives of Ziklag, far to the north the great battle has begun. The narrative moves straight to the slopes of Mount Gilboa, the long ridge on the south-eastern side of the Valley of Jezreel. The battle Samuel foretold at Endor is under way, and Israel is losing.

The chapter is short and stark. Three of Saul's sons fall, among them Jonathan, David's friend, who had once climbed a cliff at Michmash in the conviction that the Lord could save by many or by few. Saul himself is badly wounded by archers, and he asks his armour-bearer to finish him, so that the Philistines will not torture him.

The defeat is total. Israelites living in the valley and beyond the Jordan abandon their towns. The Philistines fasten the king's body to the wall of Beth-shan, a fortress town at the eastern end of the valley, and send the news around their temples.

But the book does not end there. Across the Jordan lies Jabesh-gilead, the town Saul rescued from the Ammonites at the very beginning of his reign. Its people remember. The last scene of First Samuel belongs to them, and it is an act of loyalty and courage. The book closes without David, who will learn the news only at the start of Second Samuel.
