=== intro
Philippians is a letter between old friends. Paul writes it in chains, not knowing whether he will be released or executed, and yet it is full of joy. He thanks God for the Philippians every time he remembers them, he calls them his joy and crown, and he writes to them more warmly and with less correction than to almost any other church.

The friendship went back many years, and Acts tells how it began. On his second missionary journey, Paul and his companions, Silas, the young Timothy and Luke, the writer of Acts, reached Troas on the Aegean coast of Asia Minor. There Paul saw a vision in the night of a man of Macedonia pleading with him: come over and help us. They sailed at once, landed at the port of Neapolis, and walked inland to Philippi. It was the first time Paul preached in what we now call Europe.

Philippi was a Roman colony, settled by veterans of Rome's civil wars after a famous battle fought on the plain outside the city about ninety years earlier. Its people were proud of their Roman citizenship and Roman law, and the main road across northern Greece, the Via Egnatia, ran through its forum. On the Sabbath Paul went out beyond the city gate to a place of prayer by the river, where a group of women had gathered. One of them, Lydia, a dealer in purple cloth from Thyatira, believed, and her household was baptised, and her home became the first meeting place of the church. Then Paul cast a spirit of divination out of a slave girl whose owners made money from her fortune-telling. They had Paul and Silas dragged before the magistrates, beaten with rods and thrown into prison. That night an earthquake shook the prison open, and the jailer and his household believed. The next day the magistrates, alarmed to learn they had beaten Roman citizens, asked the missionaries to leave the city.

So the church at Philippi was born in suffering, and from the start it was generous. When Paul moved on to Thessalonica, the Philippians sent him help more than once, and again when he was in Corinth. No other church, he says, shared with him in this way. Paul visited them again on his third journey.

Now Paul is a prisoner once more, and the Philippians have sent one of their members, Epaphroditus, with a gift of money and to help him. He is in Rome, under the house arrest that Acts describes at its close, and he mentions the palace guard, and believers from the household of Caesar. He is there because he had been arrested in Jerusalem a few years before, after a riot in the temple, held for two years in Caesarea, and sent to Rome after appealing to the emperor. The letter dates from the early sixties of the first century.

Paul's case was approaching a decision, and that he faced the real possibility of death. Epaphroditus had fallen seriously ill while with him, and nearly died. Word of his illness had reached Philippi, and Epaphroditus was distressed that they were worried. Now he had recovered, and Paul was sending him home, carrying this letter.

So Paul writes to thank them for their gift, to tell them how things stand with him, and to explain why Epaphroditus is returning. He also writes with concerns. The Philippians were facing opposition of their own. There was some disunity among them, including a quarrel between two women who had worked alongside Paul. And Paul warns them against teachers who insisted on circumcision for Gentile believers, the same issue that had troubled the churches of Galatia.

The letter moves easily between these things. Paul begins with news of his imprisonment and his hope. He urges the Philippians to be of one mind, and points them to the example of Christ, who humbled himself even to death on a cross. He speaks of his plans to send Timothy, his closest co-worker. Then, with a sudden warning, he tells his own story of the privileges he gave up for Christ. He closes with appeals for peace and joy, and with a gracious thank-you for their gift.

=== 1
Paul writes with Timothy, who had been with him when the church in Philippi was founded. He addresses all the believers there, and he mentions the overseers and deacons, the leaders of the congregation. Then he thanks God for them, warmly and at length. They have been partners with him in the gospel from the first day until now.

Then Paul turns to his own circumstances, which the Philippians are anxious about. He is in chains, but he insists that his imprisonment has helped to spread the gospel. The whole palace guard has come to know why he is held, and many believers have grown bolder. Some, he admits, are preaching out of rivalry with him, hoping to make his imprisonment harder, but Paul rejoices that Christ is preached either way.

He also speaks openly about the possibility of his death. He is torn between departing to be with Christ, which he calls far better, and remaining alive for their sake. He expects to remain. The chapter ends with an appeal for the Philippians to stand firm together, without fear, in the face of opposition of their own.

=== 2
Paul has urged the Philippians to stand firm together in the face of opposition. Now he presses the appeal for unity further. If they have known any encouragement in Christ, he says, they will make his joy complete by being of one mind, with humility, each looking to the interests of others.

To show what that looks like, Paul points to Christ. The passage that follows is poetic in form, almost a hymn. It traces the path of Christ from the form of God, down through his becoming human and taking the form of a servant, all the way to death on a cross, the death Rome reserved for slaves, rebels and the lowest criminals. Then it rises to his exaltation, when every knee will bow and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord.

The second half of the chapter turns to practical plans. Paul hopes to send Timothy soon, and he describes Timothy's faithful service with real warmth. He is also sending back Epaphroditus, the messenger the Philippians had sent to him, who had been seriously ill and nearly died. Paul asks them to welcome him home with honour.

=== 3
With his travel plans for Timothy and Epaphroditus explained, Paul begins what sounds like a conclusion: finally, rejoice in the Lord. Then, with startling abruptness, he issues a warning. Watch out for those dogs, he writes, those evildoers, those mutilators of the flesh.

The people he means are those who taught that Gentile believers must be circumcised, the same issue that had troubled the churches of Galatia. His language is fierce, turning their own claims back on them.

To answer them, Paul tells his own story. If anyone could boast of a pedigree under the law, he could. He lists his credentials: circumcised on the eighth day, of the tribe of Benjamin, a Pharisee, zealous enough to persecute the church, faultless in keeping the law. But all of it, he says, he now counts as loss for the sake of knowing Christ. He pictures himself as a runner, straining toward the finish. Then he warns against those who live as enemies of the cross, and he reminds the Philippians, citizens of a proud Roman colony, that their citizenship is in heaven.

=== 4
The last chapter ended with the reminder that the Philippians' citizenship is in heaven. Now that hope gives way to a series of personal appeals. Paul begins with two women, Euodia and Syntyche, who had worked alongside him in spreading the gospel and who are now in disagreement. He urges them to agree in the Lord, and he asks a trusted member of the church, whom he calls his true yokefellow, to help them. It is a rare glimpse of the women who led and laboured in Paul's churches.

Then come words that have become some of the most familiar in the New Testament: rejoice in the Lord always; do not be anxious about anything; the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding. Paul writes them from prison.

The last part of the letter is Paul's thank-you for the gift the Philippians sent with Epaphroditus. He recalls how they supported him from the very beginning, when he left Macedonia and while he was in Thessalonica. He explains that he has learned to be content whether he has little or much, and he speaks of their gift as a fragrant offering to God. The letter ends with greetings, including from those in the household of Caesar.
