=== intro
Hebrews is one of the great puzzles of the New Testament. It is a substantial, carefully argued work, written in some of the finest Greek in the New Testament, and yet we do not know who wrote it, exactly who first received it, or where they lived. It begins without a greeting, like a sermon rather than a letter, and only at the very end does it turn into a letter, with personal news and greetings. The author calls it a word of exhortation, and it may well have begun life as a sermon, written down and sent to a congregation the author knew and hoped to visit again.

For centuries Hebrews was often attributed to Paul, and in many old Bibles it carries his name. But the book itself never names its author, and its style and way of arguing are quite unlike Paul's letters. The writer also says that the message of salvation was confirmed to him and his readers by those who heard the Lord, which places him in the generation after the first apostles. Early Christians were already unsure. Some suggested Barnabas, and later readers proposed Apollos, the learned teacher from Alexandria whom Acts describes as powerful in the Scriptures. One early scholar, Origen, concluded that only God knows who wrote it, and that remains the honest answer. What the book does reveal is a writer steeped in the Greek translation of the Old Testament, a teacher and pastor who knew Timothy, and who cared deeply for his readers.

The title, to the Hebrews, was added early in the book's history, and it reflects what the contents suggest. The readers seem to have been believers, most likely Jewish believers, who knew the Scriptures of Israel well, along with the priesthood, the sacrifices and the tabernacle. The whole argument assumes that these things matter to them. A closing greeting from those from Italy suggests a link with Italy, perhaps with Rome itself, though it could mean either that the writer was in Italy or that the readers were. The date is uncertain too, but because the book speaks of the old sacrifices as though they were still being offered and never mentions the destruction of the temple in Jerusalem in the year seventy, many place it in the years before that.

What was happening to these believers? In their early days they had suffered for their faith: public insults, prison for some, and the seizure of their property, all of which they had borne with joy. That had not yet cost any of them their lives. But now, some time later, their enthusiasm was fading. Some had stopped meeting together. They were in danger of drifting, and perhaps of turning back from Christ to the familiar forms of worship they had known before, in which so much of their heritage was bound up. The writer's whole purpose is to keep them from falling away.

His method is to show, from their own Scriptures, that everything they valued has been surpassed and fulfilled in Jesus. He is greater than the angels through whom the law was given, greater than Moses, and greater than Joshua, who led Israel into the land but did not give them lasting rest. Above all, he is the great high priest, appointed not in the line of Aaron but in the order of Melchizedek, the mysterious priest king who blessed Abraham in Genesis. He serves in a better sanctuary, under a better covenant, the new covenant promised through Jeremiah, and he has offered a better sacrifice, once for all, by offering himself.

The book moves back and forth between teaching and warning. Every so often the writer breaks off from his argument to plead with his readers, and some of these warnings are among the most solemn passages in the Bible. Then, after the long central section on priesthood and sacrifice, the tone changes. He calls up a great procession of men and women from Israel's history who lived by faith without seeing the promise fulfilled, from Abel to the prophets, and urges his readers to run their own race with endurance. The book closes with practical instructions about life together, and a final blessing.

For a listener who has come through the Old Testament, Hebrews draws on almost all of it. It quotes the Psalms again and again, returns to the tabernacle of Exodus and the sacrifices of Leviticus, and revisits the wilderness generation and the patriarchs of Genesis. It is, in a sense, a guided reading of the Old Testament from the far side of the cross.

=== 1
Hebrews has no greeting. It opens with a single long sentence, carefully built, about the way God has spoken. In the past he spoke to the fathers through the prophets, at many times and in many ways. Now, in these last days, he has spoken in a Son. The writer then lays out who this Son is: heir of all things, the one through whom the universe was made, the radiance of God's glory, who made purification for sins and then sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high.

That last phrase comes from Psalm one hundred and ten, which will return again and again throughout the book.

The rest of the chapter makes a comparison that may seem surprising at first: the Son is far greater than the angels. Jewish tradition held that angels had a part in delivering the law at Sinai, and the readers seem to have held them in high regard. The writer builds his case from a chain of Old Testament quotations, mostly from the Psalms, which he reads as God himself speaking to and about his Son.

=== 2
The comparison with the angels is not finished, but first the writer pauses for the first of his warnings. If the message delivered through angels, the law, carried real consequences, how much more serious it would be to neglect the salvation announced by the Lord himself. The danger he names is drifting, like a boat slipping away from its moorings. The writer also mentions, in passing, that this message came to him and his readers through those who had heard the Lord.

Then he returns to his argument, and to a puzzle. Psalm eight speaks of human beings crowned with glory and given rule over everything. Yet that is not what we see in the world. What we do see, the writer says, is Jesus, made for a little while lower than the angels, and now crowned because he suffered death.

The rest of the chapter explains why the Son had to share fully in human flesh and blood: to break the power of death, and to become like his brothers in every way. The chapter ends by introducing, for the first time, the title that will dominate the book. Jesus is a merciful and faithful high priest.

=== 3
Having shown that the Son is greater than the angels, the writer turns to Moses, the greatest figure in Israel's history, through whom God gave the law. He does not belittle Moses. Moses was faithful in all God's house, he says, as a servant. But Jesus is faithful over that house as a Son, and as its builder.

From Moses the writer moves naturally to the generation Moses led out of Egypt. He quotes at length from Psalm ninety-five, a psalm still sung in synagogue and church, which recalls the rebellion of the Israelites in the wilderness. That generation saw God's works for forty years, yet hardened their hearts, and God swore that they would not enter his rest. Their bodies fell in the desert, as the book of Numbers records.

The writer presses the point home. The word today in the psalm, he says, still speaks. His readers, like the Israelites, have been brought out and are on their way, and the danger is the same: an unbelieving heart that turns away from the living God. He urges them to encourage one another daily, as long as it is still called today.

=== 4
The writer is still reflecting on Psalm ninety-five and its warning that the wilderness generation would never enter God's rest. Now he asks what that rest really is. It cannot simply be the land of Canaan, he argues, because long after Joshua led the people into the land, God was still speaking through the psalm about entering his rest today. So the promise remains open.

He links this rest with the seventh day of creation, when God rested from all his works, and concludes that a Sabbath rest still remains for the people of God. The readers are urged to make every effort to enter it.

The chapter then turns on two memorable passages. The first describes the word of God as living and active, sharper than any double-edged sword, laying bare the thoughts of the heart. The second returns to the high priest introduced at the end of chapter two. Jesus has passed through the heavens, and he is able to sympathise with weakness, because he was tempted in every way and yet was without sin.

=== 5
The writer now begins the long central theme of the book: Jesus as high priest. He starts with what every Jewish reader would know about the office. A high priest is chosen from among the people, to act for them before God and offer sacrifices for sins, including his own. And no one takes the honour for himself. He must be called by God, as Aaron was.

So it was with Christ, the writer says, and he brings together two psalms. The first, Psalm two, calls him God's Son. The second, Psalm one hundred and ten, calls him a priest forever, in the order of Melchizedek. That name will be the key to the next few chapters. The writer adds a moving glimpse of Jesus's earthly life, praying with loud cries and tears to the one who could save him from death.

Then he stops. He has much more to say about this, he tells his readers, but it is hard to explain, because they have become slow to listen. By now they should be teachers, yet they need milk again, like infants, rather than solid food.

=== 6
The writer has just rebuked his readers for needing milk rather than solid food. He now urges them to leave the elementary teachings behind and move on towards maturity.

What follows is one of the most debated passages in the New Testament. The writer warns that it is impossible to restore to repentance those who have been enlightened, tasted the heavenly gift, shared in the Holy Spirit, and then fallen away. Christians have long differed over exactly whom he has in mind. What is clear is that the writer immediately softens his tone. He is confident of better things in his readers' case. God has not forgotten their love and their service to the saints, and he wants them to show the same diligence to the end.

To steady them, he goes back to Abraham. When God made his promise to Abraham, he confirmed it with an oath, swearing by himself. That hope, the writer says, is an anchor for the soul, reaching behind the curtain into the inner sanctuary, where Jesus has gone ahead as a high priest in the order of Melchizedek. With that phrase he is ready, at last, to explain what he means.

=== 7
The writer now explains the figure he has been pointing to for two chapters. Melchizedek appears only briefly in the Old Testament. In Genesis fourteen, after Abram, as he was still called, rescues his nephew Lot from a coalition of kings, a king of Salem who is also a priest of God Most High comes out to meet him, blesses him, and receives a tenth of everything. Centuries later, Psalm one hundred and ten speaks of a king who is a priest forever in the order of Melchizedek.

The writer draws out the significance of both passages. He notes the meaning of the name, king of righteousness, and of Salem, peace, and observes that Genesis records no parents, genealogy, birth or death for Melchizedek. And he argues that because Abraham gave him a tenth and received his blessing, Melchizedek is greater than Abraham, and greater, therefore, than Levi and the priests descended from him.

This matters because Jesus came from the tribe of Judah, not from the priestly tribe of Levi. His priesthood rests not on descent but on the power of an indestructible life, and on God's oath. Unlike the priests who died and were replaced, he lives forever, and he offered his sacrifice once for all.

=== 8
The writer pauses to sum up. The point of everything he has been saying, he tells his readers, is that we do have such a high priest, seated at the right hand of the throne of the Majesty in heaven.

Now he widens the comparison from the priest to the place where he serves, and the covenant under which he serves. The priests on earth minister in a sanctuary that is only a copy and shadow of the heavenly one. He recalls the instruction given to Moses on Mount Sinai, to make the tabernacle according to the pattern he was shown on the mountain.

The heart of the chapter is the longest Old Testament quotation in the New Testament. It comes from the prophet Jeremiah, who spoke in the last years before Jerusalem fell to Babylon, and who promised that the days were coming when God would make a new covenant with Israel and Judah. Under it God would write his laws on their hearts and remember their sins no more. The writer draws a simple conclusion: by calling this covenant new, God has made the first one old.

=== 9
Having introduced the new covenant, the writer turns to the worship of the old one, and he takes his readers back to the tabernacle described in the book of Exodus. He walks through it briefly: the outer room, the Holy Place, with its lampstand, table and consecrated bread, and then, behind the second curtain, the Most Holy Place, with the ark of the covenant and the cherubim above the mercy seat.

He focuses on one moment in Israel's year, the Day of Atonement described in Leviticus. Only once a year did the high priest go into the inner room, and never without blood. For the writer, that closed curtain says that the way into God's presence had not yet been opened.

Against that background he sets the work of Christ. Christ has entered not a man-made sanctuary but heaven itself, and not with the blood of goats and calves, but with his own blood, once for all. The writer recalls how Moses sealed the first covenant with blood at Sinai, and he looks ahead to the day when Christ will appear a second time, not to deal with sin, but to bring salvation to those who wait for him.

=== 10
The writer brings his long argument about sacrifice to its conclusion. The law, he says, is only a shadow of the good things to come. Sacrifices offered year after year could never take away sins, or they would have stopped. He quotes Psalm forty, in which the speaker tells God that sacrifices are not what he desired, and says, here I am, I have come to do your will. The writer hears these words on the lips of Christ, whose one offering has made perfect for all time those being sanctified.

With that, the argument turns to appeal. Since the way into the Most Holy Place is now open, the readers are to draw near to God, hold fast their hope, and not give up meeting together, as some have started to do.

Then comes another sombre warning about those who deliberately keep on sinning after receiving the truth. But the writer follows it, as before, with encouragement. He reminds his readers of their early days, when they endured public insult, cared for prisoners, and joyfully accepted the seizure of their property. He urges them not to throw away their confidence now, and ends with words from the prophet Habakkuk: my righteous one will live by faith.

=== 11
The last chapter ended with the word faith, and this chapter takes it up. It opens with a short description of what faith is, the assurance of things hoped for and the conviction of things not seen. Then it unrolls one of the best known passages in the Bible, a procession of men and women from Israel's history, each introduced with the same words: by faith.

Almost every name will be familiar. The roll begins with Abel, Enoch and Noah from the early chapters of Genesis, and lingers longest over Abraham and Sarah, who lived as strangers in the promised land, looking for a better country. It moves on through Isaac, Jacob and Joseph, then to Moses and the exodus, the fall of Jericho, and Rahab.

Then the pace quickens. The writer says he has no time to tell of Gideon, Barak, Samson, Jephthah, David, Samuel and the prophets, and he gathers the rest into a rush of brief allusions to victories, rescues, sufferings and martyrdoms. The chapter's final note is that none of them received what was promised. God had planned something better, so that they would not be made perfect apart from us.

=== 12
The great procession of chapter eleven now becomes a crowd of witnesses, gathered round as if in an arena, and the readers are runners on the track. The writer urges them to throw off every weight and run with endurance, their eyes fixed on Jesus, who endured the cross and has sat down at the right hand of God.

He speaks frankly about their present hardships. Quoting the book of Proverbs, he tells them to understand their suffering as the discipline a father gives the son he loves. They have not yet resisted to the point of shedding blood, he notes, a hint that none of them has yet died for the faith.

Then comes a warning drawn from Genesis, the story of Esau, who sold his birthright for a single meal and could not afterwards win back the blessing. The chapter ends with two contrasting mountains. Israel came to Mount Sinai, with its fire, darkness and trembling. The readers have come to Mount Zion, the heavenly Jerusalem, with its angels, its assembly and its mediator of a new covenant. The writer closes with a quotation from the prophet Haggai about God shaking heaven and earth once more, and with a kingdom that cannot be shaken.

=== 13
The great argument of Hebrews is complete, and the final chapter turns to the practical life of the community. The instructions come quickly: brotherly love, hospitality to strangers, care for those in prison, faithfulness in marriage, contentment rather than love of money. The writer asks them to remember their former leaders, who first taught them, and to imitate their faith.

One last time he returns to the language of sacrifice and the tabernacle. He warns against strange teachings about foods, and notes that the bodies of the animals sacrificed on the Day of Atonement were burned outside the camp. Jesus too suffered outside the city gate, and the readers are to go out to him and share his disgrace.

The closing lines are those of a letter. The writer asks for prayer and hopes to be restored to his readers soon. He offers a final blessing, calling Jesus the great Shepherd of the sheep. And he adds a few lines of news: Timothy has been released, and the writer hopes to come with him. Those from Italy send their greetings.
