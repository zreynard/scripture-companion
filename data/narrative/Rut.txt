=== intro
The book of Ruth begins with a single phrase that places it in time: in the days when the judges ruled. The listener has just heard what those days were like, the cycles of idolatry and oppression, the violence, and the closing refrain that everyone did what was right in his own eyes. Ruth is set in the same years, but it could hardly feel more different. There are no battles here, no kings or armies, no deliverers. There is a small town, a family, a harvest, and a handful of ordinary people who treat one another with remarkable faithfulness.

The town is Bethlehem in Judah, in the hills a few miles south of Jerusalem. Its name means house of bread, but the story opens with a famine there. A man named Elimelech takes his wife Naomi and their two sons east to Moab, the high plateau beyond the Dead Sea. The move is more significant than it sounds. The Moabites were descended from Lot, Abraham's nephew, and they had a long and bitter history with Israel. It was a king of Moab who hired Balaam to curse Israel in the wilderness, Moabite women who drew Israel into idolatry at Peor, and, in the book of Judges, another king of Moab, Eglon, who ruled over Israel for eighteen years. The law of Moses barred Moabites from entering the assembly of the Lord.

Yet the heroine of this book is a Moabite woman. In Moab Naomi buries her husband, sees her two sons marry Moabite wives, and then buries both sons as well. One of the young widows is Ruth, and she refuses to leave her. She goes with her mother-in-law to Bethlehem, a foreigner and a widow with nothing, and there, gleaning in the fields at harvest time, she meets a landowner named Boaz.

Two customs of Israelite life are important for the story. The first is gleaning. The law commanded farmers not to harvest to the very edges of their fields, and to leave behind whatever fell, so that the poor, the widow and the foreigner could gather it. The second is the role of the kinsman-redeemer, a close relative who had the right and the duty to protect a family member in trouble, by buying back land that had been sold, for example, or by keeping a family's name and inheritance from dying out. These customs give the book its shape, and much of its tension.

The author is not named. Jewish tradition credits Samuel, and the closing genealogy runs down to David. In English Bibles Ruth sits between Judges and First Samuel, where its setting belongs. In the Hebrew Bible it is placed among the Writings, and it came to be read each year at the Feast of Weeks, the festival of the grain harvest.

The story is told in four short chapters, each with its own scene: the road from Moab, the harvest field, the threshing floor by night, and the town gate. God never speaks in the book and works no wonders, and the narrator names his hand only twice, when he gives his people food again and when he gives Ruth a son. Yet he is quietly present throughout, in blessings spoken between people, in a field that Ruth happens to choose, in the turn of events. The book ends with a birth and a family line that leads to King David, and much later the Gospel of Matthew would name Ruth in the ancestry of Jesus.

=== 1
The book wastes no time. In a few short sentences, famine drives Elimelech's family from Bethlehem to Moab, and then death empties the household. Elimelech dies. His two sons, Mahlon and Chilion, marry Moabite women, Orpah and Ruth, and after about ten years both sons die as well. Naomi is left alone in a foreign land, with no husband, no sons, and two widowed daughters-in-law.

A widow without sons had no one to protect or provide for her. When Naomi hears that the Lord has given his people food again, she sets out for home, and the young women go with her. On the road she stops and urges them to go back to their mothers' houses, where they might yet marry again. She has nothing to offer them.

The three women weep together. Orpah kisses her mother-in-law and turns back, a sensible choice that the story does not condemn. Ruth clings to Naomi, and her words of loyalty have been spoken at countless weddings and funerals since.

The chapter ends in Bethlehem, where the whole town is stirred. Naomi's name means pleasant, but she asks to be called Mara, which means bitter. She went away full, she says, and the Lord has brought her back empty. The last line notes, almost in passing, that they arrive at the beginning of the barley harvest.

=== 2
Naomi and Ruth have arrived in Bethlehem with nothing, just as the barley harvest begins in the spring. Before the story continues, the narrator introduces a new character, telling the listener something Ruth does not yet know: Naomi has a relative on her husband's side, a man of standing and noble character, from the clan of Elimelech. His name is Boaz.

Ruth takes the initiative. She asks Naomi's permission to go and glean in the fields, gathering the stalks of grain the harvesters leave behind, as the law of Moses allowed the poor and the foreigner to do. It was humble work, and for a young foreign woman alone among the harvesters it was not without danger.

The narrator says that Ruth happens to come to the part of the field belonging to Boaz, and the phrasing invites the listener to smile. Boaz arrives from town and greets his workers with a blessing, and they answer him with another. His first question is about the young woman he has not seen before. The kindness that follows is generous and practical. By evening Ruth goes home with a remarkable quantity of barley, and when Naomi hears the name of the man whose field it was, her bitterness begins to give way to hope.

=== 3
The harvests of barley and wheat have come and gone, weeks of hard work, and Ruth has gleaned in Boaz's fields throughout. Boaz has been kind, but nothing more has happened. Now Naomi, who once told her daughters-in-law that she could offer them no future, begins to plan one for Ruth.

Her plan is bold and, to modern ears, puzzling. Boaz will be winnowing barley on the threshing floor that night. Winnowing meant tossing the threshed grain into the evening breeze so that the chaff blew away, and at the end of the harvest the owner might sleep beside the heap to guard it. Ruth is to wash, put on perfume and her best clothes, go down to the threshing floor and keep out of sight until he has finished eating and drinking. When he lies down, she is to uncover his feet and lie down there.

The scene is told with great delicacy, and much is left unsaid. What matters is the request Ruth makes in the middle of the night. She asks Boaz to spread the corner of his garment over her, a gesture that meant taking a woman under his protection in marriage, and she reminds him that he is a kinsman-redeemer. Boaz's reply is warm, but it also brings a complication that no one has mentioned until now.

=== 4
Ruth has returned from the threshing floor with six measures of barley and a promise, and Naomi has told her to wait, because Boaz will not rest until the matter is settled. The scene moves to the town gate of Bethlehem, where legal business was done in public, before witnesses.

Boaz sits down at the gate, and before long the closer relative he mentioned walks by. The book never gives this man's name. Boaz gathers ten elders and lays out the case skilfully, beginning with a piece of land that belonged to Elimelech and that Naomi is now selling. Only then does he add what else the redemption will involve. An old legal custom, the handing over of a sandal, seals the outcome.

The rest of the chapter is full of blessing. The elders pray that Ruth will be like Rachel and Leah, the mothers of Israel's tribes, and that his house will be like the house of Perez, whom Tamar bore to Judah, a family line that had once before been saved from dying out in an unexpected way. A son is born, and the women of Bethlehem, who heard Naomi say that she came home empty, now watch her take a child onto her lap.

The book closes with a short genealogy that runs from Perez, son of Judah, through Boaz and the child Obed to Jesse, and then to one last name: David.
