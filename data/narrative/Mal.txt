=== intro
Malachi is the last book of the Old Testament in our Bibles, and the last of the Twelve. Its name means my messenger. It is the prophet's name, and it echoes a famous line in the third chapter, where the Lord says, I will send my messenger. The book tells us nothing more about the man who spoke these words.

The book gives no date, but its setting is clear enough. The temple is standing and sacrifices are being offered, so we are some time after Haggai and Zechariah, when the second temple was completed in five hundred and fifteen before Christ. Judah is ruled by a governor, as it was under the Persians. The problems Malachi addresses are the very ones the listener met in the closing chapters of Ezra and Nehemiah: priests careless in their duties, offerings of blind and lame animals, tithes withheld so that the temple storerooms stood empty, Jewish men marrying women who worshipped other gods, and the breaking of marriages. Malachi belongs to the fifth century before Christ, to the time of Ezra and Nehemiah.

It was a time of disappointment. The exiles had come home, the temple had been rebuilt, and the prophets had spoken of glory, of nations streaming to Zion and a king from David's line. Generations later, Judah was still a small and poor province of a foreign empire. The harvests were uncertain, the promises seemed unfulfilled, and religion had settled into a weary routine. People had begun to wonder aloud whether serving God made any difference at all.

Malachi answers them in a distinctive way. The book is built as a series of disputes. The Lord makes a statement, the people object with a question, often beginning with the word how, and the Lord replies. I have loved you, he says, and they ask, how have you loved us? You have despised my name, he says to the priests, and they ask, how have we despised it? Six times the pattern recurs, and it gives the book the feel of a real argument, with the people's own complaints quoted back to them.

The disputes move from the Lord's love for Israel, through the failings of the priests and the faithlessness of the people in marriage, to their doubts about God's justice and their neglect of tithes. Through them all runs the expectation of a day when the Lord will come to his temple, refine his people, and set the righteous apart from the wicked.

The book's final words look ahead to that day, and to a messenger who will come before it, Elijah the prophet. After Malachi, no further prophetic book was added to the Hebrew Scriptures, and some four hundred years passed before the events of the New Testament. When the story resumes, the Gospels begin with John the Baptist in the wilderness, preparing the way of the Lord, and Jesus would say that John was the Elijah who was to come.

=== 1
The first dispute begins with the Lord's love. How have you loved us, the people reply, and the answer reaches back to the twin brothers of Genesis, Jacob and Esau, and the Lord's choice of Jacob. Now Esau's descendants, the Edomites, have seen their mountain homeland laid waste, and they will not be able to rebuild it. Edom's ruin, close at hand, is offered as evidence that the Lord has not abandoned his own people. Paul would later quote these lines in his letter to the Romans.

Then the Lord turns to the priests. A son honours his father and a servant his master, but the priests despise his name, and when they ask how, the answer is blunt. They are offering blind, lame and sick animals on his altar. Try offering them to your governor, the Lord says, and see whether he is pleased. Better to shut the temple doors altogether. In contrast, among the nations, from the rising of the sun to its setting, the Lord's name will be great.

=== 2
The word to the priests continues, now as a solemn warning. If they will not listen and honour the Lord's name, their blessings will become curses.

Against their failures Malachi sets a portrait of what a priest should be. The Lord's covenant with Levi, ancestor of the priestly tribe, was one of life and peace. True instruction was in his mouth, and he turned many from sin, for a priest is the messenger of the Lord of Hosts. These priests have caused many to stumble.

In the second half of the chapter the dispute widens to the whole people, and to faithfulness in marriage. Men of Judah have married women who worship other gods, the problem Ezra and Nehemiah also confronted, and others have divorced the wife of their youth. They weep at the altar because their offerings are refused, but the Lord was witness to the covenant they betrayed. The chapter ends with a complaint that leads into the next dispute: where is the God of justice?

=== 3
The people have asked where the God of justice is, and the Lord answers. He will send his messenger to prepare the way, and then the Lord they are seeking will suddenly come to his temple. Mark's Gospel opens with these words, joined to a line from Isaiah, as it introduces John the Baptist.

That coming will not be comfortable. He will be like a refiner's fire and a launderer's soap, purifying the sons of Levi, and coming in judgement on sorcerers, adulterers, and those who cheat workers or oppress the widow, the fatherless and the foreigner.

The next dispute follows. Return to me, the Lord says, and they ask, how shall we return? The answer is practical: they are robbing God of tithes and offerings. Nehemiah found the Levites gone back to their fields for the same reason. The Lord invites the people to bring the full tithe and test him.

The final dispute answers those who say it is futile to serve God. Those who fear the Lord speak together, and a scroll of remembrance is written before him.

=== 4
This short chapter closes the book; in the Hebrew text it forms the end of chapter three.

The day that the previous chapter anticipated is described now, burning like a furnace. For the arrogant and evildoers it will be fire in stubble, leaving neither root nor branch. But for those who fear the Lord's name, the sun of righteousness will rise with healing in its wings.

The book ends with two last words. The first looks back. Remember the law of my servant Moses, given at Horeb, the mountain also called Sinai. The second looks forward. Before the great day comes, the Lord will send Elijah the prophet, who will turn the hearts of fathers to their children and the hearts of children to their fathers.

Then the prophetic voice falls silent. Luke's Gospel takes up these very words, when an angel appears in the temple to an elderly priest named Zechariah and tells him that his son will go before the Lord in the spirit and power of Elijah.
