=== intro
Genesis is the book of beginnings. It opens before there is any world at all, with God alone, and it closes in Egypt, with a coffin waiting to be carried home. Between those two moments lies the story of how the world came to be, how it went wrong, and how God set about putting it right by choosing one family and making them a promise.

Jewish and Christian tradition gives it to Moses, together with the four books that follow it, and those five books were always read as a single work, the Torah, the foundation of Israel's life. Its first hearers were the people Moses led out of Egypt, a nation of former slaves camped in the wilderness. For them Genesis answered the most basic questions. Who is the God who has just rescued us? What kind of world has he made? And who are we, that he should care about us at all?

The book falls into two great movements. The first eleven chapters range across the whole of humanity. God creates the heavens and the earth with a word and calls them good. He forms the first man and woman and places them in a garden called Eden. Then comes the turning that shapes everything after it: the couple listen to the serpent, eat from the one tree God had forbidden, and are sent out of the garden. What follows is a widening shadow. A brother murders a brother. Violence fills the earth, until God sends a flood and begins again with Noah and his family. Even after the waters recede, the old pride returns, and at Babel people build a tower to make a name for themselves, until God scatters them across the earth.

Then, at the end of chapter eleven, the camera closes in on a single man. Abram lives in Ur, in the river lands of southern Mesopotamia, in what is now Iraq. His family moves north to Harran, and there God speaks to him. Leave your country and your father's house, God says, and go to a land I will show you. I will make you a great nation, and through you all the families of the earth will be blessed. That promise of land, descendants and blessing drives the rest of the book.

The second movement, from chapter twelve to the end, follows four generations of this family. Abram, later renamed Abraham, travels the hill country of Canaan as a herdsman, living in tents and digging wells, waiting years for the son God has promised. His son Isaac is a quieter figure, but he carries the promise forward. Isaac's son Jacob is a schemer who cheats his brother Esau, flees for his life, and spends twenty years working for his uncle Laban before he returns, changed, with a new name: Israel. His twelve sons become the ancestors of the twelve tribes. The last part of the book belongs to one of those sons, Joseph, sold into slavery by his brothers, who rises to govern Egypt and saves the whole family from famine.

The world of these chapters is the ancient Near East: the great civilisations of Mesopotamia to the east, Egypt to the south, and between them the narrow land of Canaan, a corridor of hills, valleys and desert edges where the patriarchs graze their flocks among Canaanite towns. The stories of Abraham and his family belong to the first half of the second millennium before Christ, some four thousand years ago.

Genesis is honest about its people. The heroes lie, play favourites, and make terrible choices, and the book rarely pauses to scold them. Again and again the promise seems about to fail: wives who cannot conceive, brothers who want to kill one another, famine in the land. And again and again God keeps it alive, often through the least likely person in the room.

Listen for the phrase that marks the book's sections: this is the account of. It returns at each new stage, like a heading in an old family history, and it reminds the listener that this is one story being handed down. By the final page the family that went down to Egypt seventy strong has settled there, safe for now, but far from the land God promised. The book of Exodus will pick up the story there, centuries later.

=== 1
The Bible begins with God, and with nothing else. There is no explanation of where God came from and no rival beside him. The earth is formless and empty, darkness covers the deep waters, and the Spirit of God hovers over them.

What follows has the shape of a poem as much as a story. Over six days God speaks, and the world takes form. The first three days bring order to the chaos: light and darkness, the sky and the seas, dry land and plants. The next three fill what has been formed: sun, moon and stars, then birds and sea creatures, then animals on the land. A refrain marks each step. God said, and it was so, and God saw that it was good.

On the sixth day comes the climax. God makes humanity, male and female, in his own image, and gives them the task of ruling and caring for the earth on his behalf. In a world where other nations told stories of gods fighting monsters to make the world, this account is strikingly calm. Here one God creates everything by his word, and calls all of it very good.

=== 2
The six days are complete, and the account rests with God on the seventh day, which he blesses and sets apart. Then the story changes pace. Chapter one surveyed the whole universe. Chapter two kneels down in the dust.

God is now called by his personal name, the Lord God, and he forms a man from the ground and breathes life into him. The Hebrew word for man, adam, echoes the word for ground, adamah, and it becomes the man's name. God plants a garden in Eden, somewhere in the east, watered by rivers that include the Tigris and the Euphrates, and places the man there to work it and keep it.

Two trees stand in the middle of the garden: the tree of life, and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, which the man is told not to eat from. For the first time something is not good: the man is alone. What happens next answers that need, and it ends with a man and a woman together in the garden, naked and unashamed.

=== 3
The man and the woman are living in the garden, with only one command to keep. Now a new character arrives: the serpent, described as more crafty than any other creature God has made. Later Scripture will reveal who stands behind it. Here it simply speaks, and its first words are a question that twists what God had said.

In a few short scenes, trust gives way to suspicion, and the couple who were naked and unashamed reach for fig leaves and hide among the trees. When they hear the Lord God walking in the garden in the breeze of the day, the questions and answers that follow are painfully human.

The consequences are spoken over the serpent, the woman and the man in turn, and they touch everything: work, childbirth, relationships, and death itself. Yet within the judgement on the serpent is a hint of hope that later readers would treasure, a promise that the woman's offspring will one day crush its head. The chapter ends with the man and the woman driven out, and cherubim and a whirling sword of flame guarding the way to the tree of life.

=== 4
Outside the garden, life goes on, and the first children are born. Eve names her firstborn Cain, and then gives birth to Abel. Cain works the soil and Abel keeps flocks.

When both brothers bring offerings to the Lord, Abel's is accepted and Cain's is not. The text does not dwell on why, but it does record God's warning to Cain, that sin is crouching at his door like an animal, waiting to master him. What follows in the field is the first murder, only a few lines after the first sin.

Cain is driven away to wander, and he settles in the land of Nod, east of Eden. The rest of the chapter traces his descendants, and with them the rise of civilisation: the first city, herds and tents, musical instruments, tools of bronze and iron. Alongside that progress runs a darker line, which reaches its peak in Lamech, who boasts of killing a man for wounding him. Then, at the very end, a new son is born to Adam and Eve. His name is Seth, and in the days of Seth's own son, Enosh, people begin to call on the name of the Lord.

=== 5
After the violence of Cain's line, Genesis turns to a different family tree. This is the line of Seth, traced from Adam to Noah across ten generations.

The chapter has a steady, almost solemn rhythm. Each man is named, he lives a certain number of years, he fathers a son, he has other sons and daughters, and then the refrain falls: and he died. The great ages, many of them stretching past nine hundred years, pass without comment. What the list does make clear is that death, announced in the garden, now reigns over every generation.

There is one striking exception. Enoch, in the seventh generation, walks with God, and then he is no more, because God takes him. The chapter closes with Lamech, a different Lamech from the one in Cain's line, naming his son Noah and hoping that this child will bring relief from the painful toil of the cursed ground. And when Noah is five hundred years old, he becomes the father of three sons: Shem, Ham and Japheth.

=== 6
The genealogy has brought us to Noah, and now the story widens to show what the world has become. The chapter opens with a brief and mysterious episode about the sons of God and the daughters of men, and the Nephilim, the mighty men of old. The text gives few details, but the Lord's verdict is plain: his Spirit will not contend with man forever, and man's days will be a hundred and twenty years.

Then comes one of the saddest verses in the Bible. The Lord sees that every inclination of the human heart is only evil all the time, and he regrets that he has made mankind. He resolves to wipe the earth clean.

But Noah finds favour in the eyes of the Lord. Noah is righteous in his generation and walks with God, and God tells him what is coming and what he must build: a great vessel of gopher wood, sealed with pitch, large enough to carry his family and pairs of every kind of creature through the waters. The chapter ends with a simple sentence that defines Noah: he did everything just as God commanded him.

=== 7
The ark is built. Now the Lord tells Noah to go in, with his wife, his three sons and their wives, and the animals, clean and unclean, gathered by kind. In seven days the rain will begin.

The narrative slows here and repeats itself, almost like a chant: Noah's age, the day, the animals going in two by two, the door shut. The detail that the Lord himself shuts them in is easy to miss, but it matters.

Then the flood comes. The springs of the great deep burst open and the floodgates of the heavens are opened. The language deliberately echoes the creation account, but in reverse: the waters that God separated on the second day now rush back together, and the dry land disappears. The waters rise above the highest mountains and every living thing on the land perishes. Only Noah is left, and those with him in the ark. The chapter closes with the waters still prevailing on the earth for a hundred and fifty days.

=== 8
The earth lies under water, and the ark drifts with its passengers. The turning point comes in a single sentence: God remembered Noah. From that moment the waters begin to fall.

A wind passes over the earth, as the Spirit once moved over the waters at creation, and the world begins to emerge again. The ark comes to rest on the mountains of Ararat, in the highlands to the north of Mesopotamia. Noah waits, and then he sends out birds, a raven first and then a dove, to discover whether the land is dry. The scene with the dove and the olive leaf has become one of the most recognised images in the world.

More than a year after the rain began, God tells Noah to come out. His first act on the new earth is to build an altar and offer sacrifices. The chapter ends with the Lord's promise that, as long as the earth endures, seedtime and harvest, cold and heat, summer and winter, day and night will never cease.

=== 9
Noah and his family stand on a cleansed earth, and God speaks to them in words that echo the blessing given to the first humans: be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth. Some things have changed. Animals may now be eaten, though not with their lifeblood, and human life is given a new protection, because humans are made in the image of God.

Then God establishes the covenant he promised Noah before the flood. It is made with Noah, his descendants and every living creature, and it carries a sign: the rainbow set in the clouds. Never again will a flood destroy all life on the earth.

The chapter ends on a sobering note. Noah plants a vineyard, drinks its wine and lies uncovered in his tent. His son Ham sees him and tells his brothers, while Shem and Japheth cover their father without looking. Noah's words afterwards, a curse on Ham's son Canaan and a blessing on Shem and Japheth, will echo through the history to come, because Canaan's descendants will one day live in the land promised to Abraham.

=== 10
The ark lies behind them, and the three sons of Noah are about to fill the earth. What follows, often called the Table of Nations, is a map of the known world drawn as a family tree.

The descendants of Japheth spread mostly to the north and west, toward Asia Minor and the coastlands of the Mediterranean. The descendants of Ham are linked with Egypt, Cush, Canaan and parts of Mesopotamia, and among them is Nimrod, a mighty hunter whose kingdom begins in Babylon and who goes on to build Nineveh. The line of Shem is given last and with special care, because it will lead to Abraham. Many of these names are peoples and places as well as persons, and some of them will return later as Israel's neighbours and enemies.

Every nation on this map springs from the one family that came out of the ark. The chapter closes with those nations spreading out over the earth, and the next one tells how one family came to be scattered, speaking many tongues.

=== 11
The nations have been named, and now we learn how they came to be divided. The chapter opens at a time when the whole earth has one language. People moving east settle on a plain in the land of Shinar, the region of Babylon, and they decide to build a city with a tower that reaches into the heavens, to make a name for themselves and avoid being scattered.

Their building materials are described with care: baked brick and tar instead of stone and mortar, the methods of Mesopotamia. The great stepped temple towers of that region, the ziggurats, stand in the background of this story. They mean the tower to reach the heavens, yet the Lord comes down to see it. He confuses their language and scatters them across the earth, and the place is called Babel.

Then the scene changes. A final genealogy runs from Shem to a man named Terah, who lives in Ur of the Chaldeans. Terah has three sons, Abram, Nahor and Haran. Abram's wife Sarai is barren. The family sets out for Canaan, but stops in Harran, far to the north, and there Terah dies. It is there, in Harran, that God is about to speak.

=== 12
The story of the whole human race now narrows to one man. Abram is seventy-five years old and living in Harran when the Lord speaks to him. Go from your country, your relatives and your father's house to the land I will show you. I will make you into a great nation and bless you, and all the families of the earth will be blessed through you.

The promise answers the curse and the scattering of the earlier chapters. At Babel people tried to make a name for themselves; here God promises to make Abram's name great. Abram goes, taking Sarai, his nephew Lot and all their possessions, and travels south into Canaan. At the oak of Moreh near Shechem, the Lord appears and promises this land to his offspring, and Abram builds an altar.

But almost immediately the promise is tested. A famine drives Abram down to Egypt, and there, fearing for his life, he asks Sarai to say she is his sister. The man of faith from the opening verses becomes a frightened man telling half-truths, and it takes God's intervention to rescue Sarai from Pharaoh's palace.

=== 13
Abram has returned from Egypt, richer in livestock, silver and gold, and he makes his way back to the altar he built near Bethel. Lot is still with him, and he too has flocks, herds and tents. The land cannot support them both, and their herdsmen begin to quarrel.

What follows is a portrait of two men making a choice. Abram, the elder, gives Lot the first pick of the land. Lot looks out over the well-watered plain of the Jordan, green like the garden of the Lord, and chooses it, moving his tents toward the city of Sodom. Genesis adds a quiet warning: the men of Sodom are wicked, sinning greatly against the Lord.

After Lot leaves, God speaks to Abram again, telling him to look north, south, east and west, because all the land he can see will belong to his offspring, as many as the dust of the earth. Abram moves his tents to the great trees of Mamre, near Hebron, which will become his home base for years to come.

=== 14
Until now, the story of Abram has been the story of a family. Suddenly it is caught up in international politics. Four kings from the east, led by Chedorlaomer, king of Elam, march against five kings of the cities of the plain, including Sodom and Gomorrah, who have rebelled after years of paying tribute.

The battle goes badly for the cities of the plain, and among the captives carried off is Lot. When the news reaches Abram, the herdsman of Mamre becomes a military leader. He gathers three hundred and eighteen trained men from his own household, pursues the invaders far to the north, and defeats them in a night attack.

On his return, Abram meets two kings. One is the king of Sodom. The other is a mysterious figure who appears and disappears within a few verses: Melchizedek, king of Salem and priest of God Most High, who brings out bread and wine and blesses Abram. The brief encounter left a deep mark on later Scripture, appearing again in Psalm one hundred and ten and in the letter to the Hebrews. The chapter ends with Abram refusing to take anything from the king of Sodom, unwilling to let anyone but God make him rich.

=== 15
After the battle, the word of the Lord comes to Abram in a vision: do not be afraid, I am your shield and your very great reward. But Abram's reply goes straight to what weighs on him. He has no son. As things stand, his heir will be a servant, Eliezer of Damascus.

God takes him outside and tells him to look at the stars and count them if he can. So shall your offspring be. Then comes one of the most important sentences in the Bible: Abram believed the Lord, and he credited it to him as righteousness. The apostle Paul would return to this verse in his letters to the Romans and the Galatians.

The rest of the chapter describes a strange and solemn ceremony. At God's command Abram cuts animals in half and arranges the pieces in two rows. In the ancient world, parties to a covenant might walk between such pieces, as if to say, may this happen to me if I break my word. As darkness falls, Abram sleeps, and God reveals that his descendants will be strangers and slaves in a foreign land for four hundred years before returning. Then a smoking firepot and a flaming torch pass between the pieces. God alone walks the path, binding himself to his promise.

=== 16
Ten years have passed since Abram arrived in Canaan, and Sarai still has no child. The promise of offspring is beginning to seem impossible, and Sarai proposes a solution that was common in their world: she gives Abram her Egyptian servant Hagar, so that she might build a family through her.

The plan works, and it goes badly wrong. Hagar conceives and begins to look on Sarai with contempt. Sarai treats her harshly, and Hagar runs away into the wilderness, on the road toward Egypt.

There, beside a spring of water, the angel of the Lord finds her. It is the first appearance of this figure in the Bible, and he speaks to a runaway slave woman with great tenderness. He tells her to return, promises that her descendants will be too many to count, and names her son: Ishmael, meaning God hears. Hagar responds by giving the Lord a name of her own, the God who sees me. The chapter ends with Ishmael's birth, when Abram is eighty-six years old.

=== 17
Thirteen years have passed since the birth of Ishmael. Abram is now ninety-nine, and the Lord appears to him and introduces himself as God Almighty. What follows is the most detailed statement of the covenant yet.

The promise grows in scope: Abram will be the father of many nations, and kings will come from him. To mark this, God changes his name from Abram, exalted father, to Abraham, father of a multitude. The land of Canaan will be an everlasting possession for his descendants, and God will be their God.

The covenant also receives a sign, to be carried in the body of every male in Abraham's household and among his descendants: circumcision. Then God turns to Sarai. Her name will now be Sarah, and she, not Hagar, will be the mother of the promised son, within a year. Abraham falls on his face and laughs at the thought of a child born to a man of a hundred and a woman of ninety. God answers that the son will be named Isaac, which means he laughs. That very day Abraham obeys, and every male in his household is circumcised, including Ishmael.

=== 18
Not long after the covenant of circumcision, Abraham is sitting at the entrance of his tent by the trees of Mamre in the heat of the day, when three men appear. The reader is told at once that this is the Lord appearing to him, though the narrative only gradually makes clear who the visitors are.

Abraham's response is a picture of ancient hospitality at its most generous. He runs to meet them, bows, and hurries to prepare a meal of fresh bread, a tender calf, curds and milk. During the meal one of the visitors announces that Sarah will have a son within the year. Sarah, listening from inside the tent, laughs to herself, and the Lord asks the question that hangs over the whole story: is anything too hard for the Lord?

As the visitors leave and look toward Sodom, the chapter turns. The Lord reveals that the outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah is great, and Abraham stands before him and begins to plead for the city where his nephew Lot lives. The conversation that follows is one of the most remarkable exchanges between a human being and God anywhere in Scripture.

=== 19
Two of the visitors, now called angels, arrive at Sodom in the evening. Lot is sitting in the gateway of the city, where the town's elders and men of standing gathered. He presses the strangers to stay in his house.

What happens that night confirms everything the outcry suggested. The men of the city, young and old, surround the house and demand that Lot hand over his guests. The scene is dark and violent, and Lot's own response is deeply troubling. The angels intervene, strike the crowd with blindness, and urge Lot to take his family and flee, because the city is about to be destroyed.

Even then Lot hesitates, and the angels must take him by the hand. The Lord rains down sulfur and fire on Sodom and Gomorrah, and Lot's wife, looking back, becomes a pillar of salt. The chapter ends in a cave in the hills, where Lot's daughters, believing there is no man left to give them children, make their father drunk on two nights and conceive by him without his knowing. From their sons come two peoples who will later be Israel's neighbours: the Moabites and the Ammonites.

=== 20
After the destruction of Sodom, Abraham moves south into the Negev and stays for a time in Gerar, a town near the coastal plain. There, remarkably, he repeats the same deception he used in Egypt years before. He says of Sarah, she is my sister, and Abimelech, king of Gerar, takes her into his household.

The timing could hardly be more dangerous. Sarah has just been promised a son within the year, and now the promised mother is in another man's house. God intervenes directly, warning Abimelech in a dream that he is a dead man because the woman is married.

The pagan king turns out to be more honourable than the patriarch. Abimelech protests his innocence, confronts Abraham, and returns Sarah with gifts of livestock, servants and silver. Abraham's explanation is revealing: he assumed there was no fear of God in this place. Then Abraham, called a prophet by God himself, prays for Abimelech, and the household is healed.

=== 21
At last, the promise is kept. The chapter opens simply: the Lord visited Sarah as he had said. She conceives and gives birth to a son in Abraham's old age, at the very time God spoke of, and Abraham names him Isaac. Sarah, who once laughed in disbelief, now laughs with joy.

But the birth of Isaac brings an old tension to the surface. At the feast celebrating Isaac's weaning, Sarah sees Ishmael, now a teenager, mocking, and she demands that Abraham send Hagar and her son away. Abraham is deeply distressed, but God tells him to listen to Sarah: Isaac is the son of promise, though Ishmael too will become a nation.

Hagar and Ishmael wander in the wilderness of Beersheba until the water runs out, and once again God hears and provides. The chapter closes with Abraham making a treaty with Abimelech over a well that Abimelech's servants had seized. The place is named Beersheba, the well of the oath, and Abraham plants a tamarisk tree there and calls on the name of the Lord, the Everlasting God.

=== 22
Some years have passed. Isaac is no longer a small child; he is old enough to carry a load of wood up a mountain. The chapter opens with a sentence that tells the reader something Abraham does not know: God tested Abraham.

The command is devastating. Take your son, your only son, whom you love, Isaac, and go to the land of Moriah, and offer him there as a burnt offering. Everything the long story has built up, the promise, the waiting, the miracle of Isaac's birth, now hangs on a knife's edge.

The narrative is spare and controlled, and much of its power lies in what it leaves unsaid. Abraham rises early. Father and son walk together. Isaac asks a single question about the lamb. In the end, God stops Abraham's hand and provides a ram caught in a thicket, and Abraham names the place, the Lord will provide. The promise is then repeated with a solemn oath. Centuries later, Chronicles records that Solomon built the temple in Jerusalem on Mount Moriah.

=== 23
Many years have passed. The last time we saw Sarah, she was laughing at the birth of her son. Now, at the age of a hundred and twenty-seven, she dies at Hebron in the land of Canaan, and Abraham mourns and weeps for her.

The rest of the chapter is taken up with what might seem an ordinary business transaction: Abraham needs a place to bury his wife. He is still a foreigner in the land God has promised him, without a single plot of his own. He approaches the Hittites who live in Hebron and asks to buy the cave of Machpelah from a man named Ephron.

The negotiation that follows is courteous, public and careful. Ephron offers the field as a gift, then names its worth, four hundred shekels of silver, almost in passing, and Abraham pays it in full in front of witnesses. It is a small but significant moment. The first piece of the promised land that Abraham legally owns is a grave, and here Sarah is buried, as later Abraham himself, Isaac, Rebekah, Leah and Jacob will be.

=== 24
Abraham is now very old, and the future of the promise depends on Isaac, who is still unmarried. Abraham is determined that his son will not marry a Canaanite woman, and equally determined that Isaac must not leave the promised land. So he sends his senior servant, under a solemn oath, back to his relatives in Mesopotamia to find a wife for Isaac.

This is the longest chapter in Genesis, and it is told with great warmth and detail. The servant travels with ten camels to the city of Nahor, Abraham's brother, and stops at the well outside the town as the women come out to draw water. There he prays for a sign: may the woman who offers water to him and his camels be the one God has chosen.

Before he has finished praying, Rebekah appears, granddaughter of Nahor. What follows is a story of hospitality, negotiation with her brother Laban, and, above all, providence. The servant repeatedly recognises the hand of God in every detail. And Rebekah, asked whether she will go with this man, answers in three words: I will go.

=== 25
The story of Abraham draws to its close. He takes another wife, Keturah, who bears him several sons, among them Midian, whose descendants will reappear in the days of Moses. But Abraham gives everything he owns to Isaac, and sends the other sons away to the east.

Abraham dies at a hundred and seventy-five, an old man and full of years, and his sons Isaac and Ishmael bury him beside Sarah in the cave of Machpelah. A short genealogy follows, showing that God kept his promise to Ishmael too: twelve sons, tribal leaders who settle in the regions toward Egypt and Assyria.

Then the focus moves to Isaac. Like Sarah before her, Rebekah is unable to conceive, until Isaac prays and the Lord answers. She carries twins who struggle within her, and the Lord tells her that two nations are in her womb, and the older will serve the younger. Esau is born first, red and hairy, and Jacob follows, gripping his brother's heel. The chapter ends with a scene that reveals both brothers' characters: a hungry Esau trading his birthright for a bowl of red stew.

=== 26
Isaac is now the head of the family, and his story often echoes his father's. A famine strikes the land, as one did in Abraham's day, and Isaac moves to Gerar, where Abimelech, king of the Philistines, is ruling. The Lord appears to him and tells him not to go down to Egypt, and repeats to Isaac the promises he made to Abraham: land, descendants and blessing for all nations.

Then Isaac repeats his father's mistake. He tells the men of Gerar that Rebekah is his sister, and once again the deception is discovered by a foreign king.

The rest of the chapter is about wells. Isaac prospers so greatly that the Philistines grow envious and fill in the wells his father had dug. Isaac reopens them and digs new ones, but each time the local herdsmen dispute them, he moves on rather than fight, until at last he finds room at a well he names Rehoboth. Eventually Abimelech comes to make peace. The chapter ends with a brief, uncomfortable note: Esau marries two Hittite women, and they make life bitter for Isaac and Rebekah.

=== 27
Isaac is old and his eyesight has failed, and he believes his death is near. He calls for Esau, his favourite son, and asks him to go hunting and prepare the kind of savoury meal he loves, so that he can give him his blessing before he dies.

In the ancient world, a father's final blessing was more than a wish. It was a solemn act that passed on the family's future. But Rebekah is listening, and she acts at once. She dresses Jacob in Esau's clothes, covers his hands and neck with goatskins, and sends him in with a meal of her own making.

The scene that follows is tense and painful, full of questions and close calls, as the blind father tries to discern which son is standing before him. When Esau returns, the deception is exposed, and his grief is one of the most moving moments in Genesis. The chapter ends with Esau planning to kill his brother once their father has died, and Rebekah planning Jacob's escape.

=== 28
Jacob must leave home. At Rebekah's urging, Isaac sends him to her brother Laban in Paddan-aram, in northern Mesopotamia, to find a wife from among their own relatives, and Isaac blesses him again, this time knowingly, passing on the blessing of Abraham. Esau, realising how much his Canaanite wives displease his parents, marries a daughter of Ishmael.

Then the story follows Jacob alone on the road north. He stops for the night at a certain place, takes a stone for a pillow and falls asleep. In a dream he sees a ladder resting on the earth with its top reaching heaven, and God's angels going up and down it, and the Lord standing above it.

God speaks to Jacob for the first time, repeating the promises given to his grandfather and father, and adding a personal assurance: I am with you and will watch over you wherever you go. Jacob wakes in awe. He sets up his stone pillow as a pillar, pours oil on it, and names the place Bethel, the house of God. His response is a vow, cautious and conditional, that if God keeps him safe, the Lord will be his God.

=== 29
Jacob continues his journey to the east and arrives at a well in the open country near Harran, where shepherds are waiting with their flocks. As he talks with them, Rachel, the daughter of his uncle Laban, arrives with her father's sheep. Jacob rolls the heavy stone from the mouth of the well, waters her flock, kisses her and weeps.

Laban welcomes his nephew, and after a month he asks what wages Jacob would like. Jacob loves Rachel, and he offers seven years of work for her hand. The years seem like only a few days to him because of his love for her.

But the deceiver is about to be deceived. On the wedding night Laban substitutes his older daughter, Leah, and Jacob does not discover the trick until morning. Laban's excuse touches a nerve: in our country the younger is not given in marriage before the firstborn. Jacob marries Rachel a week later, in exchange for seven more years of work. The chapter ends with Leah, the unloved wife, giving birth to four sons: Reuben, Simeon, Levi and Judah. Her sons' names record her longing to be loved by her husband.

=== 30
The rivalry between the two sisters now takes over the household. Leah has given birth to four sons, while Rachel remains barren, and her jealousy boils over in an anguished demand to Jacob: give me children, or I'll die.

What follows is a contest of births. Rachel gives her servant Bilhah to Jacob, and Bilhah bears two sons. Leah responds by giving her own servant Zilpah, who bears two more. A strange episode involving mandrakes, a plant believed to help with fertility, leads to further sons for Leah and a daughter, Dinah. Each child's name records the mood of the moment, triumph, grief or hope. At last God remembers Rachel, and she gives birth to Joseph.

With Joseph's birth, Jacob is ready to go home, but Laban wants him to stay, having realised that the Lord has blessed him because of Jacob. The two men agree on a wage of speckled and spotted animals from the flocks. Using a breeding method that reflects the beliefs of the time, though Jacob will later credit God rather than his own skill, Jacob's flocks multiply and he becomes exceedingly prosperous.

=== 31
After twenty years with Laban, the atmosphere has turned cold. Laban's sons are complaining that Jacob has grown rich at their father's expense, and Laban's attitude toward him has changed. Then the Lord speaks: go back to the land of your fathers, and I will be with you.

Jacob calls Rachel and Leah to the fields and explains how their father has cheated him, changing his wages ten times, and how God has nevertheless protected him. The sisters agree. While Laban is away shearing his sheep, Jacob gathers his family and flocks and sets off for Canaan. Unknown to him, Rachel takes her father's household gods.

Laban pursues them and catches up in the hill country of Gilead, east of the Jordan, but God warns him in a dream not to harm Jacob. The confrontation that follows is a tense mixture of accusation, searching, and a little comedy, as Rachel hides the household gods under a camel's saddle. In the end the two men make a covenant, setting up a heap of stones as a boundary between them. Laban calls it Mizpah, saying, may the Lord watch between you and me when we are absent from one another.

=== 32
Jacob has made peace with Laban, but a far greater danger lies ahead. Twenty years ago, he fled from his brother Esau, who had sworn to kill him. Now he must face him again.

He sends messengers ahead to Esau in the land of Seir, the region of Edom, and they return with alarming news: Esau is coming to meet him with four hundred men. Jacob is terrified. He divides his household into two camps, prays to God with an honesty we have not heard from him before, and then sends wave after wave of gifts ahead, hoping to soften his brother's anger.

That night, alone by the ford of the Jabbok River, Jacob has the most mysterious encounter in Genesis. A man wrestles with him until daybreak. The man touches Jacob's hip and wrenches it, yet Jacob refuses to let go without a blessing. He receives a new name: Israel, because he has struggled with God and with men and has prevailed. Jacob names the place Peniel, the face of God, and limps on into the sunrise.

=== 33
Jacob looks up from the river and sees Esau coming with his four hundred men. He arranges his family in order, the servants and their children first, then Leah and her children, and Rachel and Joseph last of all. Then he goes ahead of them, bowing to the ground seven times as he approaches his brother.

What happens next is not what Jacob feared. Esau runs to meet him, embraces him, throws his arms around his neck and kisses him, and they weep. After twenty years of dread, the reunion is overwhelmed with grace. Jacob insists that Esau accept his gifts, and says that seeing Esau's face is like seeing the face of God.

Esau offers to travel with him, but Jacob politely declines, and the brothers part. Rather than following Esau to Seir, Jacob travels to Sukkoth and then on to the town of Shechem in Canaan. There he buys a piece of land and builds an altar, naming it El Elohe Israel, which means God, the God of Israel.

=== 34
Jacob has settled near the town of Shechem, buying land and building an altar there. But the peace of this new home is about to be shattered.

Dinah, the daughter of Jacob and Leah, goes out to visit the women of the land. Shechem, the son of the local ruler Hamor, sees her, takes her and violates her. Afterwards he wants to marry her, and his father comes to Jacob to arrange the marriage and propose that the two peoples join together. This is a hard chapter, and the narrator does not soften it.

Jacob's sons are filled with grief and fury. They respond with deceit, agreeing to the marriages on condition that every man in the city be circumcised. While the men are still in pain, Simeon and Levi, Dinah's full brothers, attack the city and kill every male. The other brothers plunder it. Jacob's reaction is to rebuke his sons for making him a stench among the people of the land, and the chapter ends with their angry reply. The episode will be remembered when Jacob blesses his sons at the end of his life.

=== 35
After the violence at Shechem, God speaks to Jacob: go up to Bethel and settle there, and build an altar to the God who appeared to you when you fled from your brother. It is a call back to the place where God first met him.

Before they set out, Jacob tells his household to get rid of their foreign gods, purify themselves and change their clothes, and they bury the idols under the oak near Shechem. At Bethel God appears to Jacob again, confirms his new name, Israel, and repeats the promise of nations and kings and the land.

But grief follows close behind. At Bethel, Rebekah's nurse, Deborah, dies and is buried beneath an oak. Then, on the road to Ephrath, which is Bethlehem, Rachel dies giving birth to her second son. With her last breath she names him Ben-oni, son of my sorrow, but Jacob renames him Benjamin, son of my right hand. A brief and shameful note follows about Reuben and his father's concubine Bilhah. The twelve sons of Jacob are then listed together for the first time, and the chapter closes with the death of Isaac, buried by his sons Esau and Jacob.

=== 36
Before the story turns to the sons of Jacob, Genesis pauses to tell what became of Esau. This chapter records his wives, his sons and grandsons, and the chiefs and kings who descended from him.

Esau settles in the hill country of Seir, south and east of the Dead Sea, and his descendants become the nation of Edom. The name Edom means red, recalling both Esau's appearance at birth and the red stew for which he sold his birthright. The chapter includes the original inhabitants of the land, the Horites, and a list of kings who reigned in Edom before any king reigned over Israel.

Before the twins were born, the Lord told Rebekah that two nations were in her womb. Here is the first of them to rise, a people with chiefs and kings, while Jacob's sons are still shepherds in tents. Edom will remain a neighbour, and often a rival, of Israel throughout the Old Testament, down to the brief prophecy of Obadiah.

=== 37
The final section of Genesis begins with the words: this is the account of Jacob. But its central character is Jacob's son Joseph. Jacob is now living in Canaan, where his father had lived as a foreigner, and Joseph is seventeen, tending the flocks with his brothers.

The family is deeply troubled. Jacob loves Joseph more than any of his other sons, because Joseph was born to him in his old age, and he makes him a robe of many colours, a visible sign of favour. Joseph brings his father a bad report about his brothers, and then he tells them about two dreams in which his family bow down to him. His brothers' resentment turns to hatred.

When Jacob sends Joseph to check on his brothers, who are grazing the flocks far to the north near Dothan, they see him coming in the distance and plot to kill him. Reuben persuades them to throw him into an empty pit instead, intending to rescue him later, but Judah proposes selling him to a passing caravan of traders. The chapter ends with a grieving father holding a bloodstained robe, and Joseph being sold in Egypt to Potiphar, an official of Pharaoh.

=== 38
Just as the Joseph story begins, it is interrupted. Joseph has been sold into Egypt, and the narrative turns abruptly to his brother Judah, the one who suggested selling him. Judah leaves his brothers and settles among the Canaanites, marrying a Canaanite woman who bears him three sons.

His eldest son, Er, marries a woman named Tamar, but he dies, and so does his brother Onan, who refuses to fulfil his duty to provide an heir for his dead brother. Judah, fearing for his last son Shelah, sends Tamar back to her father's house and never keeps his promise to give her to Shelah in marriage.

Years pass, and Tamar takes matters into her own hands, at the risk of her life. The story exposes Judah's double standards and ends with his remarkable admission: she is more righteous than I. Tamar gives birth to twins, Perez and Zerah. The episode matters more than it might seem, because it marks the beginning of Judah's change, and because Perez will become an ancestor of King David.

=== 39
The story returns to Egypt. Joseph has been bought by Potiphar, the captain of Pharaoh's guard, and the narrative repeats a phrase that runs through this chapter like a refrain: the Lord was with Joseph.

Everything Joseph does prospers. Potiphar puts him in charge of his entire household, and the Lord blesses the house because of him. But Joseph is also well-built and handsome, and Potiphar's wife begins to pressure him, day after day, to sleep with her. Joseph refuses, unwilling to betray his master or to sin against God.

One day, when no other servants are in the house, she seizes his cloak and he runs out, leaving it in her hands. She uses it as evidence against him, and Potiphar has Joseph thrown into the prison where the king's prisoners are kept. Joseph's fortunes have collapsed for the second time. Yet even here, the refrain returns. The Lord is with him, and the prison warden soon puts him in charge of all the other prisoners.

=== 40
Joseph is in prison, but even here he has been entrusted with responsibility. Some time later, two new prisoners arrive: Pharaoh's chief cupbearer and his chief baker, both of whom have offended their master. These were trusted positions in the royal court, because they had direct access to the king's food and drink.

One night, both men have troubling dreams, and in the morning they look dejected, because there is no one to interpret them. In Egypt dream interpretation was a skilled art, practised by professionals. Joseph's response points away from himself: do not interpretations belong to God? Tell me your dreams.

Joseph interprets both dreams. One brings good news and one brings very bad news, and within three days both come true, exactly as he said. Joseph asks the restored cupbearer to remember him and mention him to Pharaoh. The chapter ends with one of the most quietly painful lines in the story: the chief cupbearer did not remember Joseph; he forgot him.

=== 41
Two full years have passed. Joseph is still in prison, forgotten. Then Pharaoh has two dreams, of seven fat cows devoured by seven gaunt ones, and of seven full heads of grain swallowed by seven thin and scorched ones. None of Egypt's magicians or wise men can interpret them.

Only now does the cupbearer remember the young Hebrew in the prison. Joseph is brought out in a hurry, shaved and dressed in fresh clothes, as was fitting for an audience with the king, and he stands before the most powerful man in the world. Once again he says that the answer will come from God, not from himself.

The interpretation is clear: seven years of abundance are coming, followed by seven years of severe famine. Joseph recommends a plan to store grain during the good years, and Pharaoh, recognising the Spirit of God in him, sets him over all Egypt, second only to the king. At thirty years old, thirteen years after he was sold, Joseph is given a new Egyptian name and a wife, and he has two sons, Manasseh and Ephraim. As the famine spreads across the world, everyone comes to Egypt to buy grain from Joseph.

=== 42
The famine has reached Canaan. Jacob hears that there is grain in Egypt and sends ten of his sons down to buy food, keeping back Benjamin, Rachel's other son, for fear that harm might come to him.

When the brothers arrive, they come before the governor of the land and bow down to him with their faces to the ground. It is Joseph, and he recognises them at once, though they do not recognise him. More than twenty years have passed, and he is dressed and speaking as an Egyptian. He remembers his dreams.

What follows is a carefully staged test. Joseph speaks harshly, accuses them of being spies, holds them in custody for three days, and then keeps Simeon as a hostage while the others return home to bring back their youngest brother as proof of their story. The brothers begin to speak among themselves about the guilt they have carried for what they did to Joseph, not realising he understands every word. At their lodging on the way, one of them finds his silver in the mouth of his sack, and at home every man finds his own, and their fear deepens. Jacob refuses to let Benjamin go.

=== 43
The famine is severe, and the grain the brothers brought from Egypt is running out. Jacob tells his sons to go back and buy more, but Judah reminds him that the governor warned them not to return without their youngest brother. Jacob is torn between the threat of starvation and the fear of losing another son of Rachel.

Here Judah steps forward in a new way. The man who once proposed selling Joseph now offers himself as a guarantee for Benjamin's safety. Jacob at last agrees, sending them with gifts, double the silver, and a prayer that God Almighty will grant them mercy.

In Egypt the brothers are brought, to their alarm, into Joseph's own house. Instead of punishment, they are welcomed, Simeon is released, and they are served a feast. Joseph is overwhelmed with emotion when he sees Benjamin and has to leave the room to weep in private. At the meal, the brothers are amazed to find themselves seated in order of age, and Benjamin is given five times as much as anyone else.

=== 44
The brothers have feasted in Joseph's house, and for once all is well. They set off for home at dawn with their donkeys loaded with grain, Simeon and Benjamin safely among them. But Joseph has arranged one final test. His own silver cup has been hidden in Benjamin's sack.

Joseph's steward overtakes them on the road and accuses them of theft. The brothers are so confident of their innocence that they rashly promise death to the guilty man and slavery for the rest. The search goes from the oldest to the youngest, and the cup is found in Benjamin's sack.

Years ago these men let Rachel's son be carried off to Egypt. Now her other son stands accused, and the rest of them are free to go home without him. Instead, they tear their clothes, reload their donkeys and return to the city together. Before Joseph, Judah steps forward and makes a long and moving plea, describing his father's love for Benjamin and his grief over the son he lost. He ends by offering himself as a slave in Benjamin's place. It is one of the great speeches of the Bible, and it marks how much Judah has changed.

=== 45
Judah has just offered himself as a slave in place of Benjamin. Joseph can no longer control himself. He sends out all his Egyptian attendants, and, weeping so loudly that the Egyptians and Pharaoh's household hear it, he makes himself known to his brothers. I am Joseph. Is my father still alive?

His brothers are terrified, unable to answer. But Joseph draws them close and speaks words that interpret the whole story: do not be distressed or angry with yourselves for selling me here, because God sent me ahead of you to preserve life. Five years of famine still lie ahead, and he urges them to bring their father and all their families down to Egypt, to live in the land of Goshen, close to him.

Pharaoh hears the news and is delighted, sending wagons to bring the family. Joseph sends his brothers back loaded with provisions and gifts, with a parting word: do not quarrel on the way. When they tell Jacob that Joseph is alive and ruling Egypt, the old man is stunned and does not believe them, until he hears all that Joseph said and sees the wagons. Then his spirit revives, and he says, I will go and see him before I die.

=== 46
Jacob sets out from Hebron with everything he owns, and at Beersheba, on the southern edge of the promised land, he stops to offer sacrifices. It is a significant place. Abraham and Isaac lived and worshipped here, and Jacob is about to leave the land God promised them.

That night God speaks to Jacob in a vision, for the last time in Genesis. Do not be afraid to go down to Egypt, for I will make you into a great nation there. I will go down with you, and I will surely bring you back again. The words recall the promise to Abraham that his descendants would be strangers in another land before returning.

The chapter then lists the names of Jacob's family who travelled to Egypt, arranged by the sons of each of his wives, for a total of seventy. Then comes the long-awaited reunion. Joseph harnesses his chariot and goes to meet his father in Goshen, and he throws his arms around him and weeps for a long time. Jacob says that he is now ready to die, having seen his son alive. Joseph then prepares his family to meet Pharaoh, advising them to describe themselves as shepherds, since shepherds were detestable to the Egyptians and they would be given land apart.

=== 47
Joseph presents five of his brothers to Pharaoh, and they ask permission to live in the land of Goshen, in the eastern part of the Nile Delta, where there is good grazing for their flocks. Pharaoh agrees. Then Joseph brings his elderly father before the king, and Jacob blesses Pharaoh. When asked his age, Jacob describes his life of a hundred and thirty years as few and difficult.

The middle of the chapter turns to Joseph's management of the famine. As the years of hunger continue, the people of Egypt spend their money, then trade their livestock, and finally sell their land and themselves to Pharaoh in exchange for grain. Joseph establishes a law that a fifth of the harvest belongs to Pharaoh. The Egyptians themselves respond with gratitude: you have saved our lives.

Meanwhile, Israel's family settles and prospers in Goshen. Jacob lives there for seventeen years. As his death approaches, he calls Joseph and makes him swear, with his hand under his thigh, not to bury him in Egypt, but to carry him back to the burial place of his fathers in Canaan.

=== 48
Some time later, Joseph is told that his father is ill. He goes to see him, bringing his two sons, Manasseh and Ephraim, who were born in Egypt before the famine. Jacob gathers his strength and sits up in bed.

He recalls the day God appeared to him at Luz, which is Bethel, and repeated the promise of land and descendants. Then he does something unexpected: he claims Joseph's two sons as his own, equal to his eldest sons Reuben and Simeon. In this way, Joseph receives a double portion, and Ephraim and Manasseh will later become tribes in Israel. Jacob also remembers burying Rachel on the road to Bethlehem.

When Joseph brings the boys forward for the blessing, he positions the older son, Manasseh, at Jacob's right hand and the younger, Ephraim, at his left. But the old man crosses his arms, placing his right hand on Ephraim's head. Joseph tries to correct him, but Jacob knows what he is doing. Once again, as so often in Genesis, the younger is placed ahead of the older.

=== 49
Jacob is near the end of his life, and he calls his twelve sons together to tell them what will happen to them in the days to come. These final words are poetry, the longest poem in Genesis, and they are part blessing, part prophecy, and part verdict on the past.

The first three sons find their history catching up with them. Reuben, the firstborn, loses his pre-eminence because of what he did with his father's concubine. Simeon and Levi are condemned for their violence at Shechem. Then the poem turns to Judah, and the tone changes completely. He is pictured as a lion, and the sceptre will not depart from him until Shiloh comes, and the allegiance of the nations is his. In time Israel's kings will come from Judah, beginning with David. Joseph also receives a long and rich blessing.

After the blessings, Jacob gives his final instructions, asking to be buried with Abraham and Isaac in the cave of Machpelah. Then he draws his feet up into the bed, breathes his last, and is gathered to his people.

=== 50
Jacob has died, and Joseph throws himself on his father's face, weeping and kissing him. He orders the Egyptian physicians to embalm his father, a process that takes forty days, and Egypt mourns for seventy days, a mark of the honour given to the father of Pharaoh's governor.

Then Joseph keeps his promise. A great procession of Egyptian officials, chariots and horsemen goes up with the family to Canaan, and Jacob is buried in the cave of Machpelah. After their return, Joseph's brothers are afraid that, with their father gone, Joseph will finally take revenge. They send a message pleading for forgiveness. Joseph weeps, and he gives the answer that sums up the whole story: what you intended against me for evil, God intended for good, to preserve the lives of many people.

The book ends with Joseph's own death at a hundred and ten. Before he dies, he tells his brothers that God will surely visit them and bring them up to the land he promised to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. He makes them swear to carry his bones with them when that day comes. Genesis closes with a coffin in Egypt, waiting.
