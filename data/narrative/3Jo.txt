=== intro
Third John is the most personal of John's letters, and in the original Greek the shortest book in the New Testament. Like Second John, it comes from the elder, the aged apostle John, living in Ephesus and watching over the churches of the province of Asia. The voice, the vocabulary and the concerns are the same as in the two letters before it.

This time the elder writes to a single individual, a man named Gaius. Gaius was one of the most common names in the Roman world. He belongs to a congregation in one of the towns of the region, and the elder plainly loves and trusts him. The elder counts him among his children.

The letter opens, as many ordinary letters of the time did, with a wish for good health. Then it turns to the matter at hand, which is the same practical issue that lay behind Second John: hospitality for travelling teachers. A group of believers, whom the elder simply calls the brothers, have gone out on behalf of the name of Christ, preaching from place to place and accepting nothing from the Gentiles. Gaius has welcomed them, although they were strangers to him, and they have returned to the elder's church with glowing reports of his kindness. Now they are travelling again, and the elder asks Gaius to send them on their way well supplied.

Second John warned against welcoming false teachers. Third John is concerned that true ones are being turned away. A leader in the congregation named Diotrephes, who loves to be first, has refused to accept the elder's authority. He ignored an earlier letter, spread malicious talk about the elder, refused to receive the travelling brothers, and expelled from the church those who did. This is the only place he is mentioned.

Against him the elder sets two other men: Gaius, who has done well, and Demetrius, who has a good report from everyone. It ends, like Second John, with the elder's hope of coming soon and speaking face to face.

=== 1
The elder's letter to Gaius opens warmly. He prays for his friend's health and wellbeing, and then explains the reason for his joy: some of the brothers have come back with news of Gaius's faithfulness. For an old man who calls the believers his children, there is no better news than that they are walking in the truth.

The next paragraph comes to the heart of the matter. Gaius has shown hospitality to travelling missionaries he had never met, and the elder asks him to keep supporting them, since they took nothing from the Gentiles.

Then the mood changes abruptly with the name of Diotrephes, and a frank account of his behaviour. The elder does not dwell on it. He commends Demetrius instead, and closes with greetings from the friends around him to the friends with Gaius, each by name.
