=== intro
Galatians is the most urgent letter Paul ever wrote. There are no warm thanks at the start and no greetings to friends at the end. Paul has heard that the churches he founded in Galatia are being persuaded to adopt a different message, and he writes in alarm, with anger and with deep affection, to call them back before it is too late.

To understand the letter we need to go back to the beginning of Paul's missionary work, as Acts describes it. The church at Antioch in Syria, the first great centre of believers outside the Jewish homeland, sent out Paul and Barnabas as missionaries. Barnabas, a Jewish believer from Cyprus, had been the first to vouch for Paul in Jerusalem after his conversion, and had brought him to Antioch to teach. The two men crossed to Cyprus and then travelled into the highlands of central Asia Minor, in what is now Turkey. They preached in Pisidian Antioch, Iconium, Lystra and Derbe, towns that lay within the Roman province of Galatia. Wherever there was a synagogue they began there, and Gentiles as well as Jews believed. They also met fierce opposition. At Lystra Paul was stoned and left for dead. Paul himself recalls that he first came to the Galatians because of an illness, and that they welcomed him as if he were an angel of God.

These are most probably the churches Paul is writing to, though some scholars think he means communities further north, among the Celtic people who had given Galatia its name. Either way, they were mainly Gentiles, people who had once worshipped the gods of their own towns and had turned to the God of Israel through faith in Jesus.

After Paul left, other teachers arrived. Paul never names them, but the letter makes their message clear. They were Jewish believers in Jesus, and they insisted that Gentile converts must be circumcised and keep the law of Moses, including its calendar of holy days, if they were truly to belong to the people of God. They seem also to have cast doubt on Paul himself, suggesting that he was a second-hand apostle, dependent on the leaders in Jerusalem, who had watered down the message to make it easier for Gentiles. Some of the Galatians were being persuaded.

This was not a small matter in the early church. The question of whether Gentile believers had to become Jews was argued in Antioch, in Jerusalem, and wherever Jewish and Gentile believers met. Acts tells of a gathering of apostles and elders in Jerusalem called to settle it. When exactly Galatians was written, and how it relates to that meeting, is one of the long-standing puzzles of the New Testament. If, as many believe, Paul wrote soon after his first journey, in the late forties of the first century, it may be the earliest of all his letters. Others place it several years later, during his travels in the fifties.

For Paul, the issue went to the heart of the gospel. If Gentiles had to keep the law of Moses to be accepted by God, then the death of Christ was not enough. He writes to defend both his message and his calling, and he does so in three movements. First he tells his own story: how he received the gospel by revelation from Jesus Christ, not from any human teacher, how his dealings with the apostles in Jerusalem confirmed it, and how he once confronted Peter himself at Antioch. Then he argues from the Galatians' own experience of the Holy Spirit and from the Scriptures, returning again and again to Abraham, the father of the Jewish people, who was counted righteous by faith centuries before the law was given. Finally he shows what the freedom he defends looks like in practice: not a licence to do as one pleases, but a life led by the Spirit, marked by love and by bearing one another's burdens. He closes with a few lines in his own large handwriting.

Many of the ideas Paul sets out here briefly and urgently he would later develop at greater length, and in a calmer tone, in his letter to the Romans.

=== 1
Paul opens with a description of himself that already signals the conflict at the heart of the letter. He is an apostle, sent not by any human authority but by Jesus Christ and God the Father. His rivals in Galatia have questioned exactly that.

In almost every other letter, Paul follows his greeting with a thanksgiving for his readers. Here he leaves it out and goes straight to his astonishment. The Galatians are so quickly deserting the one who called them and turning to a different gospel, which, Paul says, is no gospel at all. Twice he pronounces a curse on anyone who preaches another message, even an angel from heaven.

Then he begins to tell his own story. He was once a zealous persecutor of the church, advancing in Judaism beyond his contemporaries, until God revealed his Son to him. Paul's account of the years that followed is carefully chosen. He went to Arabia and back to Damascus, he visited Jerusalem only after three years, and even then he stayed just fifteen days with Cephas, that is Peter, and met James the brother of the Lord. His point is that his gospel did not come from the leaders in Jerusalem.

=== 2
Paul continues his account of his own life. Fourteen years later, he says, he went up to Jerusalem again, with Barnabas, his partner on the first missionary journey, and with Titus, a Greek believer. Which of the visits recorded in Acts this was is debated. What matters to Paul is the outcome. He set out his gospel before the leaders, and they added nothing to it. Titus was not required to be circumcised. James, Cephas and John recognised that Paul had been entrusted with the gospel to the Gentiles, and gave him the right hand of fellowship.

Then Paul tells of a moment when that agreement was put to the test. At Antioch, Peter had been eating freely with Gentile believers. When certain men came from James, Peter drew back and ate only with Jews, and the other Jewish believers followed, even Barnabas. Shared meals were at the centre of the early church's life, and to separate at table was to say that Gentile believers were not fully part of God's people.

Paul confronted Peter publicly. His account of what he said leads directly into the central claim of the letter: a person is justified not by works of the law, but by faith in Jesus Christ.

=== 3
With his own story told, Paul now turns to the Galatians themselves, and he begins with a rebuke. O foolish Galatians, he writes, who has bewitched you? When he first preached among them, Christ crucified was portrayed before their very eyes.

Paul appeals to their own experience. They received the Holy Spirit, and saw God work miracles among them, by believing what they heard, not by keeping the law. Why would they now try to complete by human effort what God began by his Spirit?

Then Paul turns to Scripture, and above all to Abraham. The rival teachers probably appealed to Abraham too, since circumcision began with him. But Paul goes back to the earlier word in Genesis: Abraham believed God, and it was credited to him as righteousness. God promised Abraham that all nations would be blessed through him, and that promise came four hundred and thirty years before the law was given at Sinai. Paul then asks what the law was for, and he compares it to a guardian who supervised a child until the child came of age. The chapter ends with a famous description of the new community in Christ, where there is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female.

=== 4
Paul has just compared the law to a guardian, and the Galatians to heirs who have now come of age. He develops that picture here. In the ancient world an heir who was still a child had no more freedom than a slave, until the time set by his father. When the time had fully come, Paul says, God sent his Son to redeem those under the law, and sent his Spirit into their hearts, so that they cry Abba, Father.

Then he asks why, having come to know God, the Galatians want to go back to slavery. Before their conversion they served gods that were no gods. Now they are beginning to observe special days and seasons, and Paul treats this as a return to the same bondage.

In the middle of the chapter Paul's tone becomes intensely personal. He reminds them how he first came to them, ill, and how warmly they received him. He calls them his children, and pictures himself in the pains of childbirth again until Christ is formed in them. The chapter ends with an illustration drawn from Genesis, the story of Abraham's two sons, Ishmael born to the slave woman Hagar and Isaac born to the free woman Sarah, which Paul reads as a picture of two covenants.

=== 5
Paul has finished his argument from Abraham's two sons, and he states its conclusion in a single sentence: it is for freedom that Christ has set us free. Now he spells out what is at stake in practical terms.

If the Galatian men accept circumcision as a requirement for belonging to God's people, Paul warns, they commit themselves to keeping the whole law, and Christ will be of no benefit to them. He speaks with unusual sharpness about the teachers who are unsettling them. There seems to have been a rumour that Paul himself still preached circumcision when it suited him, and he dismisses it.

Then the chapter turns. Freedom from the law, Paul insists, is not freedom to indulge the flesh. It is freedom to serve one another in love, which fulfils the whole law. He describes two ways of living that are opposed to one another: the acts of the flesh, which he lists, and the fruit of the Spirit, in a short list that has become one of the best known passages in his letters. The Galatian churches, it seems, were also troubled by quarrelling, and Paul warns them against biting and devouring one another.

=== 6
Paul has described life led by the Spirit. In the final chapter he gives a few practical examples of what that looks like among believers: gently restoring someone caught in wrongdoing, carrying one another's burdens, supporting those who teach them, and not growing weary in doing good. He uses the farmer's picture of sowing and reaping, which his readers in an agricultural region would have known well.

Then Paul writes the closing lines in his own hand. Letters in his world were usually written out by a secretary, and Paul did this in several of his letters as a personal mark. Here he draws attention to the large letters he is using. He does not say why, though it may be for emphasis.

In these last lines he returns one final time to the teachers who are pressing circumcision on the Galatians. He accuses them of wanting to make a good impression and to avoid persecution. For his own part he will boast only in the cross of Christ. What counts, he says, is neither circumcision nor uncircumcision, but a new creation. He ends by saying that he bears on his body the marks of Jesus, most likely the scars of the beatings and the stoning he had suffered, perhaps some of them in Galatia itself.
