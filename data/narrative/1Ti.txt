=== intro
The two letters to Timothy and the letter to Titus are usually grouped together and called the pastoral letters, because each is addressed to a trusted colleague who has been left in charge of churches. Unlike most of Paul's letters, they are written not to a congregation but to a single person, and they read like a senior leader's instructions to a deputy he knows well and trusts.

Timothy is one of the most familiar figures in Paul's story. Acts introduces him in the town of Lystra, in the hill country of what is now southern Turkey, the son of a believing Jewish mother and a Greek father. Paul took him on as a young companion at the start of his second great journey, and Timothy stayed with him for years. He travelled through Macedonia and Greece, was sent back alone to strengthen the church in Thessalonica, carried messages to Corinth, and appears as co-sender of several of Paul's letters. Paul writes of him with unusual affection, as a son.

The setting of this letter is Ephesus, the great city of the Roman province of Asia, on the western coast of Asia Minor, famous across the empire for its temple of Artemis. Paul had spent nearly three years there, according to Acts, and the church had grown strong. When he later met the Ephesian elders for the last time at Miletus, he warned them that savage wolves would come in among them, and that men from their own number would rise up and distort the truth. This letter suggests that the warning has come true. Certain teachers in the church are spreading speculations, myths and endless genealogies, arguing about the law, forbidding marriage and certain foods, and treating religion as a way to make money. Two of them, Hymenaeus and Alexander, are named.

Paul has left for Macedonia and asked Timothy to stay on in Ephesus and set things right. He hopes to come back soon, but writes in case he is delayed, so that Timothy will know how people ought to conduct themselves in the household of God. This trip to Macedonia belongs to the years after Acts closes. Paul was released from his first imprisonment in Rome, travelled again in the east, and was later arrested a second time and executed, in the middle of the sixties. This letter comes from those last years.

The letter moves between personal encouragement and practical instruction. Paul charges Timothy to confront the false teachers and remembers his own past as a persecutor shown mercy. He gives direction on prayer and public worship, on the qualities required of overseers and deacons, on caring for widows, honouring elders, the conduct of slaves, and the dangers of wealth. Throughout, he returns to Timothy himself: his youth, his health, his gifts, and the need to guard his own life as well as his teaching.

The letter's world is one of households, and Paul pictures the church as God's household, with its own order and its own reputation to protect among outsiders. Behind all of them stands the situation in Ephesus: a church troubled by false teaching from within, and a younger man asked to stand firm in the middle of it, with his mentor far away.

=== 1
The letter opens with Paul's formal title, an apostle by the command of God, and a greeting to Timothy, his true child in the faith. The formality is deliberate. Paul's authority stands behind what his deputy is being asked to do.

Then, without a long thanksgiving, Paul comes straight to the point. He reminds Timothy why he was left in Ephesus: certain men are teaching things they should not, occupying themselves with myths, genealogies and arguments about the law that they do not understand. What exactly they taught is not fully spelled out, and much has to be pieced together from hints across the letter.

In the middle of the chapter Paul turns to his own story. He was once a blasphemer and a violent persecutor of the church, and he speaks of the mercy that met him with evident wonder, breaking into praise as he does so. Then he returns to Timothy, recalling prophecies spoken over him, and names two men who have shipwrecked their faith: Hymenaeus and Alexander.

=== 2
Having charged Timothy to deal with the false teachers, Paul begins to address the life of the church itself, starting with its prayers when it gathers. First of all, he says, prayers should be offered for everyone, including kings and those in authority. In the Roman world, where Christians were a small and often suspected minority, that instruction had practical weight. Paul grounds it in the conviction that God desires all people to be saved, and that there is one God and one mediator, Christ Jesus.

From there Paul turns to the conduct of men and women in public worship. Men are to pray without anger or quarrelling. Women are to dress modestly, without displays of wealth, and to learn quietly. Paul then gives restrictions on women teaching, and supports them with a reference to Adam and Eve.

The background is the trouble in Ephesus itself, where the false teaching had found a hearing in households, and among some of the women of the church.

=== 3
Paul now turns from the gathered worship of the church to its leadership. The false teaching in Ephesus has been spread by people within the church, and so the question of who should lead is urgent.

He describes first the overseer, a word that in these letters is used alongside the title of elder for those who led and taught the congregation. The qualities he lists are mostly about character and reputation: self-control, hospitality, the ability to teach, freedom from the love of money, a well-ordered household, and a good name among outsiders. He then turns to deacons, who served the church in practical ways, with a similar emphasis on integrity, and with a brief word to the women, who are likewise to be dignified and faithful in all things.

The chapter closes by stepping back. Paul hopes to visit soon, but writes in case he is delayed, so that Timothy will know how to conduct himself in God's household. He ends with a short confession about Christ, set out like lines of an early hymn.

=== 4
The hymn that closed chapter three celebrated the truth about Christ. Paul now turns sharply to its opposite. The Spirit has warned, he says, that in later times some will abandon the faith and follow deceiving spirits. He describes the result in Ephesus with some precision: teachers who forbid marriage and insist on abstaining from certain foods.

Paul answers with the goodness of creation. Everything God made is good, and nothing is to be rejected if it is received with thanksgiving. The words echo the first chapter of Genesis, where God looks on all he has made and calls it good.

The second half of the chapter is addressed to Timothy himself. Paul contrasts physical training, which was prized in the Greek gymnasium, with training in godliness. He tells Timothy not to let anyone look down on him because he is young. By now Timothy had served with Paul for some fifteen years, yet he was still young for a leader in that culture. Paul urges him to devote himself to the public reading of Scripture, to preaching and teaching, and not to neglect the gift given to him when the elders laid their hands on him.

=== 5
Paul has just spoken to Timothy about his own example and teaching. Now he gives guidance on how Timothy should deal with different groups in the church, beginning with a principle: treat older men as fathers, younger men as brothers, older women as mothers, younger women as sisters.

Most of the chapter concerns widows. In a world with no pensions or social safety net, a woman who lost her husband could be left destitute, and the church in Ephesus supported a number of widows from its common funds. Paul sets out who should receive that support. Families must care for their own first, and the church's help is for widows who are truly alone. There was also a recognised group of older widows, enrolled on a list, devoted to prayer and good works. Paul gives cautions about younger widows, some of whom have already turned aside to follow Satan.

The chapter then turns to elders: their honour and pay, how accusations against them should be handled, and the danger of appointing anyone too quickly. In the middle of it is a personal aside about Timothy's frequent stomach ailments.

=== 6
The final chapter brings together several threads. Paul begins with slaves, who made up a large part of the population of a city like Ephesus and certainly of its churches. He asks believing slaves to honour their masters so that the gospel will not be discredited, including those whose masters are fellow believers.

Then he returns, one last time, to the false teachers. He describes them as conceited, quarrelsome and fond of disputes about words, and he adds a further charge: they treat godliness as a means of financial gain. That leads him into a reflection on contentment and the dangers of desiring wealth, which contains some of the most quoted words in the letter about the love of money.

The tone rises toward the end. Paul turns directly to Timothy, addressing him as a man of God, and charges him before God and before Christ Jesus, who made the good confession before Pontius Pilate, to keep the command until the Lord appears. He breaks into a doxology, adds a brief word for the rich, and closes with a final appeal, calling Timothy by name to guard what has been entrusted to him.
