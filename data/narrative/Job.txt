=== intro
With the book of Job we leave behind the Persian court and the rebuilt walls of Jerusalem, and step into a different kind of book altogether. Job is the first of the Bible's books of poetry and wisdom, and its story stands apart from the history of Israel. It opens like an old tale: there was a man in the land of Uz whose name was Job.

Where Uz lay is not certain. Most likely it was somewhere east of the Jordan, toward Edom or the desert fringes of Arabia, among the people the book calls the people of the East. Job's world is one of great flocks and herds, of raiding bands from the desert, of a father who rises early to offer sacrifices for his children. Moses, the Law, the temple and the kings of Israel are never mentioned, and Job himself does not appear to be an Israelite. Many readers have felt that his world resembles that of Abraham and the patriarchs, but the book gives no date, and none should be invented for it. By the time of the exile his name was already known in Israel, for the prophet Ezekiel speaks of Noah, Daniel and Job as men of outstanding righteousness. Who wrote the book, and when, are unknown, and scholarly guesses range across many centuries.

The book begins with a question, and it is not quite the one people expect. In a scene in the court of heaven, the Lord points to Job as a man without equal on earth, blameless and upright, and the accuser, called Satan, replies: does Job fear God for nothing? Take away everything he has, and he will curse you to your face. What follows tests that claim. Job loses his wealth, his children and his health, and the rest of the book wrestles with what it means to suffer without explanation and how a person may rightly speak to God, and about God, in the dark.

The shape of the book matters for following it. It is framed by prose. The opening two chapters tell the story of Job's losses, moving between heaven and earth, and the last part of the final chapter tells how the story ends. Everything in between is poetry. The listener is let in on the scene in heaven, but Job and his friends never are, and that difference runs beneath every speech.

Three friends come to comfort Job: Eliphaz the Temanite, Bildad the Shuhite and Zophar the Naamathite. After a week of silence, Job breaks out in a lament, and a long debate follows. It moves in three rounds. In each, the friends speak one after another, and Job answers each of them in turn. The first round runs from chapter four to chapter fourteen, the second from fifteen to twenty-one, and the third from twenty-two to twenty-seven, though by then the pattern is breaking down: Bildad's last speech is very short, and Zophar does not speak a third time. A poem about the hiddenness of wisdom follows, and then Job makes a long closing defence, ending with a solemn oath that he is innocent. A younger man named Elihu, who has not been mentioned before, then speaks at length. Finally the Lord himself answers Job out of a whirlwind, in two great speeches, each followed by a brief reply from Job.

The friends' argument rests on a conviction found throughout the ancient world, and in parts of Scripture too: that God rewards the righteous and punishes the wicked. From that they reason backwards. If Job is suffering so terribly, he must have sinned terribly, and his only hope is to repent. Job, who knows he has not done what they imply, refuses to confess to it. Instead he protests, accuses, pleads and demands a hearing. The language of the law court runs all through his speeches: he wants to state his case, he longs for a witness, an advocate, someone to stand between him and God. As the rounds go on, the friends grow harsher and Job grows more desperate, though moments of startling hope break through. At the end, the Lord tells the friends that they have not spoken of him rightly, as his servant Job has, a verdict that has given readers much to ponder.

The poetry of Job is some of the finest, and most difficult, in the Old Testament. Its Hebrew contains many rare words, and translators are uncertain about some lines. Its imagery is drawn from the whole of creation: stars and storms, rivers and deserts, lions, ostriches and wild donkeys, mining and weaving. One small detail is worth listening for. In the prose frame and in the Lord's speeches the narrator uses God's covenant name, the Lord. In the debate the speakers mostly say God or the Almighty.

In English Bibles, Job stands at the head of the poetic books, followed by Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes and the Song of Songs. Its position after Esther is not chronological; it simply begins a new section of the Bible. Much later, the letter of James will point to Job as an example of endurance, and of the Lord's compassion and mercy toward him.

=== 1
The book opens on earth, with a portrait of one man. Job lives in the land of Uz. He is blameless and upright, he fears God and turns away from evil, and he is the greatest man among all the people of the East. His wealth is counted the way wealth was counted in that world, in sheep, camels, oxen, donkeys and servants. He has seven sons and three daughters, and so careful is his devotion that after each round of his children's feasts he offers sacrifices for them, in case they have cursed God in their hearts.

Then the scene moves to heaven. The sons of God, the members of the heavenly court, come to present themselves before the Lord, and among them comes Satan, a name that means the accuser or the adversary. The Lord draws his attention to Job, and the accuser answers with the question that drives the whole book: does Job fear God for nothing?

The Lord allows Job to be tested, within a limit. Then we are back on earth, on a day like any other, as one messenger after another arrives at Job's door. The chapter ends with Job's response.

=== 2
Job has lost his flocks, his servants and all ten of his children, and he has not cursed God. Now the scene returns to the court of heaven, in words that closely echo the first. The Lord points again to Job, who has held fast to his integrity, though, the Lord says, he was ruined without cause.

The accuser presses further. A man will give up everything to save his own skin, he says; touch his body, and he will curse you. Again the Lord allows it, again with a limit: Job's life must be spared. Job is struck with painful sores from head to foot, and he sits among the ashes, scraping himself with a piece of broken pottery. His wife can bear it no longer, and she speaks the words the accuser had predicted. Job's answer is brief.

At the end of the chapter, three friends arrive from their homes to comfort him: Eliphaz from Teman, a place in Edom known for its wise men, Bildad the Shuhite and Zophar the Naamathite. They barely recognise him. For seven days and nights they sit with him on the ground in silence. Here the heavenly scenes end. Neither Job nor his friends will ever learn of them.

=== 3
Seven days of silence have passed, and Job is the first to speak. With his words the book changes from prose into poetry, and it will remain poetry until the closing scene.

This is not the patient Job of the first two chapters. He does not curse God, but he curses the day he was born and the night he was conceived, calling on darkness to blot them from the calendar. The prophet Jeremiah would later curse his own birth in very similar terms. Job asks why he did not die at birth, and he imagines the grave as a place of rest, where kings and slaves lie side by side, the weary are at peace and the captive no longer hears the voice of the taskmaster.

The lament ends with questions without answers. Why is light given to those in misery, who long for a death that does not come? The thing he feared has come upon him, and he has no rest. It is these words that his friends now feel they must answer.

=== 4
Job's lament has been heard, and now the first round of the debate begins. Eliphaz the Temanite speaks first, probably as the eldest of the friends.

He begins gently. Job has steadied and strengthened many others in their troubles; now trouble has come to him, and he is dismayed. Should not his reverence for God be his confidence? Then Eliphaz states the conviction on which all three friends will build: no innocent person has ever perished, and those who plough evil reap what they sow.

To support this he describes a vision that came to him in the night. A spirit glided past his face, his hair stood on end, and a voice whispered that no mortal can be righteous before God, since even the angels are not pure in his sight. Eliphaz's speech continues into the next chapter.

=== 5
Eliphaz is still speaking. He has told Job of his night vision, and now he draws out what he thinks it means. No one in heaven will take Job's side; resentment destroys the fool, and humans are born to trouble as surely as sparks fly upward.

Then he turns to advice. If I were you, he says, I would seek God and lay my cause before him, and he describes God's power to raise the lowly and frustrate the schemes of the cunning. The heart of his counsel is that suffering is God's discipline: blessed is the one God corrects, for he wounds, but he also binds up.

The speech ends with a beautiful picture of the life Job could still enjoy, secure, fruitful and long, coming to the grave like a sheaf gathered at harvest. It is meant kindly. But it assumes that Job needs correcting, and Eliphaz closes by assuring him that this is true and that Job should take it to heart.

=== 6
Job now answers Eliphaz, and his reply runs across two chapters. He begins by defending his words. If his grief could be weighed, it would be heavier than the sand of the sea; no wonder he has spoken rashly. The arrows of the Almighty are in him. His one remaining wish is that God would let him die, while he can still say he has not denied the words of the Holy One.

Then he turns to his friends. He had hoped for loyalty from them, but they have proved like the streams of the desert, which flow in spring and vanish in the summer heat, when caravans from Tema and Sheba come looking for water and find nothing. He has asked them for nothing but understanding. Show me where I have gone wrong, he says, and I will be silent. Look at me; would I lie to your face?

=== 7
Job is still answering Eliphaz, but partway through this chapter he stops addressing his friends and speaks to God directly. It is the first time he does so.

He describes his life as hard labour, like a hired worker longing for evening. His nights are long and sleepless, his skin is cracked and festering, and his days pass swifter than a weaver's shuttle, coming to an end without hope. Because he has so little time left, he will not hold his tongue.

The most striking moment comes when Job asks: what is man, that you make so much of him, that you examine him every morning? The words echo a psalm that marvels at God's care for humanity, but here they are turned on their head. Job feels watched, not cherished, and he begs God to look away and leave him alone, if only long enough to swallow.

=== 8
Bildad the Shuhite now speaks for the first time. He is blunter than Eliphaz, and he has been stung by Job's words, which he calls a blustering wind.

His first question sets out his position: does God pervert justice? Then he says something that must have cut Job deeply. If your children sinned, he says, God handed them over to the consequences. Bildad does not claim to know that they did, but the implication is plain. If Job himself is pure and seeks God, Bildad promises, God will restore him.

Where Eliphaz appealed to a vision, Bildad appeals to tradition, to the wisdom of earlier generations, since we were born only yesterday. He illustrates it with pictures from nature: papyrus that withers without its marsh, a spider's web that gives way when leaned on. The speech closes with a promise that God will yet fill Job's mouth with laughter.

=== 9
Job answers Bildad. He does not dispute that God is just. His question is a different one: how can a mortal be in the right before God? From here the language of the law court comes to the fore, and it will run through the rest of Job's speeches. He imagines taking God to court, and sees at once that it is hopeless.

God is wise and mighty beyond resistance. He moves mountains, shakes the earth, commands the sun and made the constellations of the Bear, Orion and the Pleiades. Who could summon such a judge, or answer him even once in a thousand? Job is now saying openly what he feels: that God destroys the blameless and the wicked alike.

Near the end comes a longing that will return in new forms in later chapters. If only there were someone to arbitrate between them, to lay a hand on both God and Job. But there is no one, and Job feels utterly alone.

=== 10
Job is still speaking, and he now addresses God again, voicing the complaint he said he could not hold back. Do not condemn me, he asks; tell me what charges you have against me.

The heart of the chapter is an appeal to God as Job's maker. Job recalls how God formed him in the womb, as a potter shapes clay, clothing him with skin and flesh and knitting him together with bones and sinews, and granting him life and steadfast love. Why then would God destroy the work of his own hands? Job begins to suspect that all along God was watching for a reason to condemn him.

The chapter ends where Job's first lament ended, with the wish that he had never been born, and a plea for a little comfort before he goes to the land of darkness from which there is no return.

=== 11
Zophar the Naamathite now speaks for the first time, completing the first round of the friends' speeches. He is the harshest of the three so far.

He accuses Job of babbling and mockery, and of claiming to be pure in God's sight. If only God would speak, Zophar says, and show Job the secrets of wisdom, he would learn that God has punished him less than his sin deserves.

Zophar's speech then turns to the mystery of God, whose depths are higher than the heavens and deeper than the grave, and who sees wickedness that humans cannot. Like the others, he ends with a call to repent and a bright picture of what will follow, Job's darkness turning to morning. His last word, though, is a warning about the fate of the wicked.

=== 12
All three friends have spoken, and Job now gives his longest answer of the first round, filling this chapter and the next two.

He opens with sarcasm: no doubt you are the people, and wisdom will die with you. He insists that he understands as well as they do, and protests that he, a righteous man who once called on God and was answered, has become a laughingstock.

Then Job takes up the friends' favourite subject, the power and wisdom of God, and turns it against them. Even the animals and birds know that the Lord's hand has done this, he says, the only place in the debate where God's covenant name is used. What follows sounds at first like a hymn, but it is a dark one. God tears down what cannot be rebuilt. He strips counsellors, judges, kings and priests of their wisdom and power, and he makes nations great and then destroys them. In Job's eyes this is power without any order that he can see.

=== 13
Job is still answering his friends, and his tone sharpens. He has heard their arguments, and he knows as much as they do. What he wants is to speak to the Almighty himself and argue his case before God.

He accuses the friends of whitewashing the truth with lies. They are worthless physicians, and their wisest course would be silence. Would they speak falsely on God's behalf, and do they think God will thank them for it? Their maxims, he says, are proverbs of ashes.

Then Job resolves to risk everything and take his life in his hands. The chapter contains one of the best-known lines in the book, traditionally rendered, though he slay me, yet will I hope in him; the Hebrew can be read in more than one way. Job sets out his case like a man preparing for trial, asks God to name his sins, and then turns once more to address God directly.

=== 14
Job's long reply, and with it the first round of the debate, comes to a close in this chapter. It is a meditation on human frailty, spoken to God.

A human being, born of woman, has few days and is full of trouble. He blossoms like a flower and withers; he passes like a shadow. Job sets this against an image of hope from the natural world. A tree that is cut down may sprout again at the scent of water, but a man lies down and does not rise.

Then, for a moment, a different thought breaks in. If only God would hide him in the grave until his anger had passed, and then remember him. If a man dies, Job asks, will he live again? He imagines God calling and Job answering. But the hope fades as quickly as it came. As water wears away the stones, Job says, so God destroys the hope of man.
