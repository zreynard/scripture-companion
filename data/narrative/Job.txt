=== intro
With the book of Job we leave behind the Persian court and the rebuilt walls of Jerusalem, and step into a different kind of book altogether. Job is the first of the Bible's books of poetry and wisdom, and its story stands apart from the history of Israel. It opens like an old tale: there was a man in the land of Uz whose name was Job.

Uz lay east of the Jordan, toward Edom and the desert fringes of Arabia, among the people the book calls the people of the East. Job's world is one of great flocks and herds, of raiding bands from the desert, of a father who rises early to offer sacrifices for his children. Moses, the Law, the temple and the kings of Israel are never mentioned, and Job himself is not an Israelite. His world resembles that of Abraham and the patriarchs, though the book itself gives no date and does not name its author. By the time of the exile his name was already known in Israel, for the prophet Ezekiel speaks of Noah, Daniel and Job as men of outstanding righteousness.

The book begins with a question, and it is not quite the one people expect. In a scene in the court of heaven, the Lord points to Job as a man without equal on earth, blameless and upright, and the accuser, called Satan, replies: does Job fear God for nothing? Take away everything he has, and he will curse you to your face. What follows tests that claim. Job loses his wealth, his children and his health, and the rest of the book wrestles with what it means to suffer without explanation and how a person may rightly speak to God, and about God, in the dark.

The shape of the book matters for following it. It is framed by prose. The opening two chapters tell the story of Job's losses, moving between heaven and earth, and the last part of the final chapter tells how the story ends. Everything in between is poetry. The listener is let in on the scene in heaven, but Job and his friends never are, and that difference runs beneath every speech.

Three friends come to comfort Job: Eliphaz the Temanite, Bildad the Shuhite and Zophar the Naamathite. After a week of silence, Job breaks out in a lament, and a long debate follows. It moves in three rounds. In each, the friends speak one after another, and Job answers each of them in turn. The first round runs from chapter four to chapter fourteen, the second from fifteen to twenty-one, and the third from twenty-two to twenty-seven, though by then the pattern is breaking down: Bildad's last speech is very short, and Zophar does not speak a third time. A poem about the hiddenness of wisdom follows, and then Job makes a long closing defence, ending with a solemn oath that he is innocent. A younger man named Elihu, who has not been mentioned before, then speaks at length. Finally the Lord himself answers Job out of a whirlwind, in two great speeches, each followed by a brief reply from Job.

The friends' argument rests on a conviction found throughout the ancient world, and in parts of Scripture too: that God rewards the righteous and punishes the wicked. From that they reason backwards. If Job is suffering so terribly, he must have sinned terribly, and his only hope is to repent. Job, who knows he has not done what they imply, refuses to confess to it. Instead he protests, accuses, pleads and demands a hearing. The language of the law court runs all through his speeches: he wants to state his case, he longs for a witness, an advocate, someone to stand between him and God. As the rounds go on, the friends grow harsher and Job grows more desperate, though moments of startling hope break through. At the end, the Lord tells the friends that they have not spoken of him rightly, as his servant Job has, and that Job will pray for them.

The poetry of Job is some of the finest, and most difficult, in the Old Testament. Its Hebrew contains many rare words, some found nowhere else in the Bible. Its imagery is drawn from the whole of creation: stars and storms, rivers and deserts, lions, ostriches and wild donkeys, mining and weaving. One small detail is worth listening for. In the prose frame, and in the lines that introduce the Lord's speeches, the narrator uses God's covenant name, the Lord. In the debate the speakers mostly say God or the Almighty.

In English Bibles, Job is followed by the other poetic books: Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes and the Song of Songs. Its position after Esther is not chronological; it simply begins a new section of the Bible. Much later, the letter of James will point to Job as an example of endurance, and of the Lord's compassion and mercy toward him.

=== 1
The book opens on earth, with a portrait of one man. Job lives in the land of Uz. He is blameless and upright, he fears God and turns away from evil, and he is the greatest man among all the people of the East. His wealth is counted the way wealth was counted in that world, in sheep, camels, oxen, donkeys and servants. He has seven sons and three daughters, and so careful is his devotion that after each round of his children's feasts he offers sacrifices for them, in case they have cursed God in their hearts.

Then the scene moves to heaven. The sons of God, the members of the heavenly court, come to present themselves before the Lord, and among them comes Satan, a name that means the accuser or the adversary. The Lord draws his attention to Job, and the accuser answers with the question that drives the whole book: does Job fear God for nothing?

The Lord allows Job to be tested, within a limit. Then we are back on earth, on a day like any other, as one messenger after another arrives at Job's door. The chapter ends with Job's response.

=== 2
Job has lost his flocks, his servants and all ten of his children, and he has not cursed God. Now the scene returns to the court of heaven, in words that closely echo the first. The Lord points again to Job, who has held fast to his integrity, though, the Lord says, he was ruined without cause.

The accuser presses further. A man will give up everything to save his own skin, he says; touch his body, and he will curse you. Again the Lord allows it, again with a limit: Job's life must be spared. Job is struck with painful sores from head to foot, and he sits among the ashes, scraping himself with a piece of broken pottery. Then his wife urges him to curse God and die, the very thing the accuser had predicted. Job's answer is brief.

At the end of the chapter, three friends arrive from their homes to comfort him: Eliphaz from Teman, a place in Edom known for its wise men, Bildad the Shuhite and Zophar the Naamathite. They barely recognise him. For seven days and nights they sit with him on the ground in silence. Here the heavenly scenes end. Neither Job nor his friends is ever told of them.

=== 3
Seven days of silence have passed, and Job is the first to speak. With his words the book changes from prose into poetry, and it will remain poetry until the closing scene.

This is not the patient Job of the first two chapters. He does not curse God, but he curses the day he was born and the night he was conceived, calling on darkness to blot them from the calendar. The prophet Jeremiah would later curse his own birth in very similar terms. Job asks why he did not die at birth, and he imagines the grave as a place of rest, where kings and slaves lie side by side, the weary are at peace and the captive no longer hears the voice of the taskmaster.

The lament ends with questions without answers. Why is light given to those in misery, who long for a death that does not come? The thing he feared has come upon him, and he has no rest. It is these words that his friends now feel they must answer.

=== 4
Job's lament has been heard, and now the first round of the debate begins. Eliphaz the Temanite speaks first.

He begins gently. Job has steadied and strengthened many others in their troubles; now trouble has come to him, and he is dismayed. Should not his reverence for God be his confidence? Then Eliphaz states the conviction on which all three friends will build: no innocent person has ever perished, and those who plough evil reap what they sow.

To support this he describes a vision that came to him in the night. A spirit glided past his face, his hair stood on end, and a voice whispered that no mortal can be righteous before God, since even the angels are not pure in his sight. Eliphaz's speech continues into the next chapter.

=== 5
Eliphaz is still speaking. He has told Job of his night vision, and now he draws out what he thinks it means. No one in heaven will take Job's side; resentment destroys the fool, and humans are born to trouble as surely as sparks fly upward.

Then comes advice. If I were you, he says, I would seek God and lay my cause before him, and he describes God's power to raise the lowly and frustrate the schemes of the cunning. At the centre of his counsel is the idea that suffering is God's discipline: blessed is the one God corrects, for he wounds, but he also binds up.

The speech ends with a beautiful picture of the life Job could still enjoy, secure, fruitful and long, coming to the grave like a sheaf gathered at harvest. It is meant kindly. But it assumes that Job needs correcting, and Eliphaz closes by assuring him that this is true and that Job should take it to heart.

=== 6
Job now answers Eliphaz, and his reply runs across two chapters. He begins by defending his words. If his grief could be weighed, it would be heavier than the sand of the sea; no wonder he has spoken rashly. The arrows of the Almighty are in him. His one remaining wish is that God would let him die, while he can still say he has not denied the words of the Holy One.

Then he turns to his friends. He had hoped for loyalty from them, but they have proved like the streams of the desert, which flow in spring and vanish in the summer heat, when caravans from Tema and Sheba come looking for water and find nothing. He has asked them for nothing but understanding. Show me where I have gone wrong, he says, and I will be silent. Look at me; would I lie to your face?

=== 7
Job is still answering Eliphaz, but partway through this chapter he stops addressing his friends and speaks to God directly. It is the first time he does so.

He describes his life as hard labour, like a hired worker longing for evening. His nights are long and sleepless, his skin is cracked and festering, and his days pass swifter than a weaver's shuttle, coming to an end without hope. Because he has so little time left, he will not hold his tongue.

The most striking moment comes when Job asks: what is man, that you make so much of him, that you examine him every morning? The words echo the eighth psalm, which marvels at God's care for humanity, but here they are turned on their head. Job feels watched, not cherished, and he begs God to look away and leave him alone, if only long enough to swallow.

=== 8
Bildad the Shuhite now speaks for the first time. He is blunter than Eliphaz. How long will Job go on like this, he asks; his words are a blustering wind.

His first question sets out his position: does God pervert justice? Then he turns to Job's dead children. When your children sinned against him, he says, God gave them over to their rebellion. If Job himself is pure and seeks God, Bildad promises, God will restore him.

Where Eliphaz appealed to a vision, Bildad appeals to tradition, to the wisdom of earlier generations, since we were born only yesterday. He illustrates it with pictures from nature: papyrus that withers without its marsh, a spider's web that gives way when leaned on. The speech closes with a promise that God will yet fill Job's mouth with laughter.

=== 9
Job answers Bildad. Yes, he says, I know that it is so. His question is a different one: how can a mortal be in the right before God? From here the language of the law court comes to the fore, and it will run through the rest of Job's speeches. He imagines taking God to court, and sees at once that it is hopeless.

God is wise and mighty beyond resistance. He moves mountains, shakes the earth, commands the sun and made the constellations of the Bear, Orion and the Pleiades. Who could summon such a judge, or answer him even once in a thousand? Job is now saying openly what he feels: that God destroys the blameless and the wicked alike.

Near the end comes a longing that will return in new forms in later chapters. If only there were someone to arbitrate between them, to lay a hand on both God and Job. But there is no one, and Job feels utterly alone.

=== 10
Job is still speaking, and he now addresses God again, voicing the complaint he said he could not hold back. Do not condemn me, he asks; tell me what charges you have against me.

Most of the chapter is an appeal to God as Job's maker. Job recalls how God formed him in the womb, as a potter shapes clay, clothing him with skin and flesh and knitting him together with bones and sinews, and granting him life and loving devotion. Why then would God destroy the work of his own hands? Job begins to suspect that all along God was watching for a reason to condemn him.

The chapter ends where Job's first lament ended, with the wish that he had never been born, and a plea for a little comfort before he goes to the land of darkness from which there is no return.

=== 11
Zophar the Naamathite now speaks for the first time, completing the first round of the friends' speeches. He is the harshest of the three so far.

He accuses Job of babbling and mockery, and of claiming to be pure in God's sight. If only God would speak, Zophar says, and show Job the secrets of wisdom, he would learn that God has punished him less than his sin deserves.

Zophar then speaks of the mystery of God, whose depths are higher than the heavens and deeper than the grave, and who sees wickedness that humans cannot. Like the others, he ends with a call to repent and a bright picture of what will follow, Job's darkness turning to morning. His last word, though, is a warning about the fate of the wicked.

=== 12
All three friends have spoken, and Job now gives his longest answer of the first round, filling this chapter and the next two.

He opens with sarcasm: no doubt you are the people, and wisdom will die with you. He insists that he understands as well as they do, and protests that he, a righteous man who once called on God and was answered, has become a laughingstock.

Then Job takes up the friends' favourite subject, the power and wisdom of God, and turns it against them. Even the animals and birds know that the Lord's hand has done this, he says, the only place in the debate where God's covenant name is used. What follows sounds at first like a hymn, but it is a dark one. God tears down what cannot be rebuilt. He strips counsellors, judges, kings and priests of their wisdom and power, and he makes nations great and then destroys them. In Job's eyes this is power without any order that he can see.

=== 13
Job is still answering his friends, and his tone sharpens. He has heard their arguments, and he knows as much as they do. What he wants is to speak to the Almighty himself and argue his case before God.

He accuses the friends of whitewashing the truth with lies. They are worthless physicians, and their wisest course would be silence. Would they speak falsely on God's behalf, and do they think God will thank them for it? Their maxims, he says, are proverbs of ashes.

Then Job resolves to risk everything and take his life in his hands. The chapter contains a line many readers know by heart: though he slay me, I will hope in him. Job sets out his case like a man preparing for trial, and then turns once more to address God directly, asking him to name his sins.

=== 14
Job's long reply, and with it the first round of the debate, comes to a close in this chapter. It is a meditation on human frailty, spoken to God.

A human being, born of woman, has few days and is full of trouble. He blossoms like a flower and withers; he passes like a shadow. Job sets this against an image of hope from the natural world. A tree that is cut down may sprout again at the scent of water, but a man lies down and does not rise.

Then, for a moment, a different thought breaks in. If only God would hide him in the grave until his anger had passed, and then remember him. If a man dies, Job asks, will he live again? He imagines God calling and Job answering. But the hope fades as quickly as it came. As water wears away the stones, Job says, so God destroys the hope of man.

=== 15
The second round of the debate begins, and again Eliphaz speaks first. The gentleness of his first speech has gone. Job's words, he says, are a hot east wind, and worse, they undermine reverence for God. Job's own mouth condemns him.

Eliphaz will not allow that Job knows anything his friends do not. Was Job the first man ever born? Has he listened in on the council of God? The grey-haired and the aged are on our side, Eliphaz says, men older than your father. He repeats the thought of his night vision, that no human being can be pure before God.

Most of the speech is a portrait of the wicked man: terror in his ears, darkness waiting for him, his wealth melting away and his branches withering. Eliphaz does not say that this is Job, but he hardly needs to. The fate of the wicked will be the friends' great theme throughout this second round.

=== 16
Job answers Eliphaz, and his reply runs on into the next chapter. He has heard all this before. Miserable comforters, he calls his friends. If their places were reversed, he says, he could heap up words as they do, but he would try to bring them relief.

Then he describes what God has done to him in some of the most violent images in the book. God has torn him in anger and gnashed his teeth at him; he has seized him by the neck and set him up as a target for his archers; he rushes at him like a warrior. Job sits in sackcloth with his face red from weeping, though his hands are free of violence.

He calls on the earth not to cover his blood, so that his cry may go on being heard. And then comes a startling turn. Even now, Job says, my witness is in heaven, and my advocate is on high. Who this witness is, Job does not say.

=== 17
Job is still speaking. The hope of the last chapter, a witness in heaven, has flickered, and now his mood sinks again. His spirit is broken, his days are almost over, and the grave is waiting.

He asks God to provide a pledge on his behalf, a kind of bail, since no one else will stand surety for him. He has become a byword among the people, someone they spit at, and his body is wasting to a shadow. As for his friends, he says, he will not find a wise man among them.

The chapter ends in the deepest darkness Job has yet reached. If the grave is his home, and corruption and the worm are all the family he has left, then where is his hope?

=== 18
Bildad the Shuhite speaks for the second time, and he is irritated. Why does Job treat his friends like cattle? Should the whole earth be rearranged for his sake?

The rest of his speech is a portrait of the fate of the wicked, sharper and more detailed than Eliphaz's. The lamp of the wicked goes out, his own schemes trip him, and traps and snares lie hidden in his path. Terrors chase him at every step. Disease devours his skin, he is torn from his tent and marched off to the king of terrors, and he leaves no children and no name behind him.

Bildad never names Job in this portrait. But Job has lost his home, his children and his health, and his skin is wasting away, and every line seems chosen to fit.

=== 19
Job answers Bildad. How long will you torment me, he asks, and crush me with words? If he has gone astray, that is his own concern. What his friends refuse to see is that it is God who has wronged him.

Job describes God as an army besieging a single tent. Then he describes the people who have abandoned him: his brothers, his relatives, his guests, his servants, his own wife, and even the little boys who mock him in the street. Have pity on me, my friends, he pleads, for the hand of God has struck me.

Then Job wishes that his words could be cut into rock forever. What follows has become one of the best-known passages in the Old Testament: I know that my Redeemer lives. The word for redeemer belongs to Israel's family law. It means the close relative who stands up for a kinsman in trouble, buys him back from debt or avenges his blood. Job is sure that this Redeemer will stand on the earth at the last, and that even after his skin has been destroyed, Job himself will see God with his own eyes.

=== 20
Zophar the Naamathite speaks for the second time, and he begins by admitting how agitated he is. He has heard Job's rebuke as an insult, and he cannot keep silent.

He does not respond to Job's great cry of hope. Instead he returns to the theme of the whole second round, the fate of the wicked, and paints it in coarse and vivid colours. The triumph of the wicked is brief. Though he towers to the clouds, he perishes like his own dung. He swallows riches and must vomit them up; the evil that was sweet in his mouth turns to the poison of snakes in his stomach. God's anger rains down on him as he eats, and an arrow pierces him from behind.

Along the way Zophar suggests that such a man has crushed the poor and seized houses he did not build. He does not say it of Job directly, but the charge is beginning to take shape.

=== 21
Job's reply closes the second round, and for the first time he takes on the friends' central claim directly. They have each insisted that the wicked come to a swift and terrible end. Job asks them to look at the world as it is.

Why do the wicked live on, he asks, growing old and powerful? Their homes are safe, their herds breed, their children dance to the tambourine and the flute, and they go down to the grave in peace, though they have told God to leave them alone. How often, Job asks, is the lamp of the wicked actually put out?

He brushes aside the idea that God punishes their children instead; let the wicked man feel it himself. One man dies in prosperity and another in bitterness, and the worms cover them both. Ask any traveller, Job says. The chapter ends with his verdict on everything the friends have said: their answers are nothing but falsehood.

=== 22
The third round of the debate begins, and Eliphaz speaks for the last time. He has moved a long way from his opening courtesy. Now he openly accuses Job of great wickedness.

He even names the sins: taking pledges from his brothers for no reason, stripping the poor of their clothing, refusing water to the weary and bread to the hungry, sending widows away empty-handed. He offers no evidence. Eliphaz has reasoned that suffering as great as Job's must have a cause as great, and so he supplies one. He also accuses Job of imagining that God cannot see through the clouds.

The speech ends, as Eliphaz's speeches do, with an appeal: make peace with God, return to the Almighty, and he will hear your prayers. His last words speak of God delivering even someone who is not innocent through the clean hands of another. By the end of the book it is Eliphaz himself who will need Job's prayers.

=== 23
Job answers, but he does not take up Eliphaz's charges. His thoughts are fixed on God. Even today his complaint is bitter, and the longing he voices here is the heart of all his speeches: if only I knew where to find him, so that I could come to his seat and lay my case before him.

Job is now confident that if he could reach God, he would be heard and acquitted. But he cannot find him. He goes east and west, north and south, and God is not there. Yet God knows the way Job has taken, and Job trusts that when he has been tested, he will come out like gold.

That confidence sits side by side with dread. God does whatever he pleases, and Job is terrified in his presence. His speech continues into the next chapter.

=== 24
Job is still speaking, and now he widens his complaint beyond his own suffering to the injustice of the world. Why does the Almighty not set times for judgement, so that those who know him can see it?

He gives a bleak survey of the poor and the people who prey on them. Boundary stones are moved and flocks stolen. The orphan's donkey and the widow's ox are taken. The poor forage like wild donkeys in the wasteland, sleep naked in the mountain rain, and carry the harvest while going hungry. A fatherless baby is snatched from the breast for debt. The dying groan in the city, and God charges no one with wrong. Then come those who love the darkness: the murderer, the adulterer and the burglar.

The final verses sound more like the friends, describing the wicked being swept away. He ends with a challenge: who can prove me wrong?

=== 25
Bildad speaks for the third and final time, and his speech is only six verses long, the shortest the friends make.

He offers no new argument. Dominion and awe belong to God, whose armies are past counting and whose light rises on everyone. How then can any human being be righteous before him? If even the moon and stars are not pure in his sight, how much less a man, who is a maggot, a worm.

It is the same point Eliphaz made in his night vision at the start of the debate. The friends have nothing new left to say, and from here the pattern of the debate begins to break down.

=== 26
Job answers Bildad, beginning with heavy sarcasm. How you have helped the powerless, he says. What wise counsel you have given to one without wisdom. Whose spirit was speaking through you?

Then Job takes up the theme of God's greatness himself and does it better than Bildad. He describes God's power reaching down into the realm of the dead, which lies open before him, and out over creation. God stretches the northern sky over empty space and hangs the earth upon nothing. He binds up the waters in his clouds and draws the horizon on the face of the sea. By his power he shattered Rahab and pierced the fleeing serpent, names for the forces of chaos that ancient poetry pictured as monsters of the deep.

Then comes a famous line: these are only the fringes of his ways, a faint whisper of him. Who then could understand the thunder of his power?

=== 27
A new heading marks a fresh speech: Job continued his discourse. Zophar, whose turn it would be, never speaks a third time.

Job begins with a solemn oath. As surely as God lives, the God who has denied him justice, Job will not speak falsely. He will never admit that his friends are right. He will hold on to his integrity, he says, until he dies.

The second half of the chapter describes the fate of the wicked, the heritage the ruthless receive from the Almighty, in terms very like those the friends have used: children destined for the sword, wealth heaped up and taken by others, a house as flimsy as a moth's cocoon, and an east wind sweeping the wicked man away.

=== 28
The debate has run its course, and now comes a poem unlike anything around it. No new speaker is introduced, and after all the arguing its mood is calm and reflective, like a long breath drawn before the final speeches.

It begins underground, with miners. People cut shafts far from where anyone lives, dangle on ropes in the darkness, overturn mountains at their roots and bring hidden treasures to light: silver, gold, iron, copper and sapphires. No falcon's eye has seen these paths, and no lion has walked them.

But where can wisdom be found? It is not in the land of the living or in the depths of the sea, and it cannot be bought with the gold of Ophir or the topaz of Cush. Only God knows the way to it. The poem ends with a single sentence that recalls the first description of Job in chapter one: the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom, and to turn from evil is understanding.

=== 29
The heading from chapter twenty-seven appears again, Job continued his discourse, and this time it opens his final defence, which fills three chapters. He has finished with his friends; from now on he speaks about his own life, and at times directly to God.

This chapter looks back. Job longs for the months gone by, when God watched over him, when the friendship of God was on his tent and his children were all around him. He remembers going out to the city gate, where the elders of a town sat to settle its affairs. The young men stepped aside, the old men rose to their feet, and princes fell silent when he spoke.

They honoured him, he says, because he rescued the poor and the fatherless and made the widow's heart sing. He was eyes to the blind and feet to the lame, a father to the needy. He expected to die in his own nest, full of days. The chapter is full of warmth and dignity, and it makes what comes next all the harder.

=== 30
The last chapter remembered Job's honoured past. This one begins with two words that mark the change: but now.

Now he is mocked by young men whose fathers he would not have put with the dogs of his flocks, outcasts who once scavenged in the wasteland. They make up songs about him, keep their distance and spit in his face. Because God has unstrung Job's bow, they have thrown off all restraint, and they pour in like an army through a breach in a wall.

Then Job turns inward, to his suffering body, and to God. Pain gnaws at his bones by night. God has thrown him into the mud. I cry out to you, Job says, and you do not answer. His skin is blackened and his bones burn with fever. He has become a brother to jackals and a companion of ostriches, and his harp is tuned to mourning.

=== 31
Job's final speech reaches its climax. In the ancient world a person accused of wrongdoing might clear his name by an oath, calling down curses on himself if he were guilty. That is what Job does here, at length.

He goes through the whole of his life. If I have looked with lust at a young woman, if I have walked in deceit or been enticed by my neighbour's wife, if I have refused justice to my servants, though the same God made us both in the womb, if I have ignored the poor, the widow or the orphan, trusted in gold, worshipped the sun and the moon, rejoiced at an enemy's ruin, or hidden my sin, then let the punishment fall. The standard he sets for himself reaches beyond actions into his thoughts and motives.

Near the end Job signs his name, so to speak, and demands that God answer him and put the charges against him in writing. Then a short closing line marks the end of his speeches.

=== 32
Job has finished speaking, and his three friends have stopped answering him, because Job is righteous in his own eyes. The debate has fallen silent. Then, unexpectedly, a new voice is heard.

Elihu son of Barachel the Buzite, of the family of Ram, has not been mentioned before. He is a young man, and he has kept quiet out of respect for his elders, listening to every word. Now he is burning with anger, at Job for justifying himself rather than God, and at the three friends for condemning Job without being able to answer him.

This chapter is Elihu's introduction, and it is all about why he must speak. Wisdom does not come only with age, he says, but from the breath of the Almighty within a person. He is so full of words that he feels like new wine about to burst its wineskin. Elihu will speak for six chapters, and no one interrupts him or replies.

=== 33
Elihu now faces Job and, unlike the three friends, addresses him by name. He presents himself as an equal, formed from clay just as Job was, so that Job need not be afraid of him.

He quotes Job's complaints back to him: I am pure and without sin, yet God counts me as his enemy. Elihu's reply is that God is greater than any man, and that Job is wrong to say God does not answer. God speaks in more than one way, he says, though people do not notice. He warns people in dreams and visions of the night, to turn them from pride. And he speaks through pain, as a person lies wasting on a sickbed, close to the grave.

Then Elihu describes a messenger, a mediator, one in a thousand, who speaks up for the sufferer and declares that a ransom has been found. It is close to what Job had longed for. Elihu's distinctive idea is that suffering may be God's warning and rescue, not only punishment for past sins.

=== 34
Elihu begins a second speech, this time addressed to the wise men listening, and his tone becomes harsher. He sets out to defend the justice of God against what Job has said.

He quotes Job again: I am righteous, but God has denied me justice. Far be it from God to do wrong, Elihu replies. God repays every person according to his deeds. No one appointed him to rule the earth, and if he withdrew his breath, all flesh would perish together. He shows no favour to princes over the poor, and there is no darkness deep enough for evildoers to hide from him.

By the end, Elihu sounds very like the three friends he criticised. Job, he says, speaks without knowledge, adds rebellion to his sin, and multiplies words against God, and he wishes that Job might be tested to the limit.

=== 35
Elihu's third speech is short. He takes up a complaint he attributes to Job, that there is no profit in being righteous.

Look up at the heavens, Elihu says, and see how high the clouds are above you. God is so far above humanity that a person's sin does him no harm and a person's righteousness gives him nothing. Good and evil affect other human beings.

Then he addresses the problem of unanswered cries. People groan under oppression, Elihu says, but they do not truly seek God their Maker, who gives songs in the night. Their cries go unanswered because of their pride. If God has not answered Job, the implication is, Job should look to himself. He ends by saying that Job speaks empty words without knowledge.

=== 36
Elihu begins his fourth and final speech, which continues to the end of the next chapter. Bear with me a little longer, he says; there is more to say on God's behalf. He makes a bold claim for himself: one who is perfect in knowledge is with you.

He returns to his theme that God is mighty yet despises no one. When people are bound in affliction, God shows them their wrongdoing and opens their ears to instruction; if they listen, they will end their days in prosperity. God rescues the afflicted through their affliction. Elihu warns Job not to turn to evil in his distress.

Then the speech moves into praise of God's greatness, and its imagery rises to the sky. God draws up drops of water and distils them into rain. He spreads out the clouds, thunders from his pavilion and scatters lightning around him. It is as though a storm is gathering as Elihu speaks.

=== 37
Elihu is still speaking, and the storm he has been describing now fills his words. His heart pounds at the sound of thunder, the voice of God rolling across the sky. God commands the snow and the downpour, and by his breath the ice is formed. The clouds swirl at his direction, for correction or for blessing.

Then Elihu speaks to Job one last time. Stand still, he says, and consider the wonders of God. Do you know how he sends the lightning, or how the clouds hang in the sky? Can you spread out the heavens, hard as a mirror of cast bronze?

His final words speak of golden splendour coming out of the north, and of the Almighty, exalted in power and beyond our reach. Then Elihu falls silent. Job does not answer him, and he is not mentioned again. Another voice is about to speak.

=== 38
At last the Lord answers Job. Throughout the debate Job has demanded a hearing, and now God speaks to him directly, out of the whirlwind. For the first time since the prologue, the narrator uses the Lord's covenant name.

But the Lord does not come to answer Job's questions. He comes to ask questions of his own. Who is this who darkens my counsel with words without knowledge? Brace yourself like a man, the Lord says; I will question you, and you will answer me.

What follows is a tour of creation. Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth, while the morning stars sang together? Who shut in the sea when it burst from the womb? The questions range over the dawn, the depths of the sea, the gates of death, the storehouses of snow and hail, rain falling on desert where no one lives, and the constellations in their courses. Some carry a gentle irony. At the end of the chapter, the questions turn to the animals.

=== 39
The Lord's first speech continues, and it now becomes a gallery of wild creatures. Most of them live wild and free, far beyond the reach of human hands.

Does Job know when the mountain goats give birth, or watch the deer as they bear their young? Who set the wild donkey free to roam the salt flats, scorning the noise of the city? Will the wild ox agree to pull Job's plough? The ostrich flaps her wings and leaves her eggs in the sand, careless of her young, yet she can outrun the horse and rider.

Then comes the war horse, one of the most vivid portraits in the Bible, trembling with eagerness and laughing at fear as the trumpet sounds. Finally the hawk and the eagle soar and nest on the heights, at no command of Job's. Throughout, the Lord makes no mention of Job's suffering or of any charges against him.

=== 40
The Lord's first speech ends with a challenge: will the one who finds fault with the Almighty argue with him? Let him answer. And Job, silent since the whirlwind began, at last replies.

His reply is brief. He is small; how can he answer? He puts his hand over his mouth. He has spoken once, even twice, and will say no more. The man who so longed to argue his case has nothing to add.

But the Lord speaks again out of the whirlwind, with a second speech. Its opening question goes to the heart of the debate: would you discredit my justice, and condemn me in order to justify yourself? If Job can clothe himself in majesty and bring down every proud and wicked person, then God will admit that Job can save himself.

Then the Lord points to a creature called Behemoth, a massive beast that feeds on grass like an ox, with bones like tubes of bronze. He lies hidden among the reeds of the marsh, and when the river rages, even when the Jordan surges to his mouth, he is unafraid. Only his Maker can approach him with a sword.

=== 41
The Lord's second speech reaches its climax with a single creature, Leviathan, who fills the whole chapter. Job had mentioned Leviathan in his first lament, and elsewhere in the Old Testament the name is used for a monster of the sea, a figure of chaos that only God can master. The portrait that follows goes far beyond any ordinary animal.

The chapter opens with questions. Can Job catch Leviathan with a fishhook, put a rope through his nose, make him a pet or sell him in the market? No one is fierce enough to rouse him. Then who, the Lord asks, could stand against me?

The rest is a description of overwhelming power: scales sealed tight, eyes like the dawn, fire and smoke pouring from his mouth, iron treated as straw, and a wake that turns the sea white. The speech ends without returning to Job at all. Leviathan, the last line says, is king over all the proud.

=== 42
The Lord has spoken twice, and now Job answers him for the last time. These are his final words in the book, and they are still poetry.

Job acknowledges that God can do all things and that no purpose of his can be thwarted. He quotes the Lord's own words back to him and admits that he spoke of things too wonderful for him to know. Then comes the line that sums up what has happened to him: my ears had heard of you, but now my eyes have seen you. He ends by saying that he despises himself and repents in dust and ashes.

Then the book returns to prose for its ending. The Lord speaks to Eliphaz and his two friends, and his verdict on them is not what they would have expected. When Job has prayed for his friends, the Lord restores his fortunes twice over and gives him seven sons and three daughters. The narrator lingers over his three daughters, names them, and records that their father gave them an inheritance alongside their brothers. Job's story ends with words used in Genesis of the patriarch Isaac: he died, old and full of years.
