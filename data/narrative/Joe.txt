=== intro
Of the prophet Joel we know only his name and his father's. He is Joel son of Pethuel, and his name means the Lord is God. He tells us nothing about where he lived or when, and no king is named anywhere in his book. What he does tell us, in vivid detail, is what has just happened to the land.

A plague of locusts has swept over Judah. In the ancient Near East this was one of the most feared of all disasters. A swarm could darken the sky and strip a landscape bare in a few hours, and wave after wave of the insects could follow. Joel describes fields stripped, vines and fig trees splintered and white, seed shrivelled in the ground, cattle wandering without pasture, and fire and drought finishing what the locusts began. For a people who lived by grain, wine and oil, this was ruin. Even the daily offerings at the temple have stopped, because there is nothing left to offer.

Joel's world centres on Jerusalem, its temple and its priests. Priests and elders lead the people, and no king appears. The book speaks of God's people scattered among the nations and their land divided, and of Judeans sold as slaves to the Greeks, and it looks out on the nations round about: Tyre and Sidon, Philistia, Egypt and Edom. It stands second among the Twelve, and it shares striking lines with Amos, who follows it.

What gives Joel its power is the way he reads the disaster. To him the locusts are not simply bad luck. They are a warning of something greater, the Day of the Lord, a phrase several prophets use for the day when the Lord would act openly and decisively in judgement. That day, Joel says, is near. His response is to call the whole community to the temple: priests and elders, the old and the children, even a bride and bridegroom on their wedding day. Everyone is to fast, to weep, and to return to the Lord.

The book moves in a clear arc. It begins in lament, with the land in mourning. It sounds an alarm, as an army advances on Jerusalem, the Lord's own army, described in the colours of the locust swarm. It calls the people to turn back with all their hearts. Then the Lord answers, and the mood changes completely. He promises to restore what the locusts have eaten, and beyond that he looks to a wider future: his Spirit poured out on all kinds of people, the nations gathered for judgement, and Jerusalem secure, with water flowing from the house of the Lord.

That promise of the Spirit would be quoted by the apostle Peter on the day of Pentecost, as the first sermon of the church is preached in Jerusalem. In the Hebrew Bible the book is divided into four chapters; English Bibles, like the one read here, have three.

=== 1
The book opens with an appeal to the oldest people in the land. Has anything like this happened in your lifetime, Joel asks the elders, or in your fathers' days? Tell your children about it. Whatever he is describing, it is something no one living has ever seen.

Then he names it. Four words for locusts follow one another, each devouring what the last one left. The swarm is likened to a nation invading the land, with teeth like a lion's.

The chapter then moves through the stricken community. The drunkards have lost their wine. The farmers and vinedressers grieve over ruined crops. The priests mourn because the grain and drink offerings have stopped. Joel calls them all to sackcloth, to a fast and a solemn assembly at the house of the Lord. And for the first time he names what this calamity foreshadows: the Day of the Lord is near. The chapter ends with Joel's own prayer, as fire and drought sweep the pastures.

=== 2
The ram's horn sounds on Zion, the temple mount in Jerusalem. The Day of the Lord is coming, a day of darkness and gloom, and an army appears on the mountains like the dawn spreading across them. It runs like horses, scales the walls and climbs in through the windows, and at its head stands the Lord himself. Readers have long asked whether Joel is still describing the swarm or a human invasion it foreshadows.

Here the book turns. Even now, the Lord says, return to me with all your heart. Joel calls the people to rend their hearts and not their garments, and he recalls the words God had spoken to Moses, that he is gracious and compassionate, slow to anger and abounding in loving devotion. Everyone is to gather, and the priests are to weep between the portico and the altar.

The Lord answers, and the rest of the chapter is promise: rain and harvest, the years the locusts have eaten restored, and afterwards his Spirit poured out on all people, young and old, even servants, men and women alike.

=== 3
The horizon now widens beyond Judah's fields to the nations. When the Lord restores the fortunes of Judah and Jerusalem, he will gather the nations into the Valley of Jehoshaphat, a name meaning the Lord judges. Its location is unknown, and it may be less a place on the map than a name for the scene of judgement.

The nations have scattered God's people and divided up his land. Tyre, Sidon and the Philistine cities are accused of plundering his treasures and selling Judeans as slaves to the Greeks, far from home. A summons to war follows, reversing a famous prophecy of Isaiah and Micah: beat your ploughshares into swords. Multitudes gather in the valley of decision, and the harvest is ripe.

The book closes in Zion. The Lord roars from Jerusalem, words Amos also uses, but he is a refuge for his people. The mountains drip with sweet wine, and a spring flows from the house of the Lord as in the visions of Ezekiel and Zechariah. The famine of the opening has become abundance.
