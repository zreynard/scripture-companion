=== intro
Second Timothy is a letter written in chains, and it reads like a farewell. Paul is a prisoner in Rome. He expects to die soon, and he says so plainly: the time of his departure has come. Most of his friends are gone. He asks for his cloak, with winter coming, and for his scrolls. Above all, he wants Timothy to come to him before winter. Of all Paul's letters, this one has long been regarded as the last he wrote.

The listener has met Timothy already: the young man from Lystra, son of a Jewish mother and a Greek father, who joined Paul on his second journey and became his closest colleague. This letter adds a detail. Timothy's faith, Paul says, lived first in his grandmother Lois and his mother Eunice, and he had known the Scriptures from childhood. In the first letter Timothy was in Ephesus, facing false teachers in the church. He seems to be there still, or nearby, and some of the same troubles continue.

Paul's own situation is very different from the one at the end of Acts. There he lived for two years in Rome under guard in rented lodgings, receiving visitors freely. Here he is chained like a criminal, and a friend named Onesiphorus had to search hard to find him. He has already faced a first hearing, at which no one stood with him. The traditional reconstruction is that Paul was released after the imprisonment Acts describes, travelled again for a time, and was then arrested a second time, during the reign of the emperor Nero. After the great fire of Rome in the year sixty-four, Nero turned on the Christians of the city, and early Christian tradition holds that Paul was beheaded there in the middle or later years of that decade. As with the first letter to Timothy, many scholars believe these words come from a follower writing after Paul's death, while others see in their personal detail the clearest marks of Paul's own hand. The letter presents itself as Paul's final word, and is read that way here.

The reasons for writing are both personal and urgent. Paul wants Timothy at his side, and asks him to bring Mark, the same Mark who once deserted Paul and Barnabas on their first journey and caused a sharp quarrel between them, now valued and wanted. Paul also wants to hand on responsibility. The gospel has been entrusted to him, and he is passing it to Timothy, who must in turn pass it on to faithful people who will teach others. Behind this lies a darker picture: desertion in the province of Asia, teachers who say the resurrection has already happened, and a time coming when people will turn away from sound teaching.

The letter moves through four short chapters. It begins with memory and encouragement, calling Timothy not to be ashamed of the gospel or of Paul in prison. It goes on with images of the soldier, the athlete and the farmer, and warnings about quarrelsome teachers. It describes the hard times of the last days and points Timothy back to the Scriptures he has known since childhood. And it ends with a solemn charge, Paul's own reflection on a life nearly finished, and a list of names that brings his last circle of friends and opponents briefly into view.

This is the closing scene of Paul's life as the New Testament shows it. The man who first appears in Acts holding the cloaks of those who stoned Stephen now waits in Rome for his own death, still writing, still sending people out, and still asking for his parchments.

=== 1
The greeting is much like the one in the first letter, but the mood is different almost at once. Paul writes as a prisoner, and the opening is full of memory and longing.

He remembers Timothy in his prayers night and day. He remembers Timothy's tears, probably at their last parting. He remembers his grandmother Lois and his mother Eunice, in whom this faith first lived. And he reminds Timothy of the gift he received when Paul laid hands on him, urging him to fan it into flame, because God has not given a spirit of fear.

Paul asks Timothy not to be ashamed of the gospel, or of Paul himself in chains, but to share in suffering for it. He speaks of his own confidence in the one he has believed, and charges Timothy to guard the treasure entrusted to him.

The chapter ends with news, some bitter, some warm. Everyone in the province of Asia has turned away from Paul, including two men named Phygelus and Hermogenes. But Onesiphorus, whose kindness Timothy had seen in Ephesus, came to Rome, searched until he found Paul, and was not ashamed of his chains.

=== 2
Paul turns from those who deserted him back to Timothy, and tells him to be strong. The first task is to pass on what he has learned. The teaching Timothy heard from Paul must be entrusted to faithful men who can teach others in turn. Paul, nearing his death, is thinking about the generations after him.

He then reaches for three familiar pictures of hard, single-minded work: the soldier on duty, the athlete who competes by the rules, and the farmer who labours before he eats. He reminds Timothy of the gospel itself, Jesus Christ raised from the dead and descended from David, for which Paul is now chained like a criminal. Then he quotes a trustworthy saying, set out in balanced lines.

The second half of the chapter returns to the troubles Timothy faces. Teachers named Hymenaeus and Philetus are claiming that the resurrection has already taken place, and their talk is spreading like gangrene. Paul urges Timothy to handle the word of truth rightly, to avoid foolish arguments, and to correct opponents gently, in the hope that they may yet return to the truth.

=== 3
Having urged Timothy to deal patiently with those who oppose him, Paul widens the view. In the last days, he says, terrible times will come, and he paints a long and dark portrait of people who love themselves, love money and love pleasure, keeping the outward form of religion but denying its power. He links them with the false teachers Timothy already knows, who work their way into households. He compares them with Jannes and Jambres, names that Jewish tradition gave to the magicians of Pharaoh who opposed Moses, though the Old Testament itself does not name them.

Then Paul sets Timothy's own story against that picture. Timothy has followed Paul's teaching and way of life, and he saw what happened in Antioch, Iconium and Lystra, the towns of his own region, where Paul was driven out and, at Lystra, stoned and left for dead. Acts places those events on the first journey, before Timothy joined him, when Timothy was likely still a young man at home.

The chapter ends by pointing Timothy back to the sacred writings he has known since he was a child, the Scriptures of Israel, which Paul describes as breathed out by God.

=== 4
The final chapter opens with Paul's most solemn charge. In the presence of God and of Christ Jesus, who will judge the living and the dead, he tells Timothy to preach the word, whether the time is convenient or not, because a time is coming when people will not put up with sound teaching.

Then Paul speaks of himself. He describes his life as being poured out like a drink offering, the wine that was poured beside a sacrifice on the altar, and he looks back on a fight fought and a race finished. These are among the best known words he wrote.

The rest of the letter is personal and full of names. Demas has deserted him for Thessalonica, and others are away on missions. Only Luke, the physician and companion of many journeys, remains. Paul asks for Mark, and for the cloak and scrolls left at Troas. He warns against Alexander the coppersmith. He describes a first hearing of his case in which no one stood by him, though the Lord did. He sends greetings to Prisca and Aquila, the tentmakers he first met in Corinth, and asks Timothy one last time to come before winter. Whether Timothy reached him in time, the New Testament does not say.
