=== intro
Moses is dead. For forty years he has led Israel through the wilderness, and at the close of Deuteronomy he climbed Mount Nebo, looked out over the land he would never enter, and died there in the land of Moab. The people mourned him for thirty days. Now they are camped on the plains of Moab, at a place called Shittim, on the eastern side of the Jordan River. Across the water, a few miles away, stands the walled city of Jericho, and beyond it rise the hills of Canaan, the land promised long ago to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob.

The book of Joshua tells how Israel crossed that river and came into the land. It is named after the man who leads them. Joshua son of Nun, from the tribe of Ephraim, has been at Moses' side since the days of the exodus. He commanded Israel's fighters against the Amalekites soon after they left Egypt, he waited on the mountain while Moses received the law, and he was one of the twelve spies sent into Canaan nearly forty years before. Of those twelve, only Joshua and Caleb urged the people to trust God and go in. The rest spread fear, the people refused, and a whole generation died in the wilderness. Now that generation's children stand at the border, and Joshua, an old soldier who has waited half his life for this day, is their leader.

The book does not name its author. Jewish tradition credits it to Joshua himself, with the account of his death added after him. Canaan in these days is not an empty land but a patchwork of small city-states, each with its own king and its own walled town, set among hills and valleys and strung along the coast. Egypt to the south has long held influence here, but no great empire is in control. The peoples are named again and again: Canaanites, Amorites, Hittites, Hivites, Perizzites, Girgashites and Jebusites.

The book moves in three great stages. The first half is a story of crossing and conquest. The waters of the Jordan stand still, as the sea once did for Moses, and the people pass over on dry ground. Jericho falls. Then, in a series of swift campaigns, first in the centre of the land, then the south, then the far north, Israel breaks the power of the Canaanite kings. Yet even in victory there are setbacks: a defeat at a small town called Ai, caused by one man's theft, and a treaty made too quickly with the people of Gibeon, who arrive in disguise.

The second half is very different. Chapter after chapter maps the land as it is divided among the twelve tribes, by lot, with boundaries traced from town to town and spring to spring. To a modern listener these lists can feel like reading a land registry, and in a sense that is exactly what they are. They record that God's promise has become real ground, with real borders, belonging to real families. Along the way there are glimpses of individuals: Caleb, now eighty-five, asking for the hill country he saw as a young spy, and the daughters of a man named Zelophehad claiming the inheritance Moses had promised them.

The book also faces the hardest feature of these chapters squarely. Israel is commanded to devote the Canaanite cities to destruction, a practice of total war that troubles modern readers. Joshua presents it as God's judgement on peoples whose wickedness had long been building, and it is not presented as permission for Israel to do as it pleases: the same severity falls on an Israelite who breaks faith, while a Canaanite woman named Rahab, who trusts Israel's God, is spared and welcomed.

The final stage is a farewell. Joshua, now very old, gathers the leaders and then the whole nation, first at an unnamed place and then at Shechem, the place where Abraham built his first altar in Canaan. He recounts all that God has done and calls them to choose whom they will serve. The people promise to serve the Lord, and the book ends with three burials: Joshua himself, the bones of Joseph, carried all the way from Egypt as he had asked, and Eleazar the priest, son of Aaron.

Joshua is a book of fulfilment, but not of completion. Again and again it notes that the land is not fully taken, that Canaanites still live among the tribes. That unfinished work, and the question of whether Israel will keep the promises it makes at Shechem, lead directly into the book of Judges.

=== 1
The book opens with a sentence that closes an era: after the death of Moses, the Lord speaks to Joshua. The people are still camped on the plains of Moab, east of the Jordan, and the river lies between them and the land.

God's first words to Joshua are a commission and a promise. The land that was promised to Moses is now to be entered, its borders sketched in broad strokes from the southern wilderness to Lebanon and the Euphrates, and west to the Mediterranean, which the Israelites call the Great Sea. As God was with Moses, so he will be with Joshua. Three times comes the charge to be strong and courageous, joined to a command to keep the Book of the Law close, speaking it and meditating on it day and night.

Joshua then acts. He tells the officers to prepare the people to cross within three days. He also turns to the tribes of Reuben and Gad and half the tribe of Manasseh, who had already been given land east of the Jordan by Moses. Their families may stay behind, but their fighting men must cross with their brothers. Their answer gives the new leader the backing he needs.

=== 2
Before the crossing, Joshua does what Moses once did: he sends spies. This time there are only two, and they go secretly from Shittim to Jericho, the city that guards the fords of the Jordan and the route up into the hill country.

Jericho is one of the oldest towns in the world, an oasis city of palms and springs on the floor of the Jordan valley. The spies find lodging in the house of a prostitute named Rahab, whose home is built into the city wall. Word reaches the king almost at once, and his men come knocking.

What happens next turns on Rahab. She is a Canaanite, an outsider in every sense, yet she has heard what Israel's God did at the Red Sea and to the Amorite kings Sihon and Og east of the Jordan, and she has drawn her own conclusions. Her words on the roof that night are a remarkable confession from a woman of Jericho. Before the spies leave, she asks for an oath, and a scarlet cord becomes the sign of the agreement between them.

=== 3
The spies have returned with good news: the people of the land are melting in fear. Now, early in the morning, Joshua leads the whole camp down from Shittim to the edge of the Jordan, and there they wait for three days.

It is the season of harvest, in spring, when the Jordan is swollen with rain and the melting snows of Mount Hermon and overflows its banks. There is no bridge, and the fords are dangerous. The plan is not a military one. The ark of the covenant, the sacred chest that holds the tablets of the law and represents God's presence among his people, is to go ahead, carried by the priests, with the people following at a respectful distance.

Joshua tells the people to consecrate themselves, because tomorrow the Lord will do wonders among them. The moment the priests' feet touch the water, the narrative slows, and it pays careful attention to places: a town called Adam far upstream, and the Salt Sea, which we know as the Dead Sea, to the south. The generation that grew up hearing about the Red Sea is about to see something like it for themselves.

=== 4
The whole nation has crossed the Jordan on dry ground, and the priests are still standing with the ark in the middle of the riverbed. Before they come up, God gives Joshua one more instruction: twelve men, one from each tribe, are to carry twelve stones out of the river.

This chapter is about memory. Israel's faith was handed down in families, and the stones are meant to provoke a question. When your children ask what these stones mean, you will tell them. The account also mentions a second set of stones, set up in the middle of the river itself.

The fighting men of the eastern tribes cross armed, as they had promised, and the narrator notes that on this day the Lord exalted Joshua in the sight of all Israel, as he had Moses. When the priests finally step onto the western bank, the river returns to its flood. The people make their first camp in the land at a place called Gilgal, near Jericho, on the tenth day of the first month, the very day on which, forty years earlier, each family in Egypt had chosen its Passover lamb. Gilgal will be their base for years to come.

=== 5
Israel is camped at Gilgal, and news of the crossing has spread fear among the Amorite and Canaanite kings. Militarily, this would be the moment to strike. Instead, God commands something that leaves the whole army vulnerable: the men are to be circumcised.

Circumcision was the sign of the covenant God made with Abraham, and every man who came out of Egypt had received it, but the generation born in the wilderness had not. Before they take the land, the sons must take up the covenant of their fathers. The name Gilgal is linked to a Hebrew word meaning to roll, because God says that he has rolled away the reproach of Egypt.

Then comes the first Passover in the land, and with it a quiet but significant change. The people eat the produce of Canaan, and the next day the manna, the bread from heaven that had fed them every morning for forty years, stops.

The chapter ends near Jericho with a strange encounter. Joshua meets a man with a drawn sword, and asks the obvious question: are you for us or for our enemies? The answer he receives, and the command that follows, echo the moment when Moses stood before the burning bush.

=== 6
The commander of the Lord's army has met Joshua near Jericho, and now the narrative turns to the city itself. Jericho is shut up tight. No one goes out and no one comes in.

The Lord tells Joshua how the city will be taken, and the plan is unlike any siege in the ancient world. There are no ramps, no battering rams, no tunnels under the walls. Instead there is a procession. For six days the army marches once around the city in silence, with seven priests blowing rams' horns before the ark. On the seventh day they march around seven times. The repeated sevens give the whole event the character of worship as much as warfare.

Before the final shout, Joshua lays down two commands. The city is to be devoted to the Lord, which means that nothing in it may be kept as plunder; the precious metals go to the Lord's treasury. And Rahab and her household, behind the scarlet cord, are to be spared. Both commands matter for what comes next. The chapter closes with a curse on anyone who rebuilds Jericho, a curse whose fulfilment is recorded centuries later in the book of Kings.

=== 7
Jericho has fallen, and Israel's fame is spreading through the land. But the chapter opens with a sentence the people themselves do not yet know: the Israelites have acted unfaithfully. A man named Achan, of the tribe of Judah, has taken some of the things from Jericho that were devoted to the Lord.

The next target is Ai, a smaller town in the hills above the Jordan valley, near Bethel. The route from Jericho climbs steeply, some three thousand feet, into the central hill country, and whoever holds that ground controls the roads across the land. The scouts are confident. Only a few thousand men will be needed.

What follows is Israel's first defeat in Canaan. The loss is small in numbers, about thirty-six men, but its effect is enormous: the hearts of the people melt like water, the very words Rahab used of the Canaanites. Joshua lies face down before the ark until evening, and his prayer is raw and honest. God's answer sends him back to his feet, and the morning brings a slow, searching process, tribe by tribe and family by family, until one man stands exposed.

=== 8
The trouble in the camp has been dealt with in the Valley of Achor, and now God speaks to Joshua with the same words of encouragement he heard at the start: do not be afraid or discouraged. Israel is to go up against Ai again, this time with the whole army, and this time the plunder and livestock may be kept.

The second attack on Ai is told with real military detail. Joshua sets an ambush on the west side of the town, between Ai and Bethel, while the main force camps to the north, across a valley. The plan uses the enemy's confidence against them. The men of Ai have seen Israel run once, and they expect to see it again.

After the victory, the scene shifts north to Shechem, between two mountains, Ebal and Gerizim. Back in Deuteronomy, Moses had commanded that once Israel entered the land, they should gather there, build an altar, write the law on stones, and hear its blessings and curses read aloud. Now Joshua does exactly that, before the whole assembly, including women, children and the foreigners living among them.

=== 9
Word of Jericho and Ai is spreading, and the kings west of the Jordan, from the hill country to the coast, begin to form an alliance against Israel. But one people chooses a different course. The Gibeonites live only a short distance from Ai, in a cluster of towns in the central hills, and they decide that if they cannot defeat Israel, they will deceive them.

Their plan is simple and well executed. Envoys arrive at the camp at Gilgal looking as though they have travelled for weeks: worn sacks, cracked wineskins, patched sandals, and bread that is dry and mouldy. They claim to come from a distant country, beyond the reach of the command to destroy the peoples of Canaan, and they ask for a treaty.

The Israelites inspect the evidence, and the narrator adds a short, telling comment: they did not ask counsel from the Lord. An oath sworn in the Lord's name cannot be undone, even when the truth comes out three days later. How Israel handles that promise will matter long after this chapter, for Gibeon will reappear in the history of Israel, and the oath will still be remembered in the days of Saul and David.

=== 10
The treaty with Gibeon has consequences at once. Gibeon is a large and important city, and its alliance with Israel alarms the king of Jerusalem, Adoni-zedek, whose name means my lord is righteous. He calls on four other Amorite kings of the south, from Hebron, Jarmuth, Lachish and Eglon, and together they march against Gibeon to punish it.

The Gibeonites send an urgent message to Joshua at Gilgal, and Joshua answers it. Israel's oath now binds them to defend the very people who deceived them. The army marches all night, climbing from the Jordan valley into the hills, and falls on the coalition at dawn.

The battle that follows is remembered for its strange and wonderful details: hailstones that kill more than the sword, and Joshua's cry for the sun to stand still over Gibeon and the moon over the Valley of Aijalon. The narrator quotes the words from an ancient collection of poems called the Book of Jashar, which has not survived. The second half of the chapter follows a rapid campaign across the southern hills and foothills, and its repeated phrases fall like hammer blows, one city after another.

=== 11
The south has been subdued. Now the scene moves to the far north, to Hazor, the greatest city in Canaan, in the region above the Sea of Galilee, which the book calls the Sea of Chinnereth. Its king, Jabin, summons the kings of the north to a grand alliance, and they gather by the waters of Merom with a vast army and, for the first time, horses and chariots.

Chariots were the most advanced weapons of war in that age, and Israel had none. On flat ground they could be devastating. God's word to Joshua is the familiar one, do not be afraid of them, together with an unusual order: the horses are to be hamstrung and the chariots burned. Israel is not to build its strength on the enemy's weapons.

The chapter then draws back to summarise. The war against these kings went on for a long time, and only Gibeon made peace. It also records that Joshua drove the Anakim, the giant clan whose size had terrified the spies forty years earlier, from the hill country, though some survived in Philistine towns such as Gath. The chapter ends with a sentence of quiet arrival: the land had rest from war.

=== 12
The fighting is over for now, and before the land is divided the book pauses to take stock. This chapter is a roll call of defeated kings, the kind of victory list that ancient rulers carved on monuments.

It begins on the eastern side of the Jordan, with the two kings Moses defeated before he died: Sihon, the Amorite king who ruled from Heshbon, and Og, king of Bashan, the last of an ancient people called the Rephaim. Their lands became the home of Reuben, Gad and half of Manasseh.

Then come the kings west of the Jordan whom Joshua defeated, named one by one, each followed by the same single word of tally. Some are familiar from the chapters just read, Jericho, Ai, Jerusalem, Hebron, Lachish and Hazor. Others, such as Megiddo, Taanach and Tirzah, appear here for the first time, and some of them will become important places later in Israel's history. The final count is thirty-one kings. The list closes the first half of the book; the second half turns from taking the land to sharing it out.

=== 13
Years have passed since the campaigns began, and Joshua is now an old man. The narrator says so, and then God says it too, with a frankness that sets the tone for the rest of the book: you are old, and very much of the land remains to be possessed.

The first part of the chapter names what is still unconquered. In the south-west lies the coastal plain of the Philistines, with their five cities, Gaza, Ashdod, Ashkelon, Gath and Ekron, and in the north the coast of the Sidonians and the mountains of Lebanon. God promises to drive out these peoples himself, and tells Joshua to divide the land among the tribes regardless.

Before turning to the land west of the Jordan, the book looks back east, to the territory Moses had already given to Reuben, Gad and half of Manasseh: the high plateau beyond the Dead Sea, the hills of Gilead and the rich pastures of Bashan. Among the place names is a reminder of the death of Balaam, the diviner once hired to curse Israel. Twice the chapter notes that the tribe of Levi receives no land, because its portion is the Lord himself and the offerings made to him.

=== 14
Now the division of the land west of the Jordan begins. It is overseen by three authorities together: Eleazar the priest, the son of Aaron, Joshua, and the heads of the tribes. The land is to be assigned by lot, so that the outcome rests with God rather than with human bargaining. The narrator also explains the arithmetic. Joseph's descendants count as two tribes, Ephraim and Manasseh, which keeps the number of landed tribes at twelve even though Levi receives no territory.

Before any lots are cast, an old man steps forward. Caleb son of Jephunneh, of the tribe of Judah, was Joshua's companion among the twelve spies. Forty-five years earlier, the two of them had stood alone against the fearful report of the other ten, and Moses had promised Caleb the land on which his feet had walked.

Caleb is now eighty-five, and he speaks with the vigour of a younger man. He does not ask for easy land. He asks for the hill country around Hebron, where the Anakim live in fortified cities, the very people whose size had frightened Israel all those years ago.

=== 15
Judah is the first tribe to receive its allotment, and it is a large one: the whole southern part of the land, from the Dead Sea to the Mediterranean, and from the wilderness of Zin in the far south up to the hills just below Jerusalem. Judah will become the most powerful tribe, the tribe of David, and its territory is described with special care.

The chapter begins by tracing the borders along ridges, valleys and springs. The northern line runs right past Jerusalem, along the Valley of Ben-hinnom on its southern side.

In the middle comes a short family story. Caleb takes Hebron, as promised, and offers his daughter Acsah in marriage to whoever captures the nearby town of Debir. The man who does so is Othniel, Caleb's nephew, a name to remember, because he will reappear as the first of the judges. Acsah herself proves resourceful, asking her father for springs of water to go with her dry land in the Negev.

The rest of the chapter lists Judah's towns by region: the Negev, the western foothills, the hill country and the wilderness by the Dead Sea. Many will reappear in the stories of Saul and David. The chapter ends with a note of failure: Judah could not drive out the Jebusites from Jerusalem.

=== 16
After Judah in the south, the lot falls to the descendants of Joseph, the two tribes of Ephraim and Manasseh, who receive the heart of the country, the central hill country north of Jerusalem. This chapter sets out their shared southern boundary, running from the Jordan near Jericho up into the hills past Bethel, and down westward to Gezer and the sea.

Then it turns to Ephraim, Joseph's younger son, whom Jacob had blessed ahead of his elder brother. Ephraim's territory is the southern part of the Joseph lands, a fertile hill region that will become the centre of the northern kingdom in later centuries. So central will the tribe become that the prophets sometimes use the name Ephraim for the whole northern kingdom of Israel.

The chapter is short, and it ends with a sentence that is becoming a refrain. The Ephraimites did not drive out the Canaanites who lived in Gezer, but made them forced labourers. The work of taking the land is still unfinished.

=== 17
Ephraim's allotment has been described, and now it is the turn of Manasseh, the elder son of Joseph. Half of the tribe, the descendants of Manasseh's son Machir, have already settled east of the Jordan in Gilead and Bashan. The rest receive land west of the river, north of Ephraim, stretching toward the plain of Jezreel and the coast.

Here the five daughters of Zelophehad reappear. Their father had died without sons, and in the wilderness they had come before Moses to ask that his name and inheritance not be lost. Moses had brought their case to the Lord, and the Lord had agreed. Now Mahlah, Noah, Hoglah, Milcah and Tirzah come before Eleazar and Joshua to claim what was promised.

The chapter ends with a complaint. The tribes of Joseph say they are too numerous for the land they have been given. The real difficulty soon comes out: the open valleys are held by Canaanites with iron chariots, especially around Beth-shean and the Valley of Jezreel. Joshua's reply is practical and direct, and it is addressed to people who have more strength than they think.

=== 18
The scene shifts. Until now the camp has been at Gilgal, near the Jordan. Now the whole congregation gathers at Shiloh, in the hill country of Ephraim, and sets up the Tent of Meeting there, the portable sanctuary built in the wilderness under Moses. Shiloh will remain Israel's central place of worship for generations, and it is there, much later, that the boy Samuel will grow up.

Judah and the tribes of Joseph have received their land, but seven tribes have not, and they seem in no hurry. Joshua's question to them is pointed: how long will you put off taking possession of the land the Lord has given you? He organises a survey. Three men from each of the seven tribes go out through the country and write a description of it, town by town, in seven portions. Then lots are cast before the Lord at Shiloh.

The first lot falls to Benjamin, the tribe descended from Jacob's youngest son. Its territory is small but strategic, a strip of land between Judah and Ephraim that includes Jericho, Bethel, Gibeon and Jebus, the town better known as Jerusalem. From this little tribe, Israel's first king will one day come.

=== 19
The lots are still being cast at Shiloh, and this chapter completes the distribution with the remaining six tribes. Each receives its portion in turn.

Simeon's inheritance lies inside Judah's territory in the south, around Beersheba, because Judah's share was more than it needed. Over time Simeon would be largely absorbed into Judah. Then the lots move north, to the hills and valleys of Galilee. Zebulun, Issachar, Asher and Naphtali settle the lands around the Valley of Jezreel, the coast north of Mount Carmel, and the hills west of the Sea of Galilee.

The last lot falls to Dan, whose territory lies on the western slopes and coastal plain near Joppa. A brief note looks ahead: the Danites will lose this land and migrate to the far north, a story told at length near the end of Judges.

Then comes a touching close. When everyone else has received an inheritance, the Israelites give one to Joshua himself, the town he asks for, Timnath-serah in the hill country of Ephraim. The leader is served last.

=== 20
The land has been divided, and now two kinds of special towns are set apart within it. The first are the cities of refuge, which Moses had commanded in the wilderness.

In the ancient world, a killing usually brought a duty of vengeance. A close relative of the dead, called the avenger of blood, was expected to pursue the killer. But what if the death was an accident? These six cities provide a place where someone who has killed unintentionally can flee and be safe until the case is heard before the community. If the killing was accidental, the person may stay there, protected, until the death of the high priest, and then return home.

The cities are spread out so that one is always within reach: three west of the Jordan, Kedesh in the north, Shechem in the centre and Hebron in the south, and three to the east, Bezer, Ramoth in Gilead and Golan. Notably, the protection extends to foreigners living among Israel as well. After the long years of war, the book pauses here over the ordinary workings of justice in a settled land.

=== 21
The cities of refuge have been named. Now the leaders of the Levites come to Shiloh to remind Eleazar and Joshua of another promise from Moses. The Levites have no territory of their own, but they were to be given towns to live in, with pastureland for their animals.

This chapter lists forty-eight of these Levitical towns, scattered through every tribe. They are distributed by lot among the three clans descended from Levi's sons, Kohath, Gershon and Merari, with the priests, the descendants of Aaron, receiving towns in Judah, Simeon and Benjamin, close to what would later become Jerusalem. The result is that the tribe set apart for God's service lives among all the others, rather than in one corner of the land. Among the towns given to the priests is Anathoth, which centuries later will be the home of the prophet Jeremiah.

The list ends with a short, ringing summary that gathers up the whole first part of the story. The Lord gave Israel all the land he had sworn to their fathers, he gave them rest on every side, and not one of all his good promises had failed.

=== 22
The land is divided and the fighting is over, and Joshua now keeps faith with the eastern tribes. Reuben, Gad and half of Manasseh have fought alongside their brothers for years, as they promised at the very beginning. Joshua commends them, blesses them, and sends them home across the Jordan with a share of the plunder.

But on their way, at the edge of the river, they build a great altar. To the tribes west of the Jordan, this looks like the beginning of rebellion. The law allowed sacrifice only at the one altar before the tabernacle, and the memory of what unfaithfulness had cost the nation was still fresh, both at Peor in the wilderness and in the sin of Achan. The whole congregation gathers at Shiloh, ready for war.

Instead of attacking, they send a delegation: Phinehas the priest, the son of Eleazar, with ten leaders, one from each tribe. Phinehas was known for his zeal at Peor, where his action had stopped a plague. The confrontation that follows on the far side of the Jordan turns on whether a river will divide God's people, and on how a misunderstanding can be handled before it becomes a civil war.

=== 23
A long time has passed. Israel has rest from its enemies, and Joshua is now very old. Knowing that he will soon die, he calls together the leaders of Israel, the elders, heads, judges and officers. The place is not named.

What follows is a farewell speech, the kind that Moses gave at the end of his life in Deuteronomy, and it is full of echoes of that book. Joshua looks back on what the Lord has done, fighting for Israel. He looks around at the nations that still remain in the land. And he looks ahead with a clear warning about what will happen if Israel begins to mix with those nations, intermarrying with them and calling on their gods.

The tone is both grateful and grave. Joshua repeats the summary heard at the end of chapter twenty-one, that not one of the Lord's good promises has failed. But he adds its shadow. The same faithfulness that kept every good promise will also keep every warning. With this speech, the old soldier hands over to the leaders the responsibility he has carried since Moses died.

=== 24
For his final act, Joshua gathers all the tribes of Israel at Shechem. The place is full of memory. It was here that Abraham first built an altar in the land of Canaan, and here that Jacob buried the foreign gods of his household under an oak. Here too, earlier in this book, the people heard the law read between the mountains of Ebal and Gerizim.

Joshua speaks in the name of the Lord, and he tells Israel's story from the beginning: Abraham's family serving other gods beyond the Euphrates, the patriarchs, the slavery in Egypt, the exodus, the wilderness, and the gift of a land they did not toil for. Then he sets a choice before the people, and declares his own.

The exchange that follows has the shape of a covenant ceremony, with words spoken, written down and witnessed by a great stone beneath an oak near the sanctuary. Joshua presses the people hard and will not accept an easy answer.

The book then ends with three burials. Joshua dies at a hundred and ten and is buried in his own town. The bones of Joseph, carried out of Egypt as he asked at the end of Genesis, are laid to rest at Shechem, on the land Jacob bought. And Eleazar the priest, son of Aaron, is buried in the hill country of Ephraim. With them a generation passes.
