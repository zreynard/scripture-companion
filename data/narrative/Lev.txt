=== intro
Exodus ended with a finished tent and a filled one. The tabernacle had been set up at the foot of Mount Sinai, on the first day of Israel's second year of freedom, and the cloud of the Lord's glory came down and filled it so completely that even Moses could not go in. That is the moment Leviticus begins. The Lord, now dwelling among his people, calls to Moses from the tent and begins to speak.

The book never moves from that spot. There are no journeys and no battles. When Leviticus ends, Israel is still camped at Sinai, and the book of Numbers opens only a month after the tabernacle was raised. Almost everything in between is speech, the words of the Lord to Moses, often with the instruction to pass them on to Aaron and his sons or to the whole people. The Hebrew title is simply the book's first word, and he called. The English name comes through Greek and Latin and means the book of the Levites, because so much of it concerns the priests, Aaron and his descendants, who belonged to the tribe of Levi.

Jewish and Christian tradition has long held Moses responsible for the book, and the book presents its laws as given through him at Sinai. Scholars have debated how and when the material was gathered and shaped, but its setting is never in doubt. It speaks to a people newly rescued from Egypt, camped in the wilderness, about to set out for a land the Lord has promised them.

The question at the heart of Leviticus grows straight out of the end of Exodus. A holy God has come to live in the middle of a camp of ordinary, imperfect people. How can that be? How can they approach him, and how can he remain among them, without his holiness destroying them? The book's answer comes in sacrifices, in a priesthood, in a calendar, and in a whole pattern of daily life marked by the distinction between holy and common, clean and unclean.

The material falls into several broad sections. The first seven chapters describe the main kinds of offering: the burnt offering, the grain offering, the peace offering, the sin offering and the guilt offering, each with its own purpose. Then the story briefly comes back into view. Aaron and his sons are ordained as priests over seven days, and on the eighth day they offer the first sacrifices, and fire comes out from the presence of the Lord. But almost at once two of Aaron's sons, Nadab and Abihu, bring unauthorised fire before the Lord, and they die. It is a sobering moment, and it shows what is at stake in everything else the book says.

The next section deals with what makes a person clean or unclean: food, childbirth, skin diseases, mildew and bodily discharges. To modern ears these chapters can sound strange, but in Leviticus uncleanness is not the same as sin. It is a condition that keeps a person from approaching the sanctuary until it is dealt with. At the centre of the book stands the Day of Atonement, the one day each year when the high priest enters the Most Holy Place behind the veil, to cleanse the sanctuary and the people from all their sins.

The later chapters are often called the Holiness Code, because of the refrain that runs through them: be holy, because I, the Lord your God, am holy. Here holiness reaches into every part of life, into family and sexual relationships, business, harvest, justice and care for the poor. Here too are the great festivals of Israel's year, the Sabbath year and the Year of Jubilee, and one more brief story, about a man who blasphemes the name of the Lord. The book draws towards its close with blessings promised for faithfulness and warnings of judgment and exile for rebellion, and it ends with rules about vows and gifts to the sanctuary.

Leviticus can be the hardest book of the Bible for a modern listener, full of blood, fat, priests' garments and diseases of the skin. But it was central to Israel's life, and it gave the vocabulary for much of what followed. Jesus called the command love your neighbour as yourself, found here in chapter nineteen, the second greatest commandment. The letter to the Hebrews reads the priesthood, the sacrifices and the Day of Atonement as shadows of the work of Christ. To hear Leviticus well is to understand what it meant, in the ancient world, for God to say: I will walk among you and be your God, and you will be my people.

=== 1
The tabernacle is newly built, and the cloud of the Lord's glory rests on it. Now the Lord calls to Moses from inside the Tent of Meeting, and the first thing he speaks about is how an ordinary Israelite may come near with an offering.

The chapter describes the burnt offering, the most complete of all the sacrifices, in which the whole animal is burned on the altar and goes up in smoke to the Lord. The worshipper may bring a bull from the herd, a sheep or goat from the flock, or, if that is all he can afford, a dove or a young pigeon. The animal must be male and without defect.

Watch for the part the worshipper plays himself. He brings the animal to the entrance of the tent, lays his hand on its head, and slaughters it, while the priests, Aaron's sons, handle the blood and the altar. The laying on of the hand identifies the animal with the one who offers it, and the offering is accepted on his behalf, to make atonement for him. A phrase recurs at the end of each case: a food offering, a pleasing aroma to the Lord.

=== 2
The burnt offering has been described. Now the instructions turn from animals to grain. The grain offering is a gift from the harvest, the produce of a farmer's fields, and it is the only one of the main offerings in which nothing dies.

It can be brought in several forms: fine flour with olive oil and frankincense, or loaves and wafers baked in an oven, cooked on a griddle or in a pan, or heads of new grain roasted over the fire. In every case the priest takes a handful, called the memorial portion, and burns it on the altar. The rest belongs to Aaron and his sons, and this is one of the ways the priests, who had no farmland of their own, were to be fed.

Two ingredients are forbidden on the altar, leaven and honey, both associated with fermentation. One is required in every offering: salt, called here the salt of the covenant of your God. Salt preserves, and the phrase seems to speak of a covenant meant to last.

=== 3
After the burnt offering and the grain offering comes a third kind, the peace offering, sometimes translated the fellowship offering. It may be an animal from the herd or the flock, male or female, but it must be without blemish.

The procedure begins much like the burnt offering. The worshipper lays his hand on the animal's head and slaughters it at the entrance to the tent, and the priests splash its blood against the altar. But this time only part of the animal is burned: the fat around the internal organs, the kidneys and the lobe of the liver, and, for a sheep, the fat tail. As the chapter later says, all the fat belongs to the Lord.

What happens to the rest is described in chapter seven. Most of the meat is eaten by the worshipper and his family, and a portion goes to the priest. The peace offering was a shared meal in the presence of God, and it was often brought in thanksgiving or to keep a vow. The chapter ends with a permanent rule for Israel wherever they live: they must not eat fat or blood.

=== 4
The first three offerings could be brought freely, as gifts. Now the Lord turns to offerings that are required when something has gone wrong. This chapter introduces the sin offering, which many scholars prefer to call the purification offering, because its blood cleanses the sanctuary from the defilement caused by sin.

It deals with unintentional sins, things done wrongly through error or ignorance, and it is arranged by rank. If the anointed priest sins, or the whole community, the offering is a young bull, and the priest carries its blood into the tent itself, sprinkling it before the veil and putting it on the horns of the golden altar of incense. For a leader, the offering is a male goat; for one of the ordinary people, a female goat or a lamb, and the blood is put on the horns of the bronze altar outside.

The pattern suggests that the more responsible the person, the more serious the effect of the sin, and the deeper into the sanctuary the cleansing must reach. A refrain closes each case: the priest will make atonement for them, and they will be forgiven.

=== 5
The sin offering has been introduced for unintentional wrongs. This chapter adds some particular cases where it is needed. They include someone who stays silent when he should testify, someone who touches something unclean without realising, and someone who swears a rash oath. In each case, when the person becomes aware of it, he must confess what he has done and bring his offering.

The chapter then makes allowances for the poor. Someone who cannot afford a lamb or goat may bring two doves or two young pigeons, and someone who cannot afford even those may bring a small measure of fine flour. No one is to be shut out of forgiveness because of poverty.

The second half of the chapter introduces the last of the main offerings, the guilt offering, sometimes called the reparation offering. It deals with cases where something has been taken or damaged, starting with the holy things that belong to the Lord. The offering is a ram, and the person must also repay the value of what was harmed, with a fifth added.

=== 6
The guilt offering was introduced at the end of the previous chapter for wrongs against the Lord's holy things. This chapter opens by applying it to wrongs against a neighbour: lying about a deposit or a lost item, stealing, extortion, swearing falsely. Such sins, the text says, are also unfaithfulness to the Lord. The guilty person must first repay his neighbour in full, with a fifth added, and only then bring his ram to the priest.

Then the focus changes. Chapters one to five spoke to the people about what to bring. Now the Lord speaks to Aaron and his sons, the priests, about how to handle each offering once it is brought. These are instructions for those who serve at the altar.

The first concerns the burnt offering, and one detail stands out: the fire on the altar must be kept burning continually. It must never go out. There are also rules for the priests' share of the grain offering and of the sin offering, which are most holy and must be eaten in the courtyard of the tent, and for a regular grain offering of the anointed priest himself, which is burned completely.

=== 7
The instructions for the priests continue. This chapter completes the rules for the guilt offering and then turns to the peace offering, the shared meal, explaining in more detail who may eat it and when.

A peace offering might be brought in thanksgiving, or to fulfil a vow, or simply as a freewill gift, and the rules differ slightly for each. The meat of a thanksgiving offering must be eaten on the same day; others may be eaten on the next day too, but anything left on the third day must be burned. Anyone who is ceremonially unclean must not eat it. Then come the portions set aside for the priests: the breast, which is waved before the Lord, and the right thigh. This is to be their share for all generations.

The chapter closes with a summary of the whole section. These are the regulations for the burnt offering, the grain offering, the sin offering, the guilt offering, the ordination offering and the peace offering, which the Lord gave Moses on Mount Sinai.

=== 8
The laws of the offerings are complete, and Leviticus now returns to the story. Back in Exodus, on the mountain, God gave Moses detailed instructions for ordaining Aaron and his sons as priests. Now, at the newly built tabernacle, those instructions are carried out.

The whole community gathers at the entrance to the Tent of Meeting to watch. Moses, not Aaron, leads the ceremony, because the priests cannot yet act as priests. He washes Aaron and his sons, dresses Aaron in the high priest's garments one by one, from the tunic to the gold plate on his turban, and anoints the tabernacle, the altar and Aaron himself with the sacred oil. Then come the three sacrifices described in Exodus: a bull as a sin offering, a ram as a burnt offering, and the ram of ordination, whose blood is placed on the right ear, thumb and big toe of each new priest.

A phrase is repeated through the chapter, and it is the key to it: as the Lord commanded Moses. The chapter ends with Aaron and his sons told to stay at the entrance to the tent for seven days and nights, until their ordination is complete.

=== 9
For seven days Aaron and his sons have stayed at the entrance to the Tent of Meeting while their ordination was completed. Now it is the eighth day, and for the first time the new priests are to carry out the service of the tabernacle themselves.

Moses calls Aaron, his sons and the elders of Israel and tells them what to bring: a sin offering and a burnt offering for Aaron himself, then sin, burnt, peace and grain offerings for the people. He gives the reason in a single sentence. Today the Lord will appear to you. Aaron offers first for himself and then for the people, exactly as the opening chapters of the book prescribed, and then he lifts his hands toward the people and blesses them.

Moses and Aaron go into the tent together. When they come out and bless the people again, the moment everything has been building towards arrives, and the glory of the Lord appears to the whole community. What the people see next fills them with joy and awe.

=== 10
The day began with the new priesthood serving for the first time, and it reached its height when fire came out from the presence of the Lord and consumed the offering on the altar, while the people shouted for joy. Now, apparently on that same day, everything goes wrong.

Aaron's two eldest sons, Nadab and Abihu, take their censers, the pans used for burning incense, and offer what the text calls unauthorised fire before the Lord, something he had not commanded. Exactly what they did wrong is not explained, and interpreters have suggested many possibilities. The result is immediate. Fire comes out from the Lord, the same fire that had just consumed the offering, and they die.

The rest of the chapter deals with the aftermath. Moses explains what has happened, and Aaron is silent. The bodies are carried out of the camp by two of their cousins. Aaron and his surviving sons, Eleazar and Ithamar, are told not to show the usual signs of mourning, because they are on duty before the Lord. Then the Lord speaks directly to Aaron, telling the priests not to drink wine when they serve, and giving them their great task: to distinguish between holy and common, clean and unclean, and to teach Israel. The chapter ends with a tense exchange between Moses and Aaron about the sin offering.

=== 11
The priests have just been told that their task is to distinguish between holy and common, between clean and unclean, and to teach Israel to do the same. The next five chapters set out what clean and unclean mean in practice, beginning with what the Israelites may eat.

This chapter goes through the animal world by its main divisions: animals of the land, creatures of the water, birds, insects, and the small creatures that move along the ground. Land animals may be eaten if they both have a divided hoof and chew the cud, so cattle, sheep and goats are clean, while the camel, the rabbit and the pig are not. Fish must have fins and scales. A list of birds is forbidden, most of them birds of prey or scavengers. Some insects, the locusts and grasshoppers, are allowed.

Why these animals and not others has been discussed since ancient times, and the chapter itself does not say, beyond one reason that it gives twice near the end. I am the Lord who brought you up out of Egypt to be your God; therefore be holy, because I am holy. The chapter also explains how contact with dead animals makes a person unclean, usually until evening, and what must be washed or broken as a result.

=== 12
Clean and unclean food has been set out. Now the laws turn from what enters the body to the body itself, and to the processes of life. This short chapter deals with a woman who has given birth.

After the birth of a son, the mother is unclean for seven days, and the boy is circumcised on the eighth day. Then she waits a further thirty-three days before her purification is complete. For a daughter, both periods are doubled. The text does not explain the difference. During this time she is not to touch anything holy or enter the sanctuary, and the uncleanness is linked specifically to the flow of blood that follows childbirth, not to the child or to any wrongdoing.

When the time is complete, she brings a lamb for a burnt offering and a dove or young pigeon for a sin offering, and the priest makes atonement for her, so that she is clean. If she cannot afford a lamb, two birds will do. Centuries later, Luke's Gospel records that Mary and Joseph brought the poorer offering of two birds, when they came to the temple after the birth of Jesus.

=== 13
After childbirth, the laws of purity turn to skin disease. Older English versions call it leprosy, but the Hebrew term covers a range of conditions, from rashes and burns to serious, lasting diseases. It is not necessarily the illness we call leprosy today.

Here the priest acts almost like a health inspector, though his concern is not medical treatment. His task is to examine and to decide. The chapter gives him a series of signs to look for: swelling, discoloured patches, whether hair in the spot has turned white, whether the sore is deeper than the skin, and whether it spreads. Where the case is unclear, the person is isolated for seven days and examined again. The priest then pronounces him clean or unclean.

The cost of being unclean was heavy. Someone with a lasting skin disease had to wear torn clothes, let his hair hang loose, cover his mouth and call out, unclean, unclean, and live alone outside the camp. The same kind of examination is then applied to mildew or mould in fabrics and leather.

=== 14
The priest has been told how to recognise a skin disease and pronounce a person unclean. Now comes the other side: what happens when someone recovers. Since that person lives outside the camp, it is the priest who must go out to him.

The ritual of restoration takes place in stages. First, outside the camp, two clean birds are used. One is killed over fresh water, and the living bird, dipped in its blood with cedar wood, scarlet yarn and hyssop, is released into the open field. The person washes, shaves and bathes and may then come back into the camp, though not yet into his own tent. On the eighth day he brings offerings to the tabernacle, and the priest places blood and oil on his right ear, right thumb and right big toe, in a ceremony that closely resembles the ordination of the priests. Once again there is provision for the poor.

The second half of the chapter looks ahead to life in Canaan, when the Israelites will live in houses rather than tents. It sets out how to deal with mildew or mould that appears in a house's walls, up to the point of tearing the house down.

=== 15
The laws of purity end with discharges from the body. This chapter deals with the reproductive organs, of men and women, and with discharges both normal and abnormal.

The chapter is carefully balanced. It begins with a man who has an unusual discharge, then deals with the normal emission of semen, then with intercourse between a man and a woman, then with a woman's monthly period, and finally with a woman who has a prolonged flow of blood. In each case it explains who and what become unclean, how long the uncleanness lasts, and how it is removed, usually by washing and waiting until evening. For the longer conditions, an offering of two birds is brought once the person is well.

None of this is presented as sin. These are natural parts of life. But many interpreters see in them a link with the boundary between life and death, and they make a person temporarily unfit to approach the sanctuary. The chapter closes with the reason behind all five chapters of purity law: so that Israel will not die by defiling the dwelling place of the Lord that is among them.

=== 16
The laws of clean and unclean are complete. Now the book reaches its centre, and it begins by reaching back. The Lord speaks to Moses after the death of Aaron's two sons, Nadab and Abihu, who died when they came before him with unauthorised fire. The question their deaths raised is how anyone may come into the presence of a holy God and live. This chapter gives the answer for the most holy place of all.

Aaron is not to enter the Most Holy Place, behind the veil, whenever he chooses. He may go in only once a year, on the tenth day of the seventh month, in the autumn, on the day that came to be called the Day of Atonement. He must lay aside his splendid garments and wear plain linen. He must first offer a bull for his own sins and those of his household, and he must go in shielded by a cloud of incense.

The chapter's most famous detail involves two goats. Lots are cast. One goat is sacrificed, and its blood is carried behind the veil and sprinkled on the mercy seat, to cleanse the sanctuary from the uncleanness and rebellion of Israel. Over the other, the high priest confesses all the people's sins, and it is led away into the wilderness. The English word scapegoat comes from this chapter. The day is to be kept for ever, as a solemn Sabbath when the people humble themselves.

=== 17
With the Day of Atonement, the first half of Leviticus reaches its high point. From this chapter on, the focus widens from the sanctuary and the priests to the everyday life of the whole people. This is where the section often called the Holiness Code begins, running on to chapter twenty-six.

The section opens with the question of where animals may be killed and what may be done with their blood. While Israel is in the wilderness, any ox, sheep or goat slaughtered must be brought to the entrance of the Tent of Meeting and offered as a peace offering, rather than killed out in the open fields. The text gives a pointed reason. Some of the people have been offering sacrifices to goat demons, spirits believed to haunt the wilderness, and this must stop.

At the heart of the chapter is a statement of great importance for understanding sacrifice in the Old Testament: the life of a creature is in its blood, and God has given it to Israel on the altar to make atonement for their lives. That is why blood must never be eaten, not by Israelites or by foreigners living among them, and why even the blood of a hunted animal must be poured out and covered with earth.

=== 18
The Holiness Code moves from the altar into the home. This chapter begins with a solemn statement repeated through it, I am the Lord your God, and a warning. Israel must not live like the people of Egypt, where they used to live, or like the people of Canaan, where they are going. They are to keep God's laws instead, and live by them.

Most of the chapter is a list of forbidden sexual relationships, beginning with close relatives: parents, step-parents, sisters and half-sisters, grandchildren, aunts, in-laws. It then forbids adultery, the sacrifice of children to the god Molech, sexual relations between men, and bestiality. Molech was a deity associated with child sacrifice, and his name will recur in Israel's later history, especially in the days of the kings.

The closing lines paint a vivid picture. The nations who lived in Canaan before Israel defiled themselves and the land with these practices, and the land is about to vomit them out. If Israel does the same, the land will vomit them out too. It is a warning that will echo through the rest of the Old Testament, until Israel and Judah are carried away into exile.

=== 19
The previous chapter ended with a warning against the customs of Egypt and Canaan. This chapter sets out the positive picture, and it opens with the words that sum up the whole Holiness Code. Speak to the whole congregation of Israel: be holy, because I, the Lord your God, am holy.

What follows is one of the richest chapters in the Old Testament, and it moves quickly, sometimes in a single sentence, from one area of life to another. Many of the Ten Commandments reappear here, woven together with laws about worship, harvest, business, the courts and the family. Farmers are to leave the edges of their fields and the fallen grapes for the poor and the foreigner. Wages must be paid the same day. The deaf must not be cursed, nor a stumbling block put before the blind. Judges must favour neither rich nor poor. Weights and measures must be honest.

Some of the laws are less familiar to modern ears, such as the ban on mixing two kinds of seed or cloth, or on certain ways of cutting the hair and beard. But the chapter also holds the command Jesus would call the second greatest of all, love your neighbour as yourself, and extends it to the foreigner. A refrain marks each section: I am the Lord.

=== 20
Chapter eighteen listed forbidden practices, especially in sexual relationships, and chapter nineteen set out the positive shape of a holy life. Now chapter twenty returns to many of the same offences, but this time it gives the penalties.

The chapter opens with the gravest of them: giving children to Molech, the god associated with child sacrifice, and turning to mediums and spiritists. Then it lists penalties for cursing father or mother, for adultery, and for the forbidden sexual unions described two chapters earlier. Many of them carry the death penalty, and others the sentence of being cut off from the people. To modern readers these penalties are severe, and they reflect how seriously the law regarded the family and the purity of the community gathered around God's sanctuary.

As in chapter eighteen, the warnings are set against the nations of Canaan, whose practices the Lord says he abhors, and the reminder is repeated that the land could vomit Israel out as well. The chapter draws the lines together in one sentence: you are to be holy to me, because I, the Lord, am holy, and I have set you apart from the nations to be my own.

=== 21
The Holiness Code has spoken to the whole people. Now it turns to the priests, the sons of Aaron, who serve closest to the Lord and so are held to a stricter standard.

A priest lives in constant contact with holy things, and the chapter's concern is what might make him unfit to serve. Contact with a dead body made a person unclean, so an ordinary priest may only attend to the bodies of his closest relatives. He must not shave his head or cut his body in the mourning customs of the surrounding peoples, and there are limits on whom he may marry. The high priest is held to a higher standard still. He may not go near any dead body, even his father's or mother's, and he may not show the usual signs of grief, just as Aaron was told when Nadab and Abihu died.

The second half of the chapter deals with physical defects. A descendant of Aaron who is blind, lame or otherwise impaired may not offer sacrifices at the altar, just as an animal with a defect may not be offered. But he is not cast out. He is still a priest, and he may still eat the holy food of his God.

=== 22
The instructions for the priests continue, and now they concern the sacred food. Many of the offerings Israel brings are partly eaten by the priests and their families, and this chapter sets out who may eat them and when.

A priest who is unclean, through skin disease, a discharge, contact with a corpse or anything else, must not eat the holy offerings until he has washed and the sun has set. Those outside the priest's household, including guests and hired workers, may not share them, though a slave who belongs to the household may. A priest's daughter who marries outside the priesthood loses her right to eat, but she regains it if she is widowed or divorced, childless, and returns to her father's house.

The second half of the chapter turns to the animals offered. Whether a burnt offering or a peace offering, they must be without blemish. The blind, injured and maimed are not acceptable, and the chapter gives a list of defects. There are also rules to protect newborn animals and their mothers. As so often in this section, the last word is the Lord's own: I am the Lord who sanctifies you, who brought you out of the land of Egypt to be your God.

=== 23
The laws for the priests and their offerings are complete. Now the Lord sets out Israel's sacred calendar, the appointed feasts that will shape the rhythm of their year. The chapter begins with the weekly Sabbath, and then follows the seasons, from spring to autumn.

In the first month, in the spring, come the Passover and the seven-day Feast of Unleavened Bread, recalling the night they left Egypt. Then comes a ceremony for the land they have not yet reached: when they reap their first harvest, they are to bring the first sheaf of grain to the priest, and fifty days later they are to celebrate the end of the grain harvest with an offering of new bread. This is the feast elsewhere called the Feast of Weeks, which in Greek would be known as Pentecost.

The seventh month, in the autumn, is the most crowded of the year. It opens with a day announced by trumpet blasts. On the tenth day comes the Day of Atonement, a day for the people to humble themselves and rest completely. From the fifteenth day comes the Feast of Tabernacles, a week of rejoicing at the end of the harvest, when the Israelites are to live in shelters made of branches, so that each generation will remember that their ancestors lived in temporary shelters after God brought them out of Egypt.

=== 24
The calendar of feasts has been set out. This chapter begins with the daily and weekly service inside the Holy Place. The Israelites are to bring pure olive oil, so that Aaron can keep the lamps on the golden lampstand burning before the Lord every night. And twelve loaves of bread, one for each tribe, are to be set out in two rows on the golden table every Sabbath, as a sign of the lasting covenant, and then eaten by the priests.

Then, quite suddenly, the book returns to narrative, for the second and last time. A fight breaks out in the camp between an Israelite and a man whose mother is an Israelite of the tribe of Dan but whose father is Egyptian. During the fight he blasphemes the name of the Lord, cursing it. Leviticus names his mother, Shelomith, but not him.

No one knows what should be done, so he is held in custody until the Lord makes his will clear. The answer comes as a law for everyone in Israel, native-born or foreigner, about blasphemy, and alongside it the laws about taking life and causing injury, including the principle of an eye for an eye. One standard of justice will apply to all.

=== 25
The story of the blasphemer is over, and the book returns to laws given, as this chapter carefully reminds us, on Mount Sinai. They look ahead to the land Israel will one day farm, and they extend the pattern of the Sabbath from days to years.

Every seventh year is to be a Sabbath year for the land. The fields are not to be sown nor the vineyards pruned, and whatever grows of itself may be eaten by everyone, including servants, foreigners and animals. Then, after seven of these cycles, the fiftieth year is to be the Year of Jubilee, announced with a ram's horn on the Day of Atonement. In that year land that has been sold returns to the family that first owned it, and Israelites who have sold themselves into service go free.

Behind these laws is a striking idea, which the chapter states directly. The land belongs to the Lord, and the Israelites live on it as foreigners and tenants. So land can never be sold permanently, only its harvests until the next Jubilee, and an Israelite who falls into poverty must be helped, lent to without interest, and never treated as a slave. Whether the Jubilee was ever fully practised in Israel's history is uncertain, but the prophets would remember its language of release.

=== 26
The laws given at Sinai are drawing to a close, and this chapter gathers them up with a choice. It was common in the ancient Near East for a treaty between a great king and his subjects to end with blessings for loyalty and curses for betrayal. Leviticus does the same, for the covenant between the Lord and Israel.

After a brief reminder of the commands at its heart, against idols and for the Sabbath and the sanctuary, the chapter opens with blessings. If Israel walks in God's ways, there will be rain in its season, harvests so large that the threshing lasts until the grape harvest, peace in the land and victory over enemies. Above all, God will make his dwelling among them. I will walk among you and be your God, and you will be my people.

Then come the warnings, and they are longer and darker. If Israel rejects the covenant, disaster will follow in rising waves, each introduced with the words if in spite of all this. The last stage is the loss of the land itself, with cities in ruins and the people scattered among the nations. Centuries later, the books of Chronicles would look back on the Babylonian exile as the time when the land at last enjoyed its Sabbaths, echoing this chapter. Yet even at the end the door is left open. If they confess their sin, God will remember his covenant with Jacob, Isaac and Abraham.

=== 27
The previous chapter ended with what sounds like a conclusion: these are the laws the Lord established between himself and Israel through Moses on Mount Sinai. But one more chapter follows, rather like an appendix, and it turns to a practical matter: what happens when an Israelite makes a vow, promising a person, an animal, a house or a field to the Lord.

In the ancient world people often made vows in times of need, promising a gift to God if he answered their prayer. This chapter sets out how such vows were to be valued and, in many cases, redeemed, bought back with silver. A person dedicated to the Lord was valued according to age and sex. Animals, houses and fields each had their rules, and the value of a field was tied to the number of years left until the next Jubilee. The poor were again given special consideration.

The chapter then deals with things that could not be vowed because they already belonged to the Lord, such as the firstborn of the flocks and a tenth of the harvest, the tithe. And Leviticus ends as it has run throughout, with a reminder of where these words were spoken: these are the commandments the Lord gave Moses for the Israelites on Mount Sinai. When the story resumes in Numbers, Israel will still be camped at the mountain, preparing to set out.
