=== intro
Zechariah is the longest of the Twelve, and in places the most mysterious. It begins in the same months as Haggai, in the second year of Darius king of Persia, five hundred and twenty before Christ, among the small community of returned exiles in Jerusalem. Judah was now a minor province of the Persian empire, little more than Jerusalem and the hill country around it, its people poor and its neighbours unfriendly. The temple foundation had been laid years before and then abandoned. Haggai had just roused the people to begin building again. Zechariah's first word came two months after Haggai's first, and the book of Ezra names the two prophets together as the men whose encouragement saw the work through.

Zechariah son of Berechiah, son of Iddo, was probably from a priestly family. A priest named Iddo is listed among those who returned from Babylon with Zerubbabel, and in the next generation a Zechariah is named as head of that family. His name means the Lord remembers.

The two prophets are very different. Haggai is brief and practical. Zechariah is a visionary. In a single night, a few months into the rebuilding, he sees a sequence of eight visions: horsemen among myrtle trees, horns and craftsmen, a man measuring Jerusalem, the high priest on trial, a golden lampstand, a flying scroll, a woman in a basket, and chariots going out to the four winds. An angel stands beside him to explain what he sees. The visions are full of symbols, and their style anticipates the later visions of Daniel and the book of Revelation. Through them all runs a single message to a struggling people: the Lord has not forgotten Jerusalem, he will dwell among them again, and the work they have begun will be finished, by his Spirit rather than by human strength.

The same leaders the listener met in Haggai stand at the centre: Zerubbabel the governor, of David's royal line, and Joshua the high priest. Zechariah gives both of them a place in God's purposes, and speaks of a coming figure he calls the Branch, a title already used by Isaiah and Jeremiah for a future king from David's family.

The book falls into two halves. After a call to return to the Lord, the first eight chapters contain the night visions, a symbolic crowning, and an answer to a question about fasting brought two years later. These are dated, and set firmly in the reign of Darius. The temple they speak of was completed in five hundred and fifteen before Christ, in his sixth year. The last six chapters are quite different. They are undated, they mention neither Zerubbabel nor Joshua nor the temple building, and they contain two long oracles about the future of Jerusalem and the nations, speaking of shepherds good and bad, of a king coming humbly to Zion, and of a final day when the Lord will be king over all the earth. Many scholars believe these chapters come from a later time, possibly from another hand, while others read them as Zechariah's own later work. The book does not say.

Zechariah casts a long shadow into the New Testament. The Gospel writers turned to these final chapters again and again as they told the last days of Jesus' life: the king riding on a donkey, thirty pieces of silver, the shepherd struck, the one who was pierced. Few books of the Old Testament are quoted so often in the passion narratives.

=== 1
The book opens in the autumn of Darius's second year. Haggai's call has been heeded, and building is under way. Zechariah's first message looks back. The Lord was angry with your fathers, it says, and the former prophets warned them in vain. Now the call is simple: return to me, and I will return to you.

Three months later, in the month of Shebat, comes the night of visions. In the first, Zechariah sees a man on a red horse among myrtle trees in a hollow, with other horses behind him, which have patrolled the earth and found it at peace. Darius had by then crushed the revolts of his first years, and the empire was quiet, yet Jerusalem still lay in ruins. The angel asks how long the Lord will withhold mercy from the city he has been angry with for seventy years, and the answer is comfort: he will return to Jerusalem, and his house will be built.

In the second, four horns, the nations that scattered God's people, are met by four craftsmen who will throw them down.

=== 2
In the third vision Zechariah sees a man with a measuring line, setting out to mark the width and length of Jerusalem. But an angel sends word that the city will be too full to be confined by walls. It will spread out like open villages, with crowds of people and livestock, and the Lord himself will be a wall of fire around it and the glory within it.

At the time Jerusalem was a thinly settled ruin, and its walls would not be rebuilt for another seventy years or so, under Nehemiah.

The rest of the chapter is a poem calling to the exiles who had remained in Babylon, the land of the north. Many had chosen to stay, settled and prosperous, and Zechariah urges them to escape and come home. Whoever touches God's people touches the apple of his eye. The chapter ends with a wider hope: many nations will join themselves to the Lord on that day, and he will dwell in Zion.

=== 3
The fourth vision takes place in a courtroom. Joshua the high priest stands before the angel of the Lord in filthy clothes, and beside him stands Satan, the adversary, ready to accuse him.

Joshua is not on trial for himself alone. As high priest he represents the whole people, and his stained garments seem to stand for the guilt of the nation. The accuser is rebuked. This man, the Lord says, is a burning stick snatched from the fire. Then the filthy clothes are removed, Joshua is dressed in fine robes, and at Zechariah's own interjection a clean turban is set on his head.

Joshua is charged to walk in the Lord's ways and govern his house. A promise reaches further: the Lord will bring his servant, the Branch. A stone with seven eyes is set before Joshua, and the vision closes with the old picture of peace, everyone under their own vine and fig tree.

=== 4
For the fifth vision, the angel wakes Zechariah as if from sleep. He sees a solid gold lampstand with seven lamps, fed with oil from a bowl above it, and an olive tree on either side.

The meaning comes in a word to Zerubbabel the governor, who was responsible for the rebuilding: not by might nor by power, but by my Spirit, says the Lord of Hosts. The mountain before him will become a plain. His hands laid the foundation of this house, and his hands will complete it.

The returned exiles were few and poor, and the new building looked modest beside memories of Solomon's temple. So the Lord asks who has despised the day of small things, for his eyes will rejoice to see the plumb line in Zerubbabel's hand.

At the end, the prophet asks again about the olive trees. They are the two anointed ones who stand beside the Lord of all the earth, most likely Zerubbabel and Joshua, the royal and the priestly leaders of the people.

=== 5
The sixth and seventh visions both concern the purging of the land. First Zechariah sees an enormous scroll flying through the air, twenty cubits long and ten wide, about thirty feet by fifteen. It is a curse going out over the land, against those who steal and those who swear falsely by the Lord's name, and it will enter their houses and consume them.

Then comes one of the strangest images in the book. The angel shows him a measuring basket for grain with a heavy lead cover. When the lid is lifted, a woman is sitting inside, and the angel names her Wickedness. She is forced back in, and two women with wings like a stork's carry the basket away.

They are taking it to Shinar, the old name for the land of Babylon, where the tower of Babel was built. Wickedness is to be removed from Judah altogether, and sent back to the place from which God's people have just come home.

=== 6
The eighth vision, which echoes the first, brings the night to a close. Zechariah sees four chariots coming out from between two mountains of bronze, drawn by red, black, white and dappled horses. These are the four spirits of heaven, sent out to patrol the earth, and those that go north, towards Babylon, have given rest to the Lord's Spirit there.

Then the visions end, and the prophet is told to perform a symbolic act. Three men, Heldai, Tobijah and Jedaiah, have arrived from Babylon with silver and gold. From it Zechariah is to make a crown and set it on the head of Joshua the high priest.

The words that accompany the crowning look beyond Joshua himself. Here is a man whose name is the Branch, who will build the temple of the Lord. He will sit on his throne and rule, and he will be a priest on his throne. The crown is to be kept in the temple as a memorial, and those far away will come and help to build the house of the Lord.

=== 7
Almost two years have passed since the night of visions. It is the fourth year of Darius, late in five hundred and eighteen before Christ, and the temple is still being built.

A delegation arrives from Bethel, north of Jerusalem, led by Sharezer and Regem-melech, with a practical question for the priests and prophets. Since the destruction of Jerusalem, the people had mourned every year in the fifth month, the month in which Nebuchadnezzar's commander burned the temple. With a new temple rising, should they go on fasting?

The Lord's answer turns the question back on them, asking whether all those years of fasting in the fifth and seventh months were really for him. The second fast probably remembered the murder of Gedaliah, the governor left in Judah after the city fell. Then Zechariah recalls what the earlier prophets had demanded: true justice, mercy and compassion, and no oppression of the widow, the fatherless, the foreigner or the poor. Their fathers refused to listen, and so the land was left desolate.

=== 8
The answer to Bethel continues in a series of short sayings, each beginning, this is what the Lord of Hosts says, and together they paint Jerusalem restored.

The Lord is jealous for Zion and will return to dwell there. Old men and women will sit again in the streets, leaning on their staffs, and the squares will be full of boys and girls playing. It may seem impossible to the little remnant, but not to him. Those who heard the prophets when the foundation was laid are told to let their hands be strong.

The direct reply to the delegation follows. The fasts of the fourth, fifth, seventh and tenth months, which marked the stages of Jerusalem's fall, will become festivals of joy. The final sayings picture the nations coming to seek the Lord, ten men from every language taking hold of a Jew's robe, saying, let us go with you, for we have heard that God is with you.

=== 9
Here the book changes character. The dated messages, the visions and the interpreting angel are gone. Instead come two long oracles, each headed a burden, which fill the rest of the book.

The first oracle opens with judgement moving through the lands around Israel: Hadrach and Damascus in Syria, Hamath, the rich Phoenician cities of Tyre and Sidon, and the Philistine cities along the coast. Some interpreters connect this line of march with Alexander the Great's campaign two centuries after Zechariah, but the text names no conqueror.

Next is the passage for which the chapter is best known. Rejoice greatly, Daughter of Zion, your king comes to you, righteous and victorious, humble and riding on a donkey. He will speak peace to the nations, and his rule will reach from sea to sea. All four Gospels describe Jesus entering Jerusalem this way, and Matthew and John quote the verse. Its last verses set prisoners of hope free and rouse Zion's sons against the sons of Greece.

=== 10
The oracle continues with an invitation and a warning. The people are to ask the Lord for rain in spring, for he sends the showers, not the idols and diviners with their empty comfort. Because of them the people wander like sheep without a shepherd.

Shepherds were a common picture for rulers in the ancient world, and from here to the end of this oracle they are a central theme. The Lord is angry with the shepherds who have failed his flock, and he will care for it himself. From Judah will come the cornerstone, the tent peg and the battle bow, strong leaders for the people.

The promise reaches beyond Judah to the house of Joseph, the long-lost northern tribes. The Lord will whistle for them and gather them from distant lands. They will return from Egypt and Assyria, names that by now stood for the lands of the old captivity, and they will fill Gilead and Lebanon until there is no room for them. It is a picture of all Israel reunited, as Hosea and Ezekiel had foreseen.

=== 11
This is among the hardest chapters in the Old Testament to interpret. It opens with a lament over the fallen cedars of Lebanon and oaks of Bashan, and shepherds wailing over their ruined glory.

Then the prophet is given a role to act out. He is to shepherd a flock marked for slaughter, a people exploited by those who buy and sell them. He takes two staffs, Favour and Union, and in one month dismisses three shepherds, whose identity is an old puzzle. But the flock detests him, and he gives them up. He breaks the first staff, and for his wages they weigh out thirty pieces of silver, the sum the law of Moses set as compensation for a slave. The Lord tells him to throw this magnificent price to the potter in the house of the Lord. Matthew's Gospel sees this fulfilled when Judas's thirty pieces of silver bought a potter's field.

The second staff is then broken, ending the brotherhood between Judah and Israel, and the chapter closes with a woe on a worthless shepherd who abandons the flock.

=== 12
The second oracle begins here and carries the book to its end. Its subject is Jerusalem on a coming day, and the phrase on that day sounds again and again.

All the nations of the earth gather against Jerusalem, but the city becomes a cup that sends them reeling, and a heavy stone that injures anyone who tries to lift it. Even the weakest in Jerusalem will be like David.

Then the tone changes from victory to grief. The Lord will pour out on the house of David and the people of Jerusalem a spirit of grace and prayer, and they will look on me, he says, the one they have pierced, and mourn as for an only child. It is compared to the wailing at Hadad-rimmon in the plain of Megiddo, probably a memory of the national grief for King Josiah, who fell in battle there. Clan by clan the land mourns, royal and priestly families alike. John's Gospel recalls the words about the pierced one at the crucifixion.

=== 13
The mourning leads directly to cleansing. On that day a fountain will be opened for the house of David and the people of Jerusalem, to wash away sin and impurity.

Idolatry will be wiped from the land, and false prophecy with it. Prophecy will have fallen into such disrepute that a man's own parents will turn on him if he claims to speak for the Lord, and false prophets will give up the hairy cloak that marked them and claim to be farmers.

Then, abruptly, comes a short poem. The Lord calls for the sword to awake against his shepherd, the man who stands close to him. Strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered. Jesus quoted those words on the night of his arrest, as he warned his disciples that they would desert him. The poem goes on to a people tested by fire, a third refined like silver and gold, until they call on the Lord and he answers them.

=== 14
The final chapter describes a day of the Lord still to come. It begins with disaster: the nations gather against Jerusalem, and the city is captured. Then the Lord himself goes out to fight.

His feet stand on the Mount of Olives, east of the city, and the mountain splits in two, opening a valley of escape. The people will flee as they fled from the earthquake in the days of Uzziah king of Judah, the same earthquake that dates the preaching of Amos, more than two centuries earlier.

What follows is a world transformed. Living water will flow out from Jerusalem, east to the Dead Sea and west to the Mediterranean, and the Lord will be king over all the earth. Survivors from the nations will go up to Jerusalem year by year to celebrate the Feast of Tabernacles. And everything will be holy. The words once engraved on the high priest's turban, Holy to the Lord, will be written on the bells of the horses, and every cooking pot in Jerusalem will be as sacred as the vessels of the altar.
