=== intro
The listener who has come through Samuel and Kings has already walked with David and Solomon, watched the kingdom divide, and seen Jerusalem burn. Chronicles goes back over that same ground. It is not a sequel but a second telling, written for a different generation and from a different vantage point, and the difference in vantage point is the key to the whole work.

Kings was shaped for people living in the shadow of the catastrophe, explaining how a nation that had God's promises could end in ruin. Chronicles comes from the far side of the exile. Babylon has fallen to the Persians, and the Persian king Cyrus has allowed the deported people of Judah to go home. Some have returned. They have rebuilt the temple in Jerusalem, a modest building compared with Solomon's, and they live in a small province of a vast empire, with a governor appointed by a foreign king and no son of David on any throne. They have their lives back, but they live with questions: whether they are still the people of God, whether his promises to David still stand, why the temple matters and who should serve in it, and where they belong.

Chronicles was originally a single book in Hebrew, divided into two only later, and its Hebrew title means something like the events of the days. The author does not give his name. Jewish tradition gives the work to Ezra the scribe, and the last lines of Second Chronicles are repeated almost word for word at the start of the book of Ezra. He writes in the Persian period, with a deep love for the temple and its Levites. He draws openly on older writings, naming royal annals and the records of prophets, and he clearly knew much of the material we have in Samuel and Kings.

He begins in a way no other history book in the Bible does: with Adam. The first nine chapters are almost entirely names, tracing the generations from the beginning of humanity through Abraham and Jacob to the tribes of Israel, with special care for the royal line of Judah, the priestly line of Levi and the tribe of Benjamin. To a modern listener this can feel like a long corridor before the doors open. To the returned exiles it was a deed of belonging. It told them that they were not a broken remnant who had lost their past, but the living continuation of a family that went all the way back to the first man, and it ends by listing those who settled again in Jerusalem after the exile.

Then the story proper begins, with the death of Saul on Mount Gilboa, told in a single chapter. Almost everything else in First Chronicles belongs to David. Here the comparison with Samuel is revealing. Chronicles passes over David's years as a fugitive, his rivalry with the house of Saul, his adultery with Bathsheba and the murder of Uriah, and the revolt of Absalom that tore his family apart. The writer is not pretending those things never happened; his readers knew them well. He chooses to concentrate on two things: the kingdom God established through David, and the worship David organised around the ark of the covenant.

So the chapters that follow linger where Samuel moved quickly. David captures Jerusalem and gathers his mighty men. All Israel comes to make him king. The ark is brought up to the city, first disastrously and then with great joy, and the Levites are appointed as singers and musicians. God promises David a house, a dynasty that will last for ever, while telling him that his son, not he, will build the temple. There are wars, and there is a census that brings a plague, and the plague stops at a threshing floor that becomes the site of the temple. The last third of the book describes David preparing for a building he will never see: gathering materials, organising the priests, Levites, singers and gatekeepers into their divisions, and finally handing the kingdom and the plans to Solomon.

Running through the book is the conviction that Israel is one people. The writer loves the phrase all Israel, and he wants every tribe included, north as well as south. He also writes as someone who knows that the throne is empty, yet he keeps the promise to David at the very centre of his story. For a people with a temple but no king, that promise was both a memory and a hope.

The pace is unhurried, and the lists return in the later chapters, but every list is doing work. By the end of First Chronicles David has died full of years, and Solomon sits on the throne of the Lord. Second Chronicles will carry the story from the building of the temple to its destruction, and on to the decree of Cyrus that brought the exiles home.

=== 1
The book opens with a name and no introduction at all: Adam. In a few lines the generations run from Adam to Noah and his three sons, Shem, Ham and Japheth, drawn from the family trees of Genesis.

From there the chapter follows the nations, much as the Table of Nations did, so that Egypt and Canaan, the Philistines and the Arameans all find a place in one human family before Israel's own story is told. Then the line narrows through Shem to Abram, who is Abraham.

Abraham's other sons come first, Ishmael and the sons of Keturah, before the line passes to Isaac, whose sons are Esau and Israel. Chronicles almost always calls Jacob by the name Israel. Esau's descendants and the early kings of Edom close the chapter. This is the writer's habit throughout: he deals with the side branches first, and then returns to the main line.

=== 2
With the wider world accounted for, the writer names the twelve sons of Israel and turns at once to Judah. Judah was not the eldest, but he comes first, because the royal line of David descends from him.

The record does not hide the family's troubles. It mentions Judah's Canaanite wife, his firstborn Er, whom the Lord put to death, and Tamar, who bore him Perez and Zerah. It names Achar, the man the book of Joshua calls Achan, who brought trouble on Israel by taking what had been devoted to the Lord. Then the line runs through Boaz to Jesse, and Jesse's seventh son is David. His sisters' sons are there too: Abishai, Joab and Asahel, the sons of Zeruiah, and Amasa, the son of Abigail, names that will run all through the story of his reign.

The rest of the chapter maps the great clans of Judah, and small stories surface among the names: Bezalel, the craftsman of the tabernacle, and Sheshan, who had no sons and gave his daughter in marriage to his Egyptian servant Jarha. Many of the names are also towns, Bethlehem, Kiriath-jearim, Ziph and Hebron, so the list is a map of the hill country as well as a family tree. For returned exiles settling those same hills, these names were home.

=== 3
The genealogy of Judah now pauses on its most important family, the house of David. First come the six sons born to him in Hebron, then the sons born in Jerusalem, four of them by Bathsheba, Solomon among them.

From Solomon the line runs as a single thread through the kings of Judah, father to son, the names the listener met in Kings, down to Josiah's sons and to Jeconiah, called here the captive, who was taken to Babylon.

What makes the chapter remarkable is that it does not stop there. It continues through the exile to Zerubbabel, the descendant of David who led the first exiles home and oversaw the rebuilding of the temple, and on for several generations after him. For people with no king, this list said that David's family had survived. The line God had promised to keep was still alive, and its members could be named.

=== 4
After the house of David, the writer returns to the wider clans of Judah, gathering up more families, places and trades: craftsmen in a valley named for them, linen workers, and potters in the king's service.

In the middle of the names, the list stops for a man named Jabez. His mother named him with a word that sounds like pain, and he prays that God will bless him and keep him from harm, and God grants his request. It is a brief moment, but it shows a habit of this writer. His genealogies record not only births but people who called on God and were heard.

The second half of the chapter turns to Simeon, the small tribe whose towns around Beersheba lay within Judah's territory in the far south. The writer notes that Simeon never grew as large as Judah, and records how some of its clans, in the days of King Hezekiah, moved out to find new pasture.

=== 5
The genealogies now cross the Jordan to the tribes who settled on its eastern side, in Gilead and Bashan: Reuben, Gad and the half-tribe of Manasseh.

Reuben was Jacob's firstborn, and the chapter explains in a few sentences why his line does not lead the family. He lost the birthright by sleeping with his father's concubine. The birthright passed to the sons of Joseph, while leadership came through Judah, from whom a ruler arose.

The eastern tribes win battles against desert peoples called the Hagrites, and the writer notes that they won because they cried out to God and trusted him. But the chapter ends with the opposite. They were unfaithful to the God of their fathers, and the Lord stirred up the Assyrian king Tiglath-pileser, also called Pul, to carry them into exile. Even in a list of names, Chronicles is telling the story of faithfulness and exile that runs through the whole work.

=== 6
At the centre of the tribes stands Levi, the tribe set apart for the service of God, and this is one of the longest chapters in the genealogies. That alone shows where the writer's heart lies.

It begins with the high priestly line, from Aaron through Eleazar and Phinehas, down through Zadok, who served under David and Solomon, to Jehozadak, who went into captivity under Nebuchadnezzar. The first high priest after the return, Jeshua, was Jehozadak's son, so the list joined the priesthood of the writer's own day to Aaron himself.

Then come the three Levite clans, descended from Gershom, Kohath and Merari, and among them the singers David appointed once the ark had come to rest: Heman, Asaph and Ethan. Heman is a grandson of the prophet Samuel, and Asaph's name stands over a number of the psalms. Last come the towns given to the priests and Levites throughout the tribes, so that those who served the sanctuary lived among all Israel.

=== 7
This chapter moves quickly through the northern tribes: Issachar, Benjamin, Naphtali, Manasseh, Ephraim and Asher. Many entries read like military rolls, counting the fighting men of each clan, and some are dated to the days of David.

By the time Chronicles was written, the northern tribes had long been scattered by Assyria, and the returned community came largely from Judah, Benjamin and Levi. Yet the writer insists on including the north. For him Israel is all twelve tribes. Only Zebulun and Dan receive no lists of their own.

A few human details break through. Zelophehad has only daughters, the women whose right to inherit Moses upheld. Ephraim mourns for many days over two sons killed by the men of Gath when they went down to take their livestock, and he names the son born afterwards Beriah, because tragedy had come upon his house. His daughter Sheerah builds the towns of Lower and Upper Beth-horon. And Ephraim's line leads at last to Nun and his son Joshua, who brought Israel into the land.

=== 8
Benjamin has already appeared briefly, but now it receives a chapter of its own. This small tribe lay just north of Judah, around Jerusalem, and stayed bound to Judah after the kingdom divided. Benjamites returned from exile alongside the people of Judah, so their record mattered.

The list moves through clans living at Geba, Aijalon, Ono and Lod, and some in Jerusalem itself. Then it reaches the family that makes Benjamin important to the story: the house of Saul. Kish is the father of Saul, and Saul the father of Jonathan and his brothers. The names Esh-baal and Merib-baal belong to the men Second Samuel calls Ish-bosheth and Mephibosheth.

Jonathan's descendants are traced for many generations. The house of Saul lost the throne, but it did not disappear.

=== 9
The genealogies reach their goal. The chapter opens with a summary: all Israel was recorded, but Judah was carried into exile in Babylon because of their unfaithfulness. In one sentence the writer passes over the catastrophe, and turns at once to those who came back.

What follows is a list of the first people to resettle their towns and to live in Jerusalem after the exile: families from Judah, Benjamin, Ephraim and Manasseh, then priests and Levites. A similar list appears in the book of Nehemiah. The writer gives special care to the gatekeepers who guarded the house of God day and night, and to the Levites who kept its treasuries, counted its vessels, baked its bread and led its music, while sons of the priests mixed its spices. Their duties go back to David and Samuel the seer.

The worship of the returned community stands in an unbroken line with the past. Finally, the family of Saul is listed again, almost exactly as before. It is a bridge, because the story itself is about to begin with Saul's death.

=== 10
After nine chapters of names, the narrative begins, and it begins with an ending. The Philistines have pressed into the north of the land, and on Mount Gilboa, above the Jezreel Valley, the army of Israel breaks and runs.

The listener has heard this story at the close of First Samuel, and Chronicles tells it in much the same words: the death of Jonathan and his brothers, Saul wounded by archers, the armour-bearer who will not strike, the king falling on his own sword. The Philistines hang Saul's head in the temple of Dagon, and the men of Jabesh-gilead, whom Saul had once rescued, recover the bodies and bury them.

This is all Chronicles gives of Saul's reign. There is no account of his anointing, his early victories or his long pursuit of David. What it adds instead is a verdict, given in the writer's own voice. Saul died because he was unfaithful to the Lord, because he did not keep the Lord's word, and because he consulted a medium rather than inquiring of the Lord. So the Lord put him to death and turned the kingdom over to David son of Jesse. With that sentence the book's real subject comes into view.

=== 11
Saul is dead, and the story moves straight to David being made king over all Israel. Chronicles passes over the seven years in which David reigned at Hebron over Judah alone, while Saul's son Ish-bosheth held the loyalty of the north. Here the elders of all Israel come to Hebron together, recall that David led them in battle even in Saul's day, and anoint him, as the Lord had promised through Samuel.

David's first act as king of the whole nation is to march on Jerusalem, still a Jebusite stronghold called Jebus, standing on its ridge between the territories of Judah and Benjamin. The fortress of Zion falls, and it becomes the City of David. Chronicles adds a detail that Samuel does not give: Joab wins his place as chief commander by being the first to strike.

Most of the chapter is given to David's mighty men, the warriors whose loyalty made his kingdom possible. Some of their exploits are told: the three who broke through Philistine lines to bring David water from the well at Bethlehem, water he would not drink but poured out to the Lord, and Benaiah, who killed a lion in a pit on a snowy day. The roll that follows includes a name the listener will remember with a pang, Uriah the Hittite.

=== 12
The previous chapter listed David's mighty men. This one steps back in time to show how support gathered around him even while Saul was still alive. It opens at Ziklag, the Philistine town where David lived as an outlaw in the last years of Saul's reign.

Men came to him from every direction. There were Benjamites, Saul's own kinsmen, skilled with bow and sling in either hand. There were Gadites from across the Jordan, swift and fierce, who crossed the river in flood to join him. Men of Judah and Benjamin came to his stronghold, and when David asked whether they came in peace, the Spirit came upon one of their leaders, Amasai, who answered with a short poem of loyalty. Men from Manasseh came over to him when the Philistines sent him away from their last battle against Saul.

The chapter then returns to Hebron, and to a great roll of the tribes who came armed to make David king, every tribe named, including some barely mentioned in the genealogies. Issachar is remembered for men who understood the times. It all ends in three days of feasting, with food carried in on donkeys, camels, mules and oxen from as far away as Issachar, Zebulun and Naphtali, and joy in Israel.

=== 13
David is king and Jerusalem is his. Now his first concern is the ark of the covenant. Samuel told of David's battles with the Philistines before the ark was moved; Chronicles brings the ark forward, as if to say that this was the first thing on the new king's heart. In the days of Saul, David says, we did not inquire of him.

The ark has spent long years at Kiriath-jearim, in the house of Abinadab, ever since the Philistines sent it back in the days of Samuel. David consults his commanders and the whole assembly, and calls together all Israel, from the border of Egypt to the far north, to bring it home.

The procession sets out with music and great celebration, the ark riding on a new cart guided by Uzzah and Ahio. But at a threshing floor along the way the oxen stumble, and what happens next turns the celebration to fear. David is left angry and afraid, unsure how the ark can ever come to him, and it is left instead in the house of Obed-edom the Gittite. For three months the ark stays there, and the Lord blesses his household.

=== 14
The ark is resting in the house of Obed-edom, and the story turns to show how God was establishing David's kingdom. Hiram, king of the Phoenician city of Tyre on the coast to the north, sends cedar, masons and carpenters to build David a palace. It is the first sign of international recognition, and David understands that the Lord has exalted his kingdom for the sake of his people.

His family grows in Jerusalem, and the sons born there are named, Solomon among them. Then come the Philistines. Having heard that David has been anointed king over all Israel, they come looking for him, raiding the Valley of Rephaim just south-west of the city.

Twice they come, and twice David inquires of God before he moves. Unlike Saul, who did not inquire of the Lord, David asks and waits for an answer, and the answer the second time is unusual, a signal to be heard in the tops of the balsam trees. The Philistines are driven back, the idols they abandon on the field are burned, and David's fame spreads among the nations.

=== 15
Three months have passed since the death of Uzzah, and David has learned from it. He has built houses for himself in the City of David and pitched a tent to receive the ark. Now he states plainly what went wrong the first time: no one but the Levites may carry the ark, because the Lord chose them for it. The ark was never meant to ride on a cart. The law of Moses had commanded that it be carried on poles on the shoulders of the Levites, and on the first attempt, David says, we did not seek him in the proper way.

So David gathers the priests Zadok and Abiathar and the heads of the Levite clans, and the listener hears again the names of Heman, Asaph and Ethan, appointed as singers with cymbals, harps and lyres. Chronicles cares about this kind of detail because the worship of its own day traced its roots to this moment.

Then the procession sets out once more from the house of Obed-edom, with shouting and rams' horns, David in a linen robe. And at a window, looking down on the king as he dances, stands Saul's daughter Michal, David's wife.

=== 16
The ark has arrived in Jerusalem. It is set inside the tent David has pitched for it, offerings are made, and the king blesses the people and gives every man and woman bread and cakes of dates and raisins to take home.

Then David appoints Levites to serve before the ark continually, with Asaph at their head, to give thanks and praise. At the heart of the chapter stands a song of thanksgiving. Much of it will sound familiar, because it is drawn from three psalms that also appear in the book of Psalms, the hundred and fifth, the ninety-sixth and the hundred and sixth. It recalls God's covenant with Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, calls all the earth to praise the Lord, and ends with a prayer that God would gather and deliver his people from among the nations. To a community that had only recently come back from exile, those words had a sharp edge.

The chapter closes with an arrangement that will last until Solomon's temple is built. The ark is in Jerusalem, with Asaph and his associates. But the old tabernacle made in the wilderness stands at Gibeon, a few miles to the north-west, and there Zadok and the priests offer the daily sacrifices, with Heman and Jeduthun leading the music.

=== 17
David has settled into his palace, and the ark is under a tent. The contrast troubles him, and he tells the prophet Nathan that he wants to build a house for God. Nathan's first answer is to go ahead, but that night the word of God comes to him with a different message.

This chapter follows closely the account the listener heard in Second Samuel, and it is the heart of First Chronicles. God plays on the word house. David will not build God a house; instead God will build a house for David, a dynasty. One of David's sons will build the temple, and God will be his father, and he will be God's son, and his throne will be established for ever. In Samuel the promise speaks of the king's own kingdom; here God speaks of setting him over my house and my kingdom, a small change that stresses that the throne of David is the throne of God's kingdom. And where Samuel warns that God will discipline this son when he does wrong, Chronicles leaves the warning out.

The rest of the chapter is David's response, a prayer offered as he sits before the Lord. For readers who had no king, this promise was the reason they still looked for one.

=== 18
God has promised David a house. Now Chronicles gathers the victories by which the kingdom was secured, much as Second Samuel does, in a brisk summary of campaigns in every direction.

To the west David subdues the Philistines and takes Gath. To the east Moab becomes subject and pays tribute. To the north he defeats Hadadezer, king of the Aramean state of Zobah, and the Arameans of Damascus who come to help him, placing garrisons in their land. The king of Hamath, farther north still, sends his son with gifts. To the south Edom is subdued in the Valley of Salt. Twice the refrain sounds: the Lord made David victorious wherever he went.

Chronicles adds a note that tells you what it cares about. The bronze David took from Hadadezer's cities was later used by Solomon to make the great bronze basin, the pillars and the bronze articles of the temple. The plunder of David's wars is being set aside for the house of God. A list of David's officials follows: Joab over the army, Zadok and Ahimelech as priests, and Benaiah over the royal guard.

=== 19
David's wars continue, now against the Ammonites, the people east of the Jordan whose capital was Rabbah, on the site of modern Amman. The trouble begins with an act of kindness. Nahash, king of the Ammonites, has died, and David sends envoys to comfort his son Hanun, because Nahash had once been kind to him.

Hanun's advisers persuade him that the envoys are spies. He shaves their beards and cuts their garments short, a deep public humiliation, and sends them home. Knowing that this means war, the Ammonites hire chariots and soldiers from the Aramean kingdoms to the north.

The account closely follows Second Samuel. Joab finds himself caught between the Ammonites at the city gate and the Arameans in the open country, and he divides the army with his brother Abishai, each ready to come to the other's rescue. Before the fighting begins he speaks a few plain words of courage, and leaves the outcome to the Lord. The Arameans then summon reinforcements from beyond the Euphrates, and David himself takes the field.

=== 20
The war with Ammon is not over. In the spring, the season when kings go out to war, Joab leads the army against Rabbah and lays siege to it, while David remains in Jerusalem.

The listener who remembers Second Samuel knows what happened during that siege. It was then that David saw Bathsheba from the roof of his palace, and Uriah the Hittite was sent to his death at the walls of Rabbah. Chronicles moves straight from the siege to the city's fall, with David taking the Ammonite king's heavy crown, and says nothing of what took place in between. The writer's readers knew the story well; his subject is different.

Then come three brief episodes from the Philistine wars, in which David's men kill giant warriors descended from Rapha, among them a brother of Goliath, and a man with six fingers on each hand and six toes on each foot. Second Samuel tells them near its end; in Chronicles they close the account of David's wars.

=== 21
The wars are over, and the kingdom is secure. Now comes the one sin of David that Chronicles tells in full, because of where it leads. Satan rises against Israel and incites David to count the people. Second Samuel tells the same story but says that the anger of the Lord incited David; Chronicles names Satan as the adversary who stood against Israel. Even Joab, no stranger to hard decisions, protests that the census will bring guilt on Israel.

The census is taken, and God is displeased. The prophet Gad brings David a choice of three judgements, and David chooses to fall into the hand of the Lord, because his mercies are great. A plague sweeps the land, and an angel with a drawn sword stands over Jerusalem itself.

The angel is halted at a threshing floor belonging to Ornan the Jebusite, the man Samuel calls Araunah. Ornan is threshing wheat with his sons when David comes to buy the site. The chapter ends with an altar on that threshing floor, and with an explanation of why David could not go to the tabernacle at Gibeon. The place where the plague stopped is about to become the most important ground in Israel.

=== 22
God answered David with fire on the altar at Ornan's threshing floor, and David now declares that this is the place. Here will be the house of the Lord God, and the altar of burnt offering for Israel. Second Chronicles will later name the site Mount Moriah, the name of the land where Abraham was once sent to offer Isaac.

From here to the end of the book, Chronicles tells a story that Samuel and Kings hardly mention: David's preparations for a temple he will not build. He sets foreigners in the land to work as stonecutters, and gathers iron for nails, bronze beyond weighing and cedar from the Phoenician cities of Tyre and Sidon. Solomon, he says, is young and inexperienced, and the house must be magnificent, famous throughout all lands.

Then David calls Solomon and gives him his charge. He explains why he himself may not build: he has shed much blood in war. Solomon will be a man of rest, and his name, which sounds like the Hebrew word for peace, fits the reign God has promised. David hands over what he has gathered and tells his son to be strong and courageous, words the Lord once spoke to Joshua. Then he commands the leaders of Israel to help him.

=== 23
David is now old and full of years, and the book tells, in a single sentence, that he made Solomon king over Israel. The fuller story of the handover is told later, in chapter twenty-nine. First Chronicles pauses for five chapters to describe how David organised the people who would serve in the temple and the kingdom.

He begins with the Levites, counting the men of the tribe and dividing them by their work: those who will oversee the service of the house of the Lord, officials and judges, gatekeepers, and musicians. Then the families are listed according to the three sons of Levi, Gershon, Kohath and Merari. Aaron and his sons are set apart as priests, while the descendants of Moses are counted among the ordinary Levites.

David explains why their work must change. In the wilderness the Levites carried the tabernacle and its furnishings from place to place. Now the Lord has given his people rest and has come to dwell in Jerusalem, so the Levites will no longer carry the sanctuary. They will assist the priests in the courts and chambers, prepare the bread and offerings, and stand every morning and evening to give thanks and praise.

=== 24
The previous chapter organised the Levites. Now David turns to the priests, the descendants of Aaron, who alone could offer sacrifices at the altar. The chapter recalls that Aaron's two eldest sons, Nadab and Abihu, died in the wilderness without children, so the priesthood passed through his younger sons, Eleazar and Ithamar.

With the help of Zadok, of Eleazar's line, and Ahimelech, of Ithamar's, David divides the priestly families into twenty-four divisions. The order is decided by lot, so that neither line can claim precedence, and a scribe records the names in the presence of the king. Each division would serve in turn at the temple.

The arrangement lasted for centuries, and it was still in use in the time of the New Testament. The eighth lot falls to Abijah, and the Gospel of Luke tells that Zechariah, the father of John the Baptist, belonged to the division of Abijah and was serving his turn in the temple when the angel appeared to him. The chapter closes with the remaining Levite families, who also cast lots before the king.

=== 25
The priests have their divisions. Now come the musicians, and this chapter shows how seriously Chronicles takes the music of worship. David and the commanders of the army set apart the sons of Asaph, Heman and Jeduthun, the three great families of singers, to prophesy with lyres, harps and cymbals. Their music is described as a kind of prophecy, words of thanks and praise given by God.

Heman is called the king's seer, and he is said to have had fourteen sons and three daughters, a sign of God's favour. Asaph, Jeduthun and Heman serve under the direction of the king himself.

Like the priests, the musicians are divided into twenty-four groups of twelve, two hundred and eighty-eight trained singers in all, and they too cast lots for their order of service, young and old, teacher and pupil alike. Every name drawn stands for a family of singers. Centuries later, when the exiles came home from Babylon, the sons of Asaph were among them, still singing.

=== 26
After the musicians come the gatekeepers. These were Levites who guarded the entrances of the temple, opened its doors in the morning, and kept watch over its storehouses. Among them is Obed-edom, whose family has grown to sixty-two strong and capable men, the largest of the gatekeeping families, for God had blessed him. Lots are cast for the gates on each side, east, north, south and west.

Other Levites are given charge of the treasuries. These held the gifts dedicated to the house of God, including plunder from David's wars, and also offerings dedicated long before by Samuel the seer, Saul, Abner and Joab. It is a striking list of names. Men who were rivals and enemies in the story of Samuel all appear together here, as having given something toward the house of the Lord.

Finally there are Levites who served outside Jerusalem, as officials and judges in the affairs of God and the king, on both sides of the Jordan. The book thus shows the Levites present among all the tribes of Israel, east and west.

=== 27
With the temple personnel arranged, Chronicles turns to the kingdom itself. This chapter lists the military and civil officers of David's realm.

First come the twelve army divisions, each of twenty-four thousand men, serving in rotation one month each year. Most of their commanders are familiar from the list of mighty men in chapter eleven: Jashobeam, Benaiah son of Jehoiada, and Asahel the brother of Joab, who was killed early in David's reign, so that his son commands after him. Then come the leaders of the tribes, all Israel again, though Gad and Asher are not named.

The chapter recalls the census of chapter twenty-one, noting that Joab never finished it and its total was not entered in David's records, because wrath came upon Israel. It goes on to list the stewards of the king's property, his storehouses, vineyards, olive groves, herds, camels and donkeys, which shows how the royal estate was run.

Last come David's counsellors. Two of them, Ahithophel and Hushai, played decisive roles on opposite sides during Absalom's revolt, a story Chronicles does not tell. Their names here are a quiet reminder of events the listener knows from Second Samuel.

=== 28
The lists are finished, and the story resumes with a great assembly in Jerusalem. David has summoned all the leaders of Israel: the heads of the tribes, the army commanders, the stewards of the royal estates, the officials and the mighty men. The aged king rises to his feet and speaks.

He tells them what he told Solomon in chapter twenty-two, but now in public, before all Israel. It was in his heart to build a house for the ark, but God forbade it because he was a man of war. God chose Judah, then the house of Jesse, then David, and from David's many sons he has chosen Solomon to sit on the throne of the kingdom of the Lord. The promise is repeated, with an added condition: God will establish Solomon's kingdom for ever, if he holds firmly to God's commandments.

David then turns to his son, charging him to know and serve God with a whole heart and a willing mind. Finally, he gives Solomon the plans for the temple, its courts and rooms and furnishings, down to the weight of gold for each lampstand, all of which, David says, the Lord made clear to him in writing.

=== 29
The assembly is still gathered. David has handed over the plans, and now he speaks to the whole people once more. Solomon is young and the task is great, he says, because this palace is not for man but for the Lord God. David has given from the royal stores, and now he gives his own personal treasure, the gold of Ophir and refined silver. Then he asks who else is willing.

The leaders of the families and the tribes respond with gold, silver, bronze, iron and precious stones, and the people rejoice that their leaders have given so freely and wholeheartedly. King David rejoices with them.

David then offers one of the great prayers of the Old Testament, before all the assembly. After sacrifices and a feast, Solomon is anointed king a second time, with Zadok as priest. First Kings told of a tense and hurried succession, with a rival claim from Solomon's brother Adonijah; Chronicles shows a calm and willing handover, with all David's sons pledging loyalty to Solomon. The book ends with David's death after forty years as king, full of years, riches and honour, and with the sources where his deeds were recorded, the writings of Samuel, Nathan and Gad.
