=== intro
Jonah is unlike any other book among the prophets. The others are mostly collections of the prophets' messages. Jonah is a story about a prophet, and it contains only one short sentence of his preaching. Its attention is on Jonah himself: what God asked of him, what he did instead, and what God was teaching him.

Jonah son of Amittai is known from the second book of Kings. He came from Gath-hepher, a village in Galilee in the northern kingdom, and he foretold that Jeroboam son of Jehoash would restore Israel's borders, which he did. That places Jonah in the first half of the eighth century before Christ, a little before Amos and Hosea. He was a prophet whose earlier word had been good news for his own nation. The book about him does not name its author. It tells his story in the third person, plainly and with great economy.

The city at the centre of the story is Nineveh, on the east bank of the Tigris in northern Mesopotamia, opposite the modern city of Mosul in Iraq. It was one of the great cities of Assyria, and later the empire's capital. To the people of Israel, Assyria would become the most feared power on earth. Its kings boasted in their inscriptions of the cities they had burned and the captives they had tortured, and within a few decades of Jonah's time its armies would destroy the northern kingdom, Jonah's own country, and carry its people away. In Jonah's day Assyria was passing through a period of weakness, but anyone who heard this story later would know what Nineveh became.

So when the Lord tells Jonah to go to Nineveh and preach against it, and Jonah heads in the opposite direction, the listener can feel the weight of it. He goes down to the port of Joppa and buys passage to Tarshish, a distant place at the far western end of the Mediterranean. What follows is one of the best known stories in the Bible: a storm at sea, a crew of frightened sailors, a great fish, a city in sackcloth, and a prophet sitting outside it in a temper.

The story is told with care and a good deal of irony. The non-Israelites in it, first the sailors and then the people of Nineveh, respond to God more readily than his own prophet does. Words recur with purpose. Everything is great: the city, the wind, the storm, the fish, the sailors' fear, Jonah's anger. Again and again the Lord appoints something to do his will, a fish, a plant, a worm, a scorching wind, and all of them obey him. Only Jonah resists.

The book ends not with a statement but with a question from God to Jonah, and the reader never hears his reply. Later in the Twelve, the prophet Nahum would announce Nineveh's fall, which came in six hundred and twelve before Christ. And in the Gospels Jesus speaks of the sign of Jonah, and of the people of Nineveh who repented at Jonah's preaching.

=== 1
The story begins abruptly. The word of the Lord comes to Jonah: go to the great city of Nineveh and preach against it, for its wickedness has come up before me. Nineveh lies far to the east, across the desert, beyond the Euphrates. Jonah gets up, and goes west.

The narrator says it twice: he is fleeing from the presence of the Lord. And there is a quiet pattern in his movements. He goes down to Joppa on the coast, down into a ship bound for Tarshish, and down into its hold, where he falls fast asleep.

The Lord answers with a great wind. The sailors, each crying out to his own god, cast lots to find who has brought this on them, and the lot falls on Jonah. His answer to their questions is full of irony: he worships the God who made the sea and the dry land. Before the chapter ends these pagan sailors will be offering sacrifices to the Lord.

=== 2
Jonah has been thrown overboard and swallowed by the great fish that the Lord appointed. He is there three days and three nights. From inside the fish, he prays.

What he prays is a psalm of thanksgiving, many of its phrases echoing prayers Israel already knew from the Psalms. It looks back on the moment of drowning: the current swirling round him, the breakers sweeping over him, the seaweed wrapped around his head, the sense of sinking to the roots of the mountains, shut out from God's sight. Then he remembered the Lord, and his prayer rose to the holy temple, and the Lord brought his life up from the pit.

It gives thanks for a rescue already granted. Jonah thanks God for saving him while he is still inside the fish, which is itself the means of his deliverance. He ends with a vow to offer sacrifice, and a confession that salvation belongs to the Lord. Then, in a single sentence, the Lord commands the fish, and Jonah is on dry land.

=== 3
The chapter opens almost exactly as the book did. The word of the Lord comes to Jonah a second time, and the command is the same: go to Nineveh. This time he goes.

Nineveh is described as an exceedingly great city, a three days' journey. Jonah walks a day into the city and delivers his message. In the Hebrew it is just five words: forty more days and Nineveh will be overturned. There is no call to repent, and no offer of mercy.

The response is immediate and overwhelming. The people believe God and put on sackcloth, from the greatest to the least. Word reaches the king of Nineveh, who is not named, and he leaves his throne to sit in ashes, and issues a decree that even the animals must fast. His reasoning echoes the prophet Joel: who knows, God may turn and relent. And God, seeing what they did, relents from the disaster he had threatened.

=== 4
Nineveh has been spared, and the final chapter turns to Jonah's reaction. He is furious, and for the first time he explains why he ran away. He knew all along, he says, that the Lord is gracious and compassionate, slow to anger and abounding in loving devotion, a God who relents from sending disaster. These are the words God spoke of himself to Moses on Mount Sinai, and Jonah quotes them as a complaint. He would rather die than live.

The Lord answers with a question, and then with an object lesson. Jonah goes out east of the city, builds himself a shelter and sits down to watch what will happen to Nineveh. Once more the Lord appoints things to do his will: a leafy plant to shade Jonah's head, a worm, and a scorching east wind. The story's last word belongs to God, and it compares the prophet's feelings for a plant with the Lord's care for a great city and all who live in it, people and animals alike.
