=== intro
Ezekiel is the prophet of the exile. He never preaches in the streets of Jerusalem, as Isaiah and Jeremiah did. He speaks from a settlement of displaced people in the flat river country of Babylonia, hundreds of miles from home, to men and women who have lost their land, their king and their place before God, and who are about to lose much more.

To understand how he came to be there, we need to go back a few years. At the end of the seventh century before Christ, the old Assyrian empire collapsed, and Babylon, under its formidable king Nebuchadnezzar, became the master of the Near East. The little kingdom of Judah was caught between Babylon and Egypt, and its kings kept changing sides. In five hundred and ninety-seven before Christ, after one rebellion too many, Nebuchadnezzar besieged Jerusalem. The young king Jehoiachin surrendered, and he was carried off to Babylon with his household, his officials, the craftsmen and the leading families of the city. Among them was a young priest named Ezekiel, the son of Buzi.

The exiles were settled together in southern Mesopotamia, beside the Kebar, a canal drawn off the Euphrates, at a place called Tel-abib. They were not slaves in chains. They had houses, elders, and some freedom to gather. But their hearts were in Jerusalem. Nebuchadnezzar had placed Jehoiachin's uncle Zedekiah on the throne there, and many in exile, encouraged by prophets who told them what they wanted to hear, expected the whole thing to be over soon. The temple still stood. Surely God would never abandon his own house.

Ezekiel's task was to tell them otherwise. Five years into the exile, he saw the glory of the Lord, not in the temple, but on the banks of a foreign canal, and he was sent to a rebellious people as a watchman. Back in Jerusalem, Zedekiah was drifting towards rebellion against Babylon, counting on help from Egypt. In Jerusalem, Jeremiah was warning that the city would fall. In Babylonia, Ezekiel was saying the same thing, and he said it not only with words but with strange and costly actions: lying on his side for months beside a model of the besieged city, shaving his head, packing his bags like a refugee, falling silent for long periods. He was a priest, and his concerns are a priest's concerns: the holiness of God, the purity of worship, the temple and its defilement.

Unusually for the prophets, Ezekiel dates many of his oracles, by the years of Jehoiachin's exile. These dates run from the fifth year to the twenty-seventh, roughly five hundred and ninety-three to five hundred and seventy-one before Christ, and they act as signposts. They are not always in strict order, because the book is arranged by subject as well as by time, so it is worth listening for them. They also reveal the great divide in the middle of the book. In the ninth year, the Babylonian army laid siege to Jerusalem. In the eleventh, the city fell and the temple was burned. Some months later, a survivor reached the exiles with the news, and Ezekiel's message changed.

The book moves in a clear arc. The first twenty-four chapters, before the fall, are almost entirely warning. Ezekiel is carried in vision to Jerusalem, where he sees what is being done in the temple courts, and then watches the glory of the Lord leave the temple and the city. Chapters twenty-five to thirty-two turn to the nations around Judah, among them Ammon, Moab, Edom and the Philistines, the great trading city of Tyre, and above all Egypt. Then, once the news of Jerusalem's fall has come, the prophet becomes a voice of hope: a new shepherd for God's flock, a new heart and a new spirit for his people, a valley of dry bones that live again. The book closes with a long, detailed vision of a new temple, a restored land, and the glory of the Lord returning to dwell among his people.

Ezekiel can be a difficult book. Its visions are intense, its imagery sometimes startling, and some of its language about Jerusalem's unfaithfulness is meant to offend the ear. But it is held together by a single conviction, expressed in a refrain that runs through the whole book well over sixty times: then they will know that I am the Lord. The prophet is often addressed as son of man, a reminder of his frailty before the overwhelming majesty he has seen.

In the canon, Ezekiel stands beside Jeremiah and Lamentations, and his story overlaps with the final chapters of Second Kings. His younger contemporary Daniel, also in Babylon, is mentioned by name in Ezekiel's pages. The books of Ezra and Nehemiah, which tell of the exiles' return, lie a generation and more in the future.

=== 1
The book opens with a date and a place. It is the fifth year of King Jehoiachin's exile, around five hundred and ninety-three before Christ, and Ezekiel is among the exiles by the River Kebar. He also mentions a thirtieth year; many take this to be his own age, the age at which a priest would normally have begun to serve in the temple, though the text does not say so.

Instead of a temple, Ezekiel is given a vision. A storm cloud comes out of the north, full of fire and light, and within it are four living creatures, each with four faces and four wings, and beside them wheels that gleam like beryl and move in every direction at once. Above them is a crystal expanse, and above that a throne, and on the throne a figure like a man, surrounded by brilliant light like a rainbow.

Ezekiel describes all this with great care and great hesitation. He speaks of the likeness of the appearance, as if no words are quite adequate. The point he presses home is that the God of Israel is not confined to Jerusalem. His throne moves, and it has come to Babylon.

=== 2
Ezekiel lies face down on the ground before the vision of the glory of the Lord. Now the voice from the throne speaks to him for the first time, and it calls him by the title that will be used throughout the book: son of man. It means a mortal, a human being, and it sets the prophet in his place before the overwhelming majesty of God.

The Spirit sets Ezekiel on his feet, and he receives his commission. He is being sent to the Israelites, a nation described again and again as rebellious, obstinate and stubborn. He is not promised success. Whether they listen or refuse to listen, they will know that a prophet has been among them. He is told three times not to be afraid of them, though he will live as if among briers, thorns and scorpions.

The chapter ends with a hand stretched out towards him, holding a scroll. It is written on both sides, which was unusual, and its contents are lament, mourning and woe. Ezekiel is told to open his mouth and eat what he is given.

=== 3
The commission continues. Ezekiel eats the scroll, and to his surprise it tastes as sweet as honey, even though its message is one of grief. He is sent not to a foreign people with a difficult language, who might actually listen, but to his own nation, who will not. God promises to make his face and forehead as hard as theirs, harder than flint.

Then the Spirit lifts him up, the vision departs with a great rumbling, and Ezekiel is set down among the exiles at Tel-abib, by the Kebar. For seven days he sits among them, stunned and unable to speak.

At the end of that week, the word of the Lord comes again, and Ezekiel learns that he is to be a watchman, like a sentry on a city wall whose duty is to sound the alarm. The responsibility is grave, and it is spelled out in detail. The chapter closes with another encounter with the glory of the Lord on the plain, and a strange instruction: Ezekiel is to shut himself in his house, bound and silent, speaking only when God opens his mouth.

=== 4
Ezekiel has been commissioned and confined to his house. Now his ministry begins, not with a sermon but with a series of acted signs. In the prophets, such actions were a kind of street theatre, a message given in living pictures, and Ezekiel's are among the most demanding in the Bible.

He takes a clay brick, the common building material of Babylonia, and draws Jerusalem on it. Then he lays siege to it in miniature, with ramps, camps and battering rams, and sets an iron plate between himself and the city. Remember that Jerusalem is still standing at this point, with Zedekiah on its throne, and many of the exiles expect to be going home soon. Ezekiel is showing them a city under siege.

Then he is told to lie on his left side for three hundred and ninety days, bearing the iniquity of Israel, and on his right side for forty days, bearing the iniquity of Judah. Meanwhile he must eat a meagre ration of mixed grains, baked over a fire of dung, a picture of famine within the walls and of defiled food in exile. Ezekiel, a priest who has kept himself clean all his life, protests, and God makes a concession.

=== 5
The signs of the siege continue. Ezekiel is told to take a sharp sword and use it like a barber's razor on his own head and beard. For a priest this was a startling act, since the law forbade priests to shave their heads in this way, and in that world shaving was a sign of mourning and disgrace.

He weighs the hair on scales and divides it into three. One third he burns in the city he has drawn, one third he strikes with the sword around it, and one third he scatters to the wind. A few strands he tucks into the fold of his garment, though even some of these are thrown into the fire.

Then the meaning is given, plainly and at length. This is Jerusalem, set by God at the centre of the nations, and yet more rebellious than the nations around her, a city whose sanctuary has been defiled. What is coming upon her is plague and famine, the sword and scattering. The words are heavy, and the chapter ends with the repeated seal: I, the Lord, have spoken.

=== 6
The acted signs are over, and a new oracle begins. Ezekiel is told to set his face towards the mountains of Israel and prophesy against them. He is far away in Babylonia, but the prophets often turned to face the place they addressed.

The mountains are singled out because of what happened on them. On the hilltops and under green trees across the land stood the high places, local shrines with altars and incense stands and idols, where the God of Israel was worshipped alongside other gods, or replaced by them. Earlier kings, Hezekiah and above all Josiah, had tried to sweep these shrines away, but they kept returning.

The oracle announces that the sword will come to these sanctuaries, and the bodies of the slain will lie among the idols they served. Yet there is a glimpse of something more. A remnant will survive, scattered among the nations, and there they will remember. Through all of it runs the refrain that defines the book: then you will know that I am the Lord.

=== 7
After the mountains, the land itself. This chapter is a poem, one of the most urgent in the book, and its opening words strike like a bell: the end has come upon the four corners of the land.

The language is short, repetitive and breathless. The day is near. The time has come. Doom has gone out. The ordinary life of the land, buying and selling, is suddenly meaningless, because nothing will survive to be enjoyed. A trumpet is blown to rally the defence, but nobody goes out to fight. Silver and gold, once used to make idols, are thrown into the streets as worthless.

Running through the poem is the picture of a nation losing every source of guidance it relied on. People will look for a vision from a prophet, instruction from the priests, and counsel from the elders, and find none. The king will mourn, and the people will tremble. It is a fitting close to the first stage of Ezekiel's ministry, which began with the vision by the Kebar.

=== 8
A new date opens a new section. It is the sixth year of the exile, a little over a year since Ezekiel first saw the glory by the Kebar, and the elders of Judah in exile are sitting in his house. Perhaps they have come for news or guidance. What they witness is the prophet seized by the hand of the Lord.

In vision, Ezekiel is lifted by the hair of his head and carried to Jerusalem, to the temple where he once expected to serve. The chapters that follow, eight to eleven, form a single long vision, and it begins with a guided tour. At each stop his guide tells him that he will see still greater abominations.

At the north gate stands an idol that provokes the Lord to jealousy. Through a hole in a wall, Ezekiel finds seventy elders burning incense before images of creatures carved on the walls, among them Jaazaniah son of Shaphan, probably from a prominent family in the royal court. At another gate women are weeping for Tammuz, a Mesopotamian god whose death was mourned each year. And in the inner court, between the porch and the altar, about twenty-five men have turned their backs on the temple to bow down to the rising sun.

=== 9
The tour of the temple has ended in the inner court, with men bowing to the sun, and the Lord has declared that he will not spare. Now the vision turns to judgement, and it does so within the temple itself.

Six men appear, each holding a weapon, and with them a seventh, dressed in linen, the clothing of a priest, with a scribe's writing case at his side. They come and stand beside the bronze altar. At the same moment, something momentous happens almost in passing. The glory of the God of Israel rises from the cherubim above the ark, where it has rested in the inner sanctuary, and moves to the threshold of the temple. The departure has begun.

The man in linen is sent through Jerusalem to put a mark on the foreheads of those who grieve over what is being done in the city. Then the six follow him. The judgement begins at the sanctuary, with the elders standing before the temple. Ezekiel, left alone, falls on his face and pleads for what remains of Israel.

=== 10
The executioners have gone through the city, and the man in linen has reported that his task is done. Now he receives a new one. He is to go in among the wheels beneath the cherubim, fill his hands with burning coals, and scatter them over Jerusalem. It is a picture of the city's coming destruction by fire.

Much of the chapter returns to the throne-chariot Ezekiel saw at the beginning of the book: the creatures with four faces, the gleaming wheels, the eyes, the sound of wings like the voice of God Almighty. But now he sees them in the temple courts, and now he names them. The living creatures he saw by the Kebar are cherubim, the guardians of God's presence whose images were woven into the temple curtains and set above the ark.

Meanwhile, the movement of the glory continues, step by step. It rises from the threshold of the temple, and the brightness fills the court. Then it moves out and rests on the cherubim, and together they rise and stop at the east gate of the house of the Lord.

=== 11
The glory of the Lord now waits at the east gate of the temple. There Ezekiel sees twenty-five men, the leaders of the city, and two of them are named: Jaazaniah son of Azzur, not the man from chapter eight, and Pelatiah son of Benaiah.

These men are confident. They have a saying: the city is the cooking pot, and we are the meat. They seem to mean that they are the choice portion, safe within the walls, while the exiles were the scraps thrown away. Ezekiel turns their proverb against them, and as he prophesies, Pelatiah falls dead. Ezekiel again cries out, asking whether God will make a complete end of the remnant.

The answer is a turning point in the book. Those left in Jerusalem say that the exiles are far from the Lord and that the land is now theirs. But God has been a sanctuary for the exiles, and he will gather them, give them back the land, and put a new spirit within them, removing their heart of stone. Then the glory rises from the city and rests on the mountain to the east, the Mount of Olives, and Ezekiel is carried back to Babylonia.

=== 12
The long vision of the temple is over, and Ezekiel has told the exiles everything he saw. Now he is back among them, and he is given another acted sign, this time one that requires an audience.

In broad daylight, as his neighbours watch, he is to pack a bag for exile, the few belongings a deportee could carry, and set it outside. In the evening he digs through the wall of his house, which in Babylonia would have been made of mud brick, and goes out through the hole at dusk with the bag on his shoulder and his face covered. When the people ask what he is doing, he explains that it concerns the prince in Jerusalem, King Zedekiah, and all who remain there. The prince will try to escape by night, will be caught, and will be brought to Babylon, though he will not see it. The closing chapters of Second Kings tell how exactly that came true.

The chapter ends with two sayings current among the people: one claiming that visions never come true, the other that Ezekiel's visions concern the distant future. The answer to both is the same. The word will be fulfilled, and soon.

=== 13
Ezekiel has just declared that the word of the Lord will be fulfilled without delay. Now he turns to those who have been saying the opposite. Both in Jerusalem and among the exiles there were prophets who promised peace and a quick return home, and Jeremiah's letters to the exiles show that this kind of message was circulating in Babylonia at the time.

Ezekiel calls these prophets foxes among the ruins, scavengers in a broken place rather than builders. They have not gone up into the breaches in the wall to defend the people. Instead, when someone builds a flimsy wall, they cover it with whitewash, so that it looks solid. A storm of rain, hail and wind is coming, and the wall will fall.

The second half of the chapter addresses women who prophesy from their own imagination. They seem to have practised a kind of paid magic, sewing charms and veils to ensnare people, and receiving handfuls of barley and scraps of bread as payment. The details of their practice are not fully clear to us, but their effect is: they have discouraged the righteous and encouraged the wicked.

=== 14
Once again some of the elders of Israel come and sit before Ezekiel, as they did in chapter eight. They have come to inquire of the Lord through him. But God reveals what lies behind their visit: they have set up idols in their hearts. The question is whether God should answer such men at all, and his reply is a call to repent and turn away from their idols.

The second half of the chapter takes up a different question. Might the faithful few in Jerusalem be enough to save the city, as Abraham once pleaded for Sodom? To answer it, God names three men famous for their righteousness: Noah, Daniel and Job. Many hear in the second name the young Daniel who was then serving at the Babylonian court, though some think an ancient figure of the same name is meant. Even these three could save only themselves.

Yet there is a final surprise. Some survivors will come out of Jerusalem and reach the exiles, and when the exiles see how they live, they will understand that the judgement was not without cause.

=== 15
After the elders' visit and the warning about Noah, Daniel and Job, comes a short parable, almost a riddle. It concerns the vine, one of the oldest images for Israel in the Scriptures. Psalms and prophets had pictured the people as a vine brought out of Egypt and planted by God, and the image carried a sense of pride and privilege.

Ezekiel turns it on its head. He does not speak of the vine's grapes, but of its wood. Compared to the trees of the forest, vine wood is thin and crooked. It cannot be used to make anything, not even a peg to hang a pot on. It is good only for fuel. And if that is true of a whole vine, how much less useful is a piece already burned at both ends and charred in the middle?

The application to Jerusalem is brief and direct. The city has already been through the fire once, in the siege and deportation of Jehoiachin's time. Another fire is coming. The chapter is only eight verses long, a pause before the longest and most intense oracle in the book.

=== 16
This is the longest chapter in Ezekiel, and one of the hardest in the Bible to hear. It tells the history of Jerusalem as the life of a woman, and it does so in deliberately graphic language, meant to make the hearers feel the enormity of the city's unfaithfulness.

The story begins with a baby girl, born in the land of Canaan to an Amorite father and a Hittite mother, a reminder that Jerusalem was a Canaanite city long before David took it. She is abandoned in an open field at birth, unwashed and unwanted, until the Lord passes by and says to her, live. Later he returns, finds her grown, and takes her as his wife in a covenant. He clothes her in fine linen and jewels, and she becomes a queen.

Then she trusts in her beauty and turns to others. The lovers are named: Egypt, Assyria and Babylon, the great powers Judah courted through its alliances and whose gods it worshipped. Later her two sisters appear, Samaria to the north and Sodom to the south, and Jerusalem is found to be worse than both. Yet the chapter does not end in judgement alone. God remembers the covenant of her youth and promises an everlasting one.

=== 17
After the long history of Jerusalem as an unfaithful wife, Ezekiel speaks a riddle, a parable in verse about two great eagles, a cedar and a vine. Then he explains it, and the explanation brings us right into the politics of the day.

The first eagle is Nebuchadnezzar, king of Babylon. He came to Lebanon, meaning Jerusalem with its cedar-panelled palaces, and broke off the top of the cedar: that is, he carried King Jehoiachin and the nobles away to Babylon. Then he planted a native seed, Zedekiah, and made a covenant with him, binding him by oath to remain a loyal vassal. Zedekiah grew into a low, spreading vine, safe enough as long as he kept the agreement.

But there was another great eagle, Pharaoh of Egypt, and the vine began to turn its roots towards him. Zedekiah has been sending envoys to Egypt, asking for horses and troops. Because he broke the oath sworn in the Lord's name, he will die in Babylon. The chapter ends with a different shoot from the top of the cedar, planted by God himself on a high mountain of Israel, where it will grow into a noble tree.

=== 18
The previous chapter ended with Zedekiah's broken oath and the fate it would bring. Now Ezekiel confronts a proverb that was evidently popular, in Jerusalem as well as among the exiles, for Jeremiah quotes it too: the fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge.

It was the saying of a generation that felt it was paying for the sins of others. In one sense they had a point. The book of Kings blames the coming disaster above all on the long reign of Manasseh, decades earlier. But the proverb had become an excuse, and a counsel of despair. If our fate was sealed by our grandparents, nothing we do can change it.

Ezekiel's answer is laid out like a case in law, with three generations: a righteous man, his violent son, and a grandson who sees his father's sins and does not follow them. Each will be judged by his own life. The soul who sins is the one who will die. And then the argument turns to the present, to a wicked man who turns from his ways, and to a God who takes no pleasure in anyone's death. The chapter ends with an appeal: repent and live.

=== 19
After the appeal of chapter eighteen comes a lament, a funeral song in the traditional rhythm of mourning, for the princes of Israel. Ezekiel is singing a dirge over the royal house of Judah while some of its kings are still alive.

The song pictures Judah, or perhaps the queen mother, as a lioness raising cubs. One cub grows into a young lion who learns to devour men, until the nations trap him and lead him away with hooks to Egypt. This is Jehoahaz, son of Josiah, who reigned only three months before Pharaoh Neco deposed him and carried him off. A second cub is caught in a net and brought in a cage to the king of Babylon. The song does not name him, but many take him to be Jehoiachin, the king now in exile with Ezekiel.

Then the image changes. The mother is a vine, once fruitful and strong, with branches fit for a ruler's sceptre, now uprooted, withered, and replanted in the desert. Fire has gone out from its main branch, and no strong branch is left to rule.

=== 20
A new date marks a new section. It is the seventh year of the exile, in the fifth month, about two years before the siege of Jerusalem begins, and once more elders of Israel come to sit before Ezekiel and inquire of the Lord. Once more God refuses to be consulted by them.

Instead, Ezekiel is told to give them a history lesson. It is a review of Israel's story from the beginning, but told in a way no Israelite would have told it at a festival. In Egypt, God chose them and swore to bring them out, and they clung to the idols of Egypt. In the wilderness he gave them his statutes and his Sabbaths, and they rebelled. Their children rebelled too. In the land, they worshipped on every high hill. At each stage God resolved to pour out his wrath, and at each stage he held back for the sake of his name, so that it would not be profaned among the nations.

That history leads to the present, to the exiles who want to be like the nations, serving wood and stone. It will not be allowed. God will gather them, judge them face to face in a new wilderness, and bring them back. The chapter closes with a short prophecy of fire against the forest of the south, and Ezekiel's weary aside that people dismiss him as a teller of parables.

=== 21
The parable of the fire in the southern forest is now explained, and the fire becomes a sword. Ezekiel sets his face against Jerusalem and its sanctuaries. When people ask why he groans so bitterly, he is to tell them it is because of the news that is coming.

At the heart of the chapter is the song of the sword, sharpened and polished to flash like lightning, given into the hand of the slayer. Then comes one of the most vivid scenes in the book. Ezekiel is to mark out a road that divides in two, with a signpost. One road leads to Rabbah, the capital of the Ammonites, east of the Jordan; the other to Jerusalem. Both had been involved in plotting against Babylon. At the fork stands the king of Babylon, consulting omens in the ways of his own religion: shaking arrows, consulting household idols, examining a liver. The lot falls on Jerusalem.

The prince of Israel, Zedekiah, is told to take off his crown. The throne will lie in ruins until the one comes to whom it rightfully belongs, words that echo Jacob's blessing of Judah in Genesis. The chapter ends with the sword turning against Ammon as well.

=== 22
The sword has been drawn against Jerusalem. Now Ezekiel is asked a question that has been heard before: will you judge her? Will you judge the city of bloodshed? This chapter is the indictment, the formal list of charges, and it reads almost like a reversal of the law of Moses.

Three oracles follow one another. The first names the crimes committed within the city's walls, and they touch every part of life: the shedding of blood by its princes, contempt for parents, the exploitation of foreigners, widows and orphans, the profaning of the Sabbaths, sexual sin within families, bribery and extortion. Underneath all of it lies a single charge: you have forgotten me.

The second oracle pictures the house of Israel as the dross left in a smelter's furnace after the silver has gone, and Jerusalem as the furnace into which they will be gathered and melted. The third walks through every class of society in turn, princes, priests, officials, prophets and the people of the land, and finds them all corrupt. It ends with God searching for someone to stand in the breach of the wall on the land's behalf, and finding no one.

=== 23
The indictment of Jerusalem continues with a second extended allegory, a companion to chapter sixteen. This time there are two sisters, and like that earlier chapter it uses blunt and graphic language, deliberately so, to portray unfaithfulness as something repellent.

The sisters are named Oholah and Oholibah, names built on the Hebrew word for a tent, perhaps with a reference to the tent of worship. Ezekiel explains them at once. Oholah, the elder, is Samaria, capital of the old northern kingdom of Israel. Oholibah is Jerusalem. Both belonged to the Lord, and both, from their youth in Egypt, gave themselves to others.

The lovers are again the great powers. Samaria courted Assyria, and Assyria destroyed her, a little over a century before Ezekiel's day. Jerusalem saw what happened to her sister and went further, courting Assyria, then Babylon, then turning back to Egypt. Now the Babylonians and their allies will come against her from every side, and she will drink her sister's cup. The chapter closes with the sisters judged together, for idolatry, child sacrifice and the defiling of the sanctuary.

=== 24
The book has been building towards this date. It is the ninth year of the exile, in the tenth month, on the tenth day, around the beginning of five hundred and eighty-eight before Christ. Ezekiel is told to write it down, because on this very day the king of Babylon has laid siege to Jerusalem. The same date is recorded in Second Kings. Far away in Babylonia, the prophet knows it as it happens.

The first oracle is a parable of a cooking pot, taking up the boast of Jerusalem's leaders in chapter eleven that the city was a pot and they were the meat. Now the pot is set on the fire, and its contents are boiled and burned, and then the empty pot itself is heated until its rust and filth are burned away.

Then comes the most personal moment in the book. God tells Ezekiel that he is about to take away the desire of his eyes, and that he must not mourn. That evening his wife dies. The next morning he does as he was commanded, and his grief, held in silence, becomes a sign of what is coming. The chapter ends with a promise: a survivor will bring the news of the city's fall, and on that day Ezekiel's mouth will be opened.

=== 25
The siege of Jerusalem has begun, and Ezekiel has been told that a survivor will one day bring him the news of its fall. But the book does not go straight on to that moment. Instead, before the news arrives in chapter thirty-three, comes a long section of oracles against the nations, running to the end of chapter thirty-two. Some of them are dated after the fall of the city, so the arrangement here is by subject rather than strict time.

This chapter takes four of Judah's nearest neighbours in turn, moving roughly round the compass. Ammon, east of the Jordan, clapped and stamped its feet when the sanctuary was profaned. Moab, to the south east, and Seir, another name for Edom, said Judah was just like all the other nations. Edom, further south, took vengeance on Judah. The Philistines on the coastal plain acted with an ancient hostility.

These were peoples whose relationships with Israel went back to the days of the patriarchs and the judges. Each oracle names the offence, announces the judgement, and ends the same way: then they will know that I am the Lord.

=== 26
The oracles against the nations now reach Tyre, and they will stay there for three chapters. Tyre was the great merchant city of the Phoenicians, on the coast of what is now Lebanon. Its main city stood on a rocky island just off the shore, with walls rising out of the sea, and its ships traded the length of the Mediterranean. It had dealt with Israel since the days of Hiram, who sent cedar and craftsmen for Solomon's temple.

This oracle is dated after Jerusalem has fallen, and Tyre is pleased. With Jerusalem broken, the gate to the nations has swung open, and Tyre expects the trade that once passed through Judah to come its way.

The response is a promise that many nations will come against Tyre like the waves of the sea. Nebuchadnezzar, here called king of kings, is named as the first of them, with horses, chariots and siege works. In history he did besiege Tyre, for about thirteen years, though the island city held out, as Ezekiel himself later acknowledges. The chapter closes with the princes of the coastlands stepping down from their thrones to mourn, and Tyre going down into the Pit.

=== 27
The judgement on Tyre continues, but now in the form of a lament, a funeral song sung over the city before its fall. It is one of the most richly detailed poems in the Old Testament.

Tyre is pictured as a magnificent merchant ship. Her planks are cypress from Senir, near Mount Hermon, her mast a cedar of Lebanon, her oars oak from Bashan, her sail embroidered Egyptian linen. Her rowers come from the Phoenician cities of Sidon and Arvad, and her shipwrights from Gebal, the city the Greeks knew as Byblos. Mercenaries from Persia, Lydia and Put hang their shields on her walls.

Then the poem breaks into a long catalogue of Tyre's trading partners, almost a merchant's ledger of the ancient world: silver and tin from Tarshish in the far west, horses from Beth-togarmah in the north, ivory and ebony, wine from Damascus, spices and gold from Sheba in Arabia. Judah and Israel are there too, selling wheat, honey, oil and balm. Then, at the height of her prosperity, the east wind strikes, and the great ship goes down in the heart of the sea, while sailors stand weeping on the shore.

=== 28
The last of the oracles against Tyre turn from the city to its ruler. His offence is pride. Enthroned in the heart of the seas and enriched by trade, he has come to say, I am a god. Ezekiel answers with irony: you are wiser than Daniel, no secret is hidden from you. But he is a man, and he will die at the hands of foreigners.

Then comes a lament for the king of Tyre, and the language rises to something much larger than one ruler. He is pictured in Eden, the garden of God, adorned with precious stones, a guardian cherub on the holy mountain, blameless from the day of his creation until wickedness was found in him. Because of his pride and the violence of his trade, he is cast out. Many Christian readers have heard in these words an echo of the fall of Satan behind the earthly king. Others read them as a picture of the king as a new Adam who fell. Either way, the poem draws on the story of Eden.

The chapter ends with a brief oracle against Sidon, Tyre's older neighbour, and a promise. Israel's thorny neighbours will trouble her no more, and God will gather his people and let them live securely in their land.

=== 29
The last and longest group of oracles against the nations is addressed to Egypt, and it fills four chapters. Egypt was the great power to the south, and the one Judah's kings kept turning to for help against Babylon. In Ezekiel's day its king was the Pharaoh the Bible elsewhere calls Hophra, and Zedekiah's rebellion depended on him.

The first oracle is dated to the tenth year of the exile, in the tenth month, about a year after the siege of Jerusalem began, while the city was still holding out. Pharaoh is pictured as a great monster lying in the channels of the Nile, boasting that the river is his and that he made it himself. God will drag him out with hooks and leave him in the desert. Egypt has been a staff of reeds for Israel, splintering in the hand of anyone who leaned on it.

The second oracle in the chapter jumps forward sixteen years, to the twenty-seventh year, around five hundred and seventy-one before Christ. It is the latest date in the whole book. Nebuchadnezzar's long siege of Tyre is over, and his army has gained nothing from it. God will give him Egypt instead, as wages for his labour.

=== 30
The oracles against Egypt continue. The first part of this chapter carries no date. It is a poem about the day of the Lord, a day of clouds and doom for the nations, and it sweeps across the land of Egypt from the north to the south. Egypt's allies and mercenaries fall with her, from Cush in the upper Nile valley, Put and Lud and Libya. Then the poem names one Egyptian city after another, Memphis, Zoan, Thebes, Pelusium, On and Tahpanhes, places that were the pride of an ancient civilisation. Nebuchadnezzar is named as the one who will bring the sword.

Then comes a dated oracle, from the eleventh year of the exile, in the first month, a few months before Jerusalem fell. God says that he has broken the arm of Pharaoh. This probably refers to an episode Jeremiah also records: an Egyptian army marched north to relieve the siege of Jerusalem, the Babylonians withdrew for a time to meet it, and then the Egyptians went home and the siege resumed. Any hope Zedekiah had placed in Egypt was gone. Pharaoh's other arm will be broken too, and the sword placed in the hand of the king of Babylon.

=== 31
Another dated oracle, in the eleventh year, in the third month, just weeks before the walls of Jerusalem were breached. Ezekiel is told to put a question to Pharaoh: who is like you in your greatness? And to answer it, he points to another great power.

Assyria is pictured as a magnificent cedar of Lebanon, its top among the clouds, watered by the deep springs, so that all the birds nest in its branches and all the great nations live in its shade. Even the trees in the garden of God could not compare with it. For the exiles this was recent history. Assyria had dominated their world for well over a century, and destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel, but its capital Nineveh had fallen to the Babylonians and their allies within living memory.

Because the tree grew proud of its height, it was cut down, and its branches lie broken across the mountains and valleys, and it has gone down to Sheol, the realm of the dead. Then the point is pressed home to Pharaoh. If Assyria fell, Egypt will fall.

=== 32
The last two oracles against Egypt are dated in the twelfth year of the exile. By now Jerusalem has fallen, and the news has probably reached the exiles. Both oracles are laments, funeral songs for a king who is still alive.

In the first, Pharaoh thinks of himself as a lion among the nations, but he is really a monster thrashing about in the rivers and muddying the waters. God will catch him in a net and throw him on the land, and the heavens will go dark over him. The sword of the king of Babylon is coming.

The second is one of the strangest passages in the prophets. Ezekiel follows Egypt down into the world of the dead, the Pit, where the great powers of the past already lie among the slain. Assyria is there with her company, and Elam, from the east of Babylonia, and Meshech and Tubal from the north, and Edom, and the princes of the north, and the Sidonians. All of them once spread terror in the land of the living. Now Pharaoh joins them, and finds a grim kind of comfort in their company. With this, the oracles against the nations come to an end.

=== 33
The oracles against the nations are over, and the book returns to the exiles by the Kebar. This chapter is the hinge on which the whole of Ezekiel turns.

It begins by going back to the start. Ezekiel's commission as a watchman, first given in chapter three, is repeated, almost word for word: the watchman who sees the sword coming must blow the trumpet, and each hearer is responsible for his own response. The people's mood has changed. They are no longer saying that the exile will be short. Now they say that their sins are heavy upon them, and they ask how they can live. The answer is the one Ezekiel gave in chapter eighteen. God takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked. Turn, and live.

Then comes the date the book has been waiting for. In the twelfth year of the exile, in the tenth month, a fugitive arrives from Jerusalem with the news: the city has fallen. The evening before, Ezekiel's mouth had been opened, just as he was promised when his wife died. The chapter ends with two pictures: survivors in the ruins of Judah still claiming the land, and the exiles flocking to hear Ezekiel as if he were a singer of love songs, enjoying the performance but doing nothing about it.

=== 34
Jerusalem has fallen, and from here on the tone of the book changes. Judgement is not forgotten, but the message turns towards what God will do next, and it begins with the shepherds of Israel.

In the ancient Near East, kings were often called shepherds of their people. Ezekiel uses the image to indict the rulers of Judah, the last kings of David's line and their officials. They fed themselves, wore the wool and ate the fat, but they did not strengthen the weak or heal the sick or search for the lost. So the flock has been scattered across the mountains, prey for wild animals.

The answer is that God himself will be the shepherd. He will search for his sheep, gather them from the countries, and bring them home to graze on the mountains of Israel. He will judge between the sheep too, between the strong who push and trample and the weak who are pushed aside. And he will appoint over them one shepherd, his servant David, a new king in David's line. The chapter would later be echoed in the Gospel of John, when Jesus calls himself the good shepherd.

=== 35
The promise to the flock of Israel is interrupted by one more oracle against a neighbour. It is aimed at Mount Seir, the mountainous country south of the Dead Sea, home of Edom. Edom was judged briefly in chapter twenty-five, but here the charge is developed at length, and its placement is deliberate.

The Edomites traced their descent from Esau, the brother of Jacob, and the hostility between the two peoples was ancient. When Jerusalem fell, Edom took advantage of it, delivering fugitives to the sword and moving in to claim Judah's territory. The little book of Obadiah and Psalm one hundred and thirty-seven both remember that bitterness. Edom said that the two nations, Israel and Judah, would be theirs, and that the mountains of Israel were desolate and theirs to devour.

The reply is that Mount Seir itself will be made desolate. The chapter sets up the next one, which turns to the mountains of Israel themselves, and announces that the land Edom coveted belongs to the Lord.

=== 36
At the start of the book, Ezekiel was told to set his face against the mountains of Israel and prophesy their destruction. Now, after the fall of Jerusalem and the oracle against Edom, he is told to speak to the same mountains again. This time the message is the opposite. The neighbouring nations have taunted them, saying the ancient heights are now theirs. But God is on their side. They will be tilled and sown again, their cities rebuilt, their ruins inhabited, because the exiles will soon come home.

The second half of the chapter explains why. Israel's exile has caused God's name to be profaned among the nations, who look at the scattered people and say: these are the Lord's people, and yet they had to leave his land. So God will act, not for Israel's sake, but for the sake of his holy name.

What follows is one of the great promises of the Old Testament, picking up what was first glimpsed in chapter eleven. God will gather his people, sprinkle clean water on them, and give them a new heart and a new spirit. He will remove the heart of stone and put his own Spirit within them. And the desolate land will become like the garden of Eden.

=== 37
The promise of a new heart and a new spirit has just been given. But the exiles, now that Jerusalem has fallen, are saying something else: our bones are dried up, our hope has perished, we are cut off. This chapter is God's answer, and it comes as the most famous of all Ezekiel's visions.

Once again the hand of the Lord is on him, and the Spirit carries him to a valley filled with bones, very many and very dry, like the remains of an army left unburied on a battlefield. God asks him a question: can these bones live? Ezekiel's reply is careful: Lord God, only you know. Then he is told to prophesy to the bones, and what happens next is described step by step. The Hebrew word used for breath here can also mean wind or spirit, and the vision plays on all three meanings.

The second half of the chapter is an acted sign. Ezekiel takes two sticks, writes Judah on one and Joseph, or Ephraim, on the other, and joins them in his hand. Since the days of Solomon's son Rehoboam, more than three centuries earlier, the people had been divided into two kingdoms. Now God promises one nation under one king, his servant David, with his sanctuary among them for ever.

=== 38
The people of God have been promised restoration: a new heart, a new spirit, a shepherd king, a reunited nation living securely in its land. Now, in two chapters, Ezekiel looks beyond that restoration to a final assault on it, and its defeat.

The enemy is named Gog, of the land of Magog, chief prince of Meshech and Tubal. Meshech and Tubal were peoples of the highlands of Asia Minor, mentioned in chapter twenty-seven as traders with Tyre, and Magog appears in the Table of Nations in Genesis among the descendants of Japheth. Who Gog is remains uncertain; interpreters have taken him as a historical king, a symbolic figure for the hostile nations, or a final enemy still to come. His allies come from the far edges of the known world: Persia to the east, Cush and Put to the south, Gomer and Beth-togarmah to the far north.

Gog comes, the text says, after many days, in the latter years, against a land of unwalled villages whose people have been gathered from the nations and are living in peace. But the whole campaign is under God's control. He puts hooks in Gog's jaws and draws him out, so that he may show his holiness before the nations.

=== 39
The prophecy against Gog continues, and now it describes his end. God strikes the bow from his left hand and the arrows from his right, and Gog and all his armies fall on the mountains of Israel.

The aftermath is described in unusual detail, and much of it is about cleansing the land. The people of Israel go out and burn the abandoned weapons as fuel, and there are so many that they last seven years. A burial ground is set aside in a valley east of the Sea, which will be called the Valley of Hamon-gog, the valley of Gog's horde. For seven months the people bury the dead, and afterwards men are employed to search the land for any remaining bones, so that the land is made clean. Then the birds and beasts are summoned to a great sacrificial feast on the fallen.

The chapter, and this whole section of the book, ends by gathering its threads. The nations will understand that Israel went into exile for its sins. Now God will restore his people, leave none of them behind, and pour out his Spirit on the house of Israel. The book of Revelation later takes up the names of Gog and Magog for the last battle at the end of the age.

=== 40
The last nine chapters of Ezekiel form a single great vision, and it is carefully dated. It is the twenty-fifth year of the exile, at the beginning of the year, fourteen years after the fall of Jerusalem, around five hundred and seventy-three before Christ. Ezekiel is now perhaps fifty years old. The temple he saw defiled and abandoned lies in ruins.

Once again the hand of the Lord takes him in visions to the land of Israel, and sets him on a very high mountain. On its southern slope is something like a city. There he meets a man whose appearance shines like bronze, holding a linen cord and a measuring rod. He is told to look, listen, pay attention to everything, and report it to the house of Israel.

What follows is a guided tour of a temple complex, in which the man measures as he goes. He begins with the outer wall, then the great gateway on the east side, with its guard chambers, then the outer court with its chambers and pavement, and the gates on the north and south. Then he climbs to the inner court, with its own gates, the tables where sacrifices are prepared, and rooms for the priests, the sons of Zadok. The chapter ends at the porch of the temple itself. The measurements are given in long cubits, a little over half a metre each, and the precision is part of the message: this is a sanctuary laid out by God himself.

=== 41
The tour has reached the temple building itself. Ezekiel's guide leads him through the porch into the outer sanctuary, the long hall known as the holy place, and measures it: forty cubits by twenty, the same proportions as the main hall of Solomon's temple.

Then something small but telling happens. The man goes on alone into the inner room and measures it, twenty cubits square, and tells Ezekiel: this is the Most Holy Place. Ezekiel, though a priest, does not go in. Under the law of Moses only the high priest entered that room, once a year, and the vision keeps that boundary.

The rest of the chapter describes the structure around the sanctuary: thick walls, three storeys of side rooms built around it, a separate building to the west, and the overall dimensions of the complex. Then the description moves inside again, to the wooden panelling and the carvings that cover the walls and doors, cherubim and palm trees alternating all the way round, each cherub with two faces, a man and a young lion. Before the inner sanctuary stands a wooden altar, and the guide names it: the table that is before the Lord.

=== 42
Having measured the temple building, the guide now leads Ezekiel out into the outer court on the north side, to a large building of chambers arranged in three storeys, with matching chambers on the south. The architectural details here are some of the hardest in the book to picture, and translators and commentators have often had to make their best judgement.

The purpose of the chambers is clearer. They are holy rooms where the priests who approach the Lord will eat their share of the most holy offerings, and store them. And they are where the priests will change their clothes. Garments worn in ministry are holy, and must be left behind before the priests go out among the people.

Then the guide leads Ezekiel out through the east gate and measures the entire temple area from the outside. Each side is five hundred cubits, a perfect square. The chapter ends with a sentence that sums up the purpose of all these walls, gates and measurements: the wall was there to separate the holy from the common. Ezekiel was told in chapter twenty-two that the priests of Jerusalem had failed to make that distinction. In the new temple it is built into the stones.

=== 43
This is the moment the whole book has been moving towards. Back in chapters ten and eleven, Ezekiel watched the glory of the Lord leave the temple by the east gate and depart from the city to the mountain in the east. Now his guide brings him back to that same gate, and he sees the glory of the God of Israel coming from the east, with a sound like the roar of many waters, and the earth shining with its light.

Ezekiel tells us that what he saw was like the vision of the city's destruction, and like the visions by the River Kebar. He falls on his face. The glory enters the temple by the east gate, and the Spirit lifts him into the inner court, where the glory fills the house. Then a voice from within the temple declares that this is the place of God's throne, where he will dwell among his people for ever. The kings of Israel will never again defile it, as they did when their palace stood wall to wall with his sanctuary.

The rest of the chapter turns to the great altar of burnt offering, its measurements in stepped tiers, and the seven days of offerings by which it is to be cleansed and consecrated before worship can begin.

=== 44
The glory of the Lord has entered the temple by the east gate, and now Ezekiel finds that gate shut. It is to remain shut for ever, because the Lord, the God of Israel, has entered by it. Only the prince may sit inside the gateway to eat before the Lord. This figure, called the prince rather than the king, will appear several times in the chapters that follow.

Then Ezekiel is brought to the front of the temple, where he sees the glory filling the house and falls on his face again. He is told to pay careful attention, because what follows are the laws of the temple, beginning with who may enter and serve there.

In the past, foreigners had been allowed to serve in the sanctuary. That must end. The Levites who went astray after idols will bear their guilt: they will still serve, as gatekeepers and assistants, but they will no longer approach the altar as priests. That privilege belongs to the priests descended from Zadok, who served at the temple from the days of David and Solomon, and whose line kept charge of the sanctuary when others went astray. The chapter then sets out their rules, about clothing, hair, marriage, mourning and teaching, and their inheritance, which is the Lord himself.

=== 45
The laws of the temple now widen out to the land around it. When the land is divided among the tribes, a central strip is to be set apart as a holy portion for the Lord, about eight miles from east to west. At its heart stands the sanctuary. Around it are areas for the priests' houses, for the Levites' towns, and for a city that belongs to all Israel.

On either side of this holy district lies land for the prince. The reason is given plainly. In the past the kings of Judah had seized the property of their people, and the story of King Ahab and Naboth's vineyard was not the only example. Now the prince will have his own land, and the princes of Israel are told: enough. Stop your violence and your evictions. Use honest scales and honest measures.

The rest of the chapter describes the offerings the people are to give, and the prince's duty to provide sacrifices for the whole nation at the feasts, the new moons and the Sabbaths. A purification of the sanctuary marks the start of the year, and then come Passover in the first month and the feast of the seventh month, the Feast of Tabernacles, each lasting seven days.

=== 46
The regulations for worship continue. The east gate of the inner court is to be kept shut on the six working days and opened on the Sabbath and at the new moon. The prince comes in by the porch of the gateway and stands at the gatepost while the priests offer his sacrifices, and the people of the land bow in worship at the entrance. The offerings for each occasion are listed, along with the lamb to be offered every morning.

A few details give a vivid picture of the crowds at the great festivals. Whoever comes in by the north gate must leave by the south, and whoever comes in by the south must leave by the north, so that the worshippers flow through the courts in one direction. The prince is to go in with the people and leave with them.

There is also a law about the prince's inheritance. He may give land to his sons, but a gift to a servant returns to him in the year of freedom, and he must not take the people's inheritance from them. Then the guide resumes the tour, showing Ezekiel the kitchens where the priests cook their portions, and the four corner courts where the sacrifices of the people are boiled.

=== 47
The laws of the temple are complete, and the guide brings Ezekiel back to the entrance of the house. There he sees water trickling out from under the threshold, flowing east past the south side of the altar.

The guide follows it eastward with a measuring line. After a thousand cubits Ezekiel wades through water up to his ankles, then his knees, then his waist, until it becomes a river too deep to cross. It flows down into the Arabah, the deep rift valley of the Jordan, and into the Dead Sea, the lowest and saltiest body of water on earth, where nothing lives. The river makes its waters fresh. Fishermen spread their nets along its shore from En-gedi to En-eglaim, and fruit trees line both banks, bearing fruit every month, with leaves for healing. The final chapter of the Bible, in Revelation, takes up this picture of the river and the trees.

The second half of the chapter turns to the land itself. Its borders are set out, from the Great Sea, the Mediterranean, in the west to the Jordan in the east, and it is to be divided equally among the twelve tribes. And the foreigners who have settled among them are to receive an inheritance alongside them, as if native born.

=== 48
The book's final chapter completes the division of the land. It reads like a map, drawn from north to south. Seven tribes receive parallel strips of land in the north, from Dan at the northern frontier down to Judah. Then comes the holy portion, with the sanctuary at its centre, the land of the priests descended from Zadok and of the Levites, the city and its farmland, and on either side the land of the prince. South of it lie the remaining five tribes, from Benjamin down to Gad.

It is not the old map of Joshua's day. The tribes are arranged afresh, and the land is shared out evenly. The tribes descended from Jacob's wives Leah and Rachel lie nearest the sanctuary, and those descended from their servants Bilhah and Zilpah lie further out. Readers have long differed over how these final chapters are meant to be understood, whether as a plan for the returning exiles, a symbolic picture of God dwelling with his people, or a vision of a future still to come.

The book ends with the city. It is square, with three gates on each side, each named after one of the tribes of Israel. And it receives a new name, the last words of Ezekiel's prophecy: the Lord is there.
