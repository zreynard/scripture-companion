=== intro
Second Corinthians is the most personal of Paul's letters. In First Corinthians he wrote as a founder and teacher, answering a church's questions and correcting its faults. Here he writes as a man whose relationship with that church has been strained almost to breaking, and who has just learned, to his enormous relief, that it is beginning to mend. The letter lets us see Paul under pressure: wounded, defensive, affectionate, ironic, and completely open about his own weakness.

When Paul sent First Corinthians from Ephesus, he hoped that his instructions, and a visit from Timothy, would set things right. They did not. The letters themselves let us piece together what happened next, although Acts says almost nothing about it. Paul seems to have made a brief visit across the Aegean to Corinth, which he later calls a painful visit. During it someone in the church opposed or insulted him openly, and the church, it seems, did not stand with him. Paul withdrew. Instead of returning as he had planned, he wrote them a severe letter, written, he says, out of great distress and with many tears. That letter has probably not survived, though some scholars think part of it may be preserved in the last four chapters of this one. It was carried to Corinth by Titus.

Titus is one of Paul's most trusted co-workers, though Acts never names him. He was a Greek, a Gentile convert who had gone up to Jerusalem with Paul and Barnabas years before, where the apostles did not require him to be circumcised. Paul trusted him with the hardest assignments, and the Corinthian crisis was one of them.

While Titus was away, Paul faced troubles of his own. In the province of Asia, the region around Ephesus, he went through an ordeal so severe that he despaired of life itself. He does not explain what it was. It may have been connected with the opposition at Ephesus that Acts describes, when the silversmiths stirred up a riot in honour of the goddess Artemis, but we do not know. Paul then travelled north to Troas, hoping to meet Titus on his way back. Although there was an open door for preaching there, he was too anxious to stay, and he crossed into Macedonia, the northern part of Greece where he had founded churches at Philippi and Thessalonica. There at last Titus found him, with good news. Most of the Corinthians had responded to the severe letter with grief and a renewed loyalty to Paul. They had disciplined the offender, and Paul now urges them to forgive him.

So Paul wrote this letter from Macedonia, probably about a year after First Corinthians, in the middle fifties of the first century. He was preparing for his third visit to Corinth, and Acts tells us that he did spend three months in Greece soon afterwards, the time when he wrote Romans.

Not all was settled. Other teachers had arrived in Corinth, Jewish believers who carried letters of recommendation, spoke impressively, accepted payment, and looked down on Paul. Paul calls them, with heavy irony, the super-apostles. They mocked him as weighty in his letters but unimpressive in person. Some Corinthians were drawn to them, and they questioned Paul's honesty, his changes of plan, and his refusal to accept money from them.

The letter moves in three broad stages. In the first seven chapters Paul explains his recent conduct and reflects at length on the nature of his ministry: a ministry of a new covenant, carried in fragile human vessels, marked by suffering and by the message of reconciliation with God. In chapters eight and nine he turns to the collection for the poor believers in Jerusalem, which the Corinthians had begun the year before and now needed to complete. Then, in the last four chapters, the tone changes abruptly. Paul confronts his rivals directly, and he reluctantly boasts, as he puts it, like a fool, listing not his successes but his sufferings and weaknesses. The change is so sharp that many scholars suspect this section was originally a separate letter, though others read it as Paul turning at the end to the minority still resisting him.

=== 1
Paul opens with Timothy beside him, writing to the church in Corinth and to believers across Achaia, the province of southern Greece. In most of his letters a thanksgiving for the readers follows the greeting. Here, instead, Paul praises God as the Father of compassion and the God of all comfort, and he speaks at once of suffering.

He describes a recent crisis in the province of Asia, when he and his companions were crushed beyond their strength and felt they had received the sentence of death. He does not say what it was, but he wants the Corinthians to know how near death he came, and how God rescued him.

Then Paul turns to a criticism that had reached him. He had promised to visit Corinth twice, on his way to Macedonia and on his way back, and then changed his plans. Some in Corinth took this as proof that he was unreliable, saying yes and no at the same time. Paul defends his integrity, grounding it in the faithfulness of God, and he begins to explain why he did not come: to spare them another painful visit.

=== 2
The explanation for the cancelled visit continues. Paul had already made one painful visit to Corinth, and he did not want to make another. Instead he wrote to them, out of great distress, with many tears. That letter was not meant to grieve them, he says, but to show them how much he loved them.

He then speaks about the man whose conduct had caused so much pain. Paul does not name him or describe what he did. The church has now disciplined him, and Paul urges them to forgive and comfort him, so that he is not overwhelmed by sorrow.

Then the story moves on. Paul recalls going to Troas, on the coast of Asia, where a door for preaching stood open. But he was so anxious to hear news from Titus that he could not settle, and he went on to Macedonia. At that point he breaks off the story, which he will not resume until chapter seven, and bursts into thanksgiving, using the image of a Roman victory procession, with incense rising as captives are led through the streets.

=== 3
The last chapter ended with Paul's ministry pictured as a fragrance spread by God, and with the question of who is equal to such a task. Now he anticipates an objection. Is he commending himself again? The rival teachers in Corinth had arrived with letters of recommendation, and some may have asked why Paul had none.

His answer is that the Corinthians themselves are his letter, written not with ink on paper but by the Spirit of God on human hearts. That image leads him into a comparison with the giving of the law at Mount Sinai, when the commandments were written on tablets of stone.

The rest of the chapter reflects on the story in Exodus in which Moses' face shone after he had spoken with God, so that he had to cover it with a veil. Paul uses that story to set the new covenant beside the old, and to speak of a glory that does not fade. The chapter closes with a picture of believers who, with unveiled faces, are being changed into the image of the Lord.

=== 4
From the glory of the new covenant and the unveiled faces of those who turn to the Lord, Paul now turns to the messengers who carry this message, and the contrast could hardly be greater.

His critics in Corinth seem to have said that his weakness and sufferings disqualified him as an apostle. Paul does not deny the weakness. He describes himself and his companions as clay jars holding a treasure, ordinary earthenware vessels of the kind found in every household, so that it will be clear the power belongs to God. Then come a series of paired phrases describing their life: hard pressed but not crushed, perplexed but not in despair, struck down but not destroyed.

Paul sees in his own sufferings a sharing in the death of Jesus, and in his survival a sign of Jesus' life. The chapter ends by looking beyond the present. The outer self is wasting away, Paul writes, but the inner self is being renewed day by day, and the troubles of the present will give way to an eternal glory.

=== 5
At the end of the last chapter Paul set what is seen and temporary beside what is unseen and eternal. Now he develops that thought, using the image of a tent. The body, he says, is like a tent that will one day be taken down. Paul, who worked as a tentmaker, knew such things well. Believers long instead for a permanent dwelling from God, and the Spirit is given as a pledge of what is to come.

From there Paul turns to what drives his ministry. He lives in the knowledge that all must appear before the judgment seat of Christ, and above all he is compelled by the love of Christ, who died for all. Paul has been accused of being out of his mind, or of promoting himself, and he answers both charges.

The chapter rises to a description of what God has done in Christ. Anyone in Christ is a new creation. God has reconciled the world to himself, and he has entrusted Paul with the message of reconciliation. Paul calls himself an ambassador for Christ, and he urges the Corinthians to be reconciled to God.

=== 6
As an ambassador appealing on God's behalf, Paul continues his plea here, urging the Corinthians not to receive God's grace in vain, and quoting a promise from Isaiah about the day of salvation.

Then he describes his own life once more, this time in a long, rhythmic passage. He lists the hardships that have marked his ministry: beatings, imprisonments, riots, sleepless nights and hunger. He names the qualities with which he has tried to serve, and then a series of paradoxes in which the way he appears to others is set against the truth: treated as impostors, yet genuine; dying, yet living on; poor, yet making many rich.

At this point Paul speaks to the Corinthians directly and with feeling. He has opened his heart to them, and he asks them to open theirs in return. There follows a sharp warning against being unequally yoked with unbelievers, supported by a chain of quotations from the Old Testament, which some readers have found an abrupt change of subject. Its concern is the holiness of the church as God's temple, a theme Paul had already raised in First Corinthians.

=== 7
The appeal for holiness concludes, and Paul returns to the personal plea he made just before it: make room for us in your hearts.

Then he picks up the story he left behind in chapter two. He had gone from Troas into Macedonia, still with no news of Titus, and he found no rest there either: conflicts on the outside, fears within. Then Titus arrived. His coming was a comfort in itself, but the news he brought was better still. The Corinthians had received the severe letter with grief and earnestness. They longed to see Paul, and they had acted to put matters right.

Paul admits that for a time he regretted sending that letter, knowing it would hurt them. Now he is glad, because their sorrow led to repentance. He draws a distinction between godly sorrow and worldly sorrow, and he describes the joy of Titus, who had been warmly received by them. The chapter ends on a note of relief and renewed confidence, which prepares the ground for the request that follows.

=== 8
With the relationship restored, Paul turns to a practical matter he had raised in First Corinthians: the collection for the poor believers in Jerusalem. The Corinthians had made a start the previous year, but the troubles between them and Paul seem to have stalled it.

Paul begins with the example of the churches of Macedonia, which included Philippi, Thessalonica and Berea. They had been through severe trials, and they were poor, yet they gave beyond their means and begged for the privilege of taking part. Paul holds them up to the Corinthians, and then points to the deepest example of all: Christ, who was rich, yet for their sake became poor.

The second half of the chapter is about arrangements. Titus is returning to Corinth to help complete the collection, and two other brothers, not named, will travel with him. One of them has been chosen by the churches to accompany the gift. Paul is careful to explain that he wants everything handled openly, so that no one can accuse him of mismanaging the money. Given what had been said about him in Corinth, the care is understandable.

=== 9
The collection for Jerusalem remains the subject. Paul tells the Corinthians that he has been boasting about them to the Macedonians, saying that Achaia has been ready to give since the year before. Now he is sending Titus and the brothers ahead, so that if Macedonians come with him to Corinth, neither he nor they will be embarrassed by an unfinished gift.

Then Paul reflects on the spirit of giving. He uses the familiar picture of sowing and reaping, and he says that God loves a cheerful giver. God, who supplies seed to the sower, will provide for those who give.

Paul also looks beyond the money itself. The gift will meet the needs of the believers in Judea, but it will also cause them to give thanks to God, and it will show that the Gentile churches truly share in the same faith. For Paul the collection was a sign of the unity of Jewish and Gentile believers. The chapter ends with thanks to God for his indescribable gift. We know from Romans, written a few months later in Corinth, that Achaia did contribute.

=== 10
Chapter ten opens in a different voice. The relief and warmth of the earlier chapters give way to sharp, at times bitter, self-defence. Paul now confronts directly the rival teachers who had come to Corinth, and those in the church who had been influenced by them.

He takes up what his critics were saying: that he is bold in his letters but timid in person, that his letters are weighty and forceful but his presence is unimpressive and his speaking of no account. Paul warns that when he comes, he will be as bold in action as he is on paper. He uses military language, speaking of weapons that can demolish strongholds and take every thought captive to Christ.

The rest of the chapter concerns boasting and territory. The rivals commended themselves and measured themselves by one another. Paul insists that he will boast only within the field God assigned to him, which includes Corinth, since he was the first to bring them the gospel. He did not build on another man's work, as they have done.

=== 11
Having confronted his rivals, Paul now does something he finds distasteful. Since they boast so much, he will boast too, though he insists that he speaks as a fool.

He begins with his fear for the church. He pictures himself as the father of a bride, who has promised her to one husband, Christ, and fears she will be led astray as Eve was by the serpent. The rivals preach a different Jesus, he says, and the Corinthians put up with it. He addresses the charge about money: he preached to them free of charge, supported by gifts from the believers in Macedonia, and he will not change his practice now. Then he names the rival teachers for what he believes they are: false apostles disguised as servants of righteousness.

The second half of the chapter is Paul's foolish boast. His rivals pride themselves on being Hebrews and Israelites, and so is he. But the list that follows is not of achievements. It is a catalogue of what he has suffered, and many of the events it mentions are not recorded anywhere else. It ends with a story from the very beginning of his ministry: an escape from Damascus, lowered in a basket through a window in the city wall.

=== 12
The foolish boasting goes on, forced on him, Paul says, by the Corinthians themselves. Having listed his sufferings, he now turns to visions and revelations, which the rival teachers may have claimed in abundance.

He speaks of a man in Christ, fourteen years earlier, caught up to the third heaven and into Paradise, where he heard things that cannot be told. He speaks of this man in the third person, but it soon becomes clear he is describing himself. Then he tells what followed: a thorn in the flesh, a messenger of Satan, given to keep him from becoming conceited. Paul never says what the thorn was, and many suggestions have been made, from illness to persecution, but the text does not settle it. He pleaded three times for it to be removed, and the Lord's answer has become one of the best known sayings in his letters.

In the last part of the chapter Paul drops the role of the fool. He is ready to visit Corinth a third time, and he still will not be a burden to them. He defends Titus and himself against any suggestion of exploiting them, and he admits his fear of what he might find when he arrives.

=== 13
This is Paul's final word before his third visit. In the last chapter he admitted his fear that he would find quarrels and unrepentant sin when he arrived. Now he gives a solemn warning. When he comes, he will not spare those who have persisted in wrongdoing.

He quotes the law of Moses, which required two or three witnesses to establish any charge. Some in Corinth wanted proof that Christ was speaking through Paul, and he answers that they will have it. Christ was crucified in weakness but lives by the power of God, and so it will be with his apostle. Then Paul turns their demand around. Rather than testing him, they should examine themselves.

He explains that he writes these things while absent so that he will not need to be severe in person. The letter ends with a brief call to harmony and peace, greetings from the believers with him, and a closing blessing that names the Lord Jesus Christ, God, and the Holy Spirit together. Christians have spoken it in worship for centuries since.
