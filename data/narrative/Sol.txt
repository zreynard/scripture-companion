=== intro
After the searching reflections of Ecclesiastes comes a book of an entirely different kind. The Song of Songs is love poetry, a sequence of songs in which a young woman and a young man speak of their longing for one another, delight in each other's beauty, lose each other and find each other again. There is no law here, no prophecy and no history of kings, and God is never directly named, unless in a single phrase near the end that some translate as the flame of the Lord. Yet the Song has been treasured by Jews and Christians alike for thousands of years.

Its opening line calls it Solomon's Song of Songs. In Hebrew that is a way of saying the greatest of songs, as holy of holies means the most holy place. Solomon is named several times in the poem, and the book of Kings remembers that he composed more than a thousand songs. But the Hebrew phrase can mean a song by Solomon, for him or about him, and whether he wrote it, or whether it was written in his honour or in the tradition of his court, cannot be settled. He appears in the poem as a figure of royal splendour, with his carriage, his crown and his vineyard, while the lovers themselves are never given personal names.

The listener needs to know who is speaking, because the poem moves between voices without announcing them. In Hebrew the grammar makes it clear whether a man or a woman is speaking, but in English that is often lost, which is why many Bibles add small headings. There are three main voices. The woman speaks most of all, and her voice opens and closes the Song. She is a young woman from the countryside, darkened by the sun from working in her brothers' vineyards, and near the end she is called the Shulammite. The man is sometimes pictured as a king and sometimes as a shepherd pasturing his flock, and these may be two ways of picturing the same beloved. The third voice belongs to the daughters of Jerusalem, a group of young women who act like a chorus, asking questions, offering encouragement and receiving the woman's confidences. Her brothers are mentioned too, and they have their own say near the end.

Much of the poem's language comes from the land itself, and it can sound strange to modern ears. The lovers compare each other to gazelles and doves, to a mare among Pharaoh's chariots, to a flock of goats streaming down the slopes of Gilead. The place names reach from the oasis of En-gedi by the Dead Sea to the heights of Lebanon and Hermon in the north, and from Carmel to Heshbon across the Jordan. The poem is full of springtime, vineyards and gardens, spices and fragrance, and it describes physical love openly but with delicacy, often through these images.

Readers have long asked whether the Song tells a story. Some find a sequence of courtship, wedding and married life, with a royal wedding procession at its centre. Others have seen a drama with more characters. Many read it as a collection of love songs, gathered and arranged so that certain refrains and scenes return. The most memorable of those refrains is the woman's charge to the daughters of Jerusalem, heard three times, not to arouse or awaken love until the time is right. Another is her declaration, my beloved is mine and I am his.

The Song has also been read in two ways that need not be set against each other. Its plain sense is a celebration of human love between a man and a woman, within the good creation that God called very good. From early times Jewish readers also heard in it the love between the Lord and Israel, and the Song is read in the synagogue at Passover. Christian readers, from the early church through the medieval monasteries and beyond, heard in it the love of Christ for his church and for the believer. Elsewhere the Bible itself uses the language of marriage for God's covenant love, in the prophets and in the letters of Paul, and that is part of why the Song has been read this way.

The poem builds towards a great statement near its close, that love is as strong as death, and that many waters cannot quench it. It ends, as it began, with the woman's voice calling her beloved to come.

=== 1
The Song opens with its title and then, without introduction, with the woman's voice. She longs for her beloved's kisses, his love better than wine and his name like poured-out perfume. She pictures the king bringing her into his chambers, and the young women around her join in his praise.

Then she turns to the daughters of Jerusalem and speaks about herself. She is dark, she says, yet lovely, like the black goat-hair tents of Kedar, the nomads of the desert. The sun has darkened her, because her brothers were angry with her and set her to work in the vineyards, so that she neglected her own.

The rest of the chapter is an exchange between the two lovers. She asks where he grazes his flock at midday. He replies with praise, comparing her to a mare among Pharaoh's chariots, and they answer each other's admiration. She compares him to henna blossoms in the vineyards of En-gedi, and the chapter ends with the two of them outdoors, the grass their bed and the cedars their roof.

=== 2
The lovers' exchange continues. She calls herself a rose of Sharon, a lily of the valleys, the simple wildflowers of the coastal plain. He replies that she is like a lily among thorns. She answers that he is like an apple tree among the trees of the forest, and she delights to sit in his shade.

Here, for the first time, comes the refrain that will return twice more in the Song. The woman turns to the daughters of Jerusalem and charges them not to arouse or awaken love until the time is right.

The second half of the chapter is one of the loveliest scenes in the poem. The woman hears her beloved coming, leaping across the hills like a gazelle, and she sees him peering through the lattice of her house. She recalls his invitation: arise, my darling, come away, for the winter is past, the rains are over, the flowers have appeared and the season of singing has come. The chapter ends with her declaration, my beloved is mine and I am his.

=== 3
The chapter opens at night. The woman lies on her bed, longing for the one she loves, and when she cannot find him she rises and searches through the streets and squares of the city. She asks the watchmen on their rounds whether they have seen him, and just after passing them she finds him and will not let him go. Again she charges the daughters of Jerusalem not to awaken love until the time is right. Whether this is a dream, a memory or a picture of longing, the poem does not say.

Then the scene changes completely. A voice asks who is coming up from the wilderness like a column of smoke, scented with myrrh and frankincense. It is Solomon's carriage, made of timber from Lebanon, with posts of silver and a base of gold, escorted by sixty of Israel's mightiest warriors.

The daughters of Zion are called out to see King Solomon wearing the crown his mother placed on him on the day of his wedding. The poem has reached its central celebration.

=== 4
After the wedding procession, the man speaks at length, and this chapter is his song of praise to the woman. He describes her beauty from her eyes downwards, in images drawn from the countryside and the city: her hair like a flock of goats streaming down Mount Gilead, her teeth like newly shorn sheep, her neck like the tower of David hung with warriors' shields. To ancient ears these comparisons spoke of grace, abundance and dignity. He ends this first portrait by saying that she is altogether beautiful, without a flaw.

Now, for the first time, he calls her my bride. He invites her to come with him from the high mountains of Lebanon and Hermon, and he speaks of her as his sister, a term of tender closeness in the love poetry of the ancient world.

The chapter ends with an extended picture of her as a garden, locked up and sealed, full of pomegranates, spices and flowing streams. In the last lines the woman herself answers, inviting the winds to blow on her garden and her beloved to come into it.

=== 5
The man replies to her invitation that he has come into his garden, and a voice, perhaps the friends or perhaps the poet, bids the lovers eat and drink deeply of love.

Then the mood changes, and another night scene begins, darker than the first. The woman is asleep, though her heart is awake, and she hears her beloved knocking, his hair wet with the night dew. She hesitates, having already undressed and washed her feet. By the time she rises to open the door, he has gone. She searches the city again, but this time the watchmen beat her and take her cloak.

She turns to the daughters of Jerusalem and asks them, if they find him, to tell him that she is sick with love. They answer with a question: what makes your beloved better than any other? Her reply fills the rest of the chapter, a portrait of him from head to foot, in images of gold, ivory, jewels and the cedars of Lebanon. This is my beloved, she ends, and this is my friend.

=== 6
The daughters of Jerusalem, stirred by the woman's description, offer to help her look for her beloved. She answers that he has gone down to his garden, and she repeats her declaration in a new order: I belong to my beloved and he belongs to me.

The man speaks again, and some of his words from chapter four return, the goats on Gilead and the sheep from the washing. But now he adds images of majesty. She is as beautiful as Tirzah, an ancient royal city of the north, as lovely as Jerusalem, as awe-inspiring as an army with banners. There may be sixty queens and eighty concubines, he says, but she is unique.

The closing lines of the chapter are among the hardest in the Song to interpret, and it is not always clear who is speaking. They end with a call to come back, and with the only place in the poem where the woman is called by a name of any kind, the Shulammite. Its meaning is uncertain. It may refer to a place, or it may be a feminine form of the name Solomon.

=== 7
Picking up from the call to the Shulammite, the man offers his third portrait of her, and this time he begins with her feet and moves upwards. Again the images are drawn from the land, the pools of Heshbon east of the Jordan, a tower facing Damascus, the height of Mount Carmel by the sea. He compares her stature to a palm tree, and says that he will climb it.

Then the woman answers. I belong to my beloved, she says, and his desire is for me. Some readers have heard in these words a quiet echo of the garden in Genesis, where desire was spoken of in the language of judgement, here restored to mutual delight.

The chapter closes with her invitation. She asks her beloved to go with her into the countryside, to spend the night among the wildflowers and rise early to see whether the vines have budded and the pomegranates are in bloom. There, she says, she will give him her love.

=== 8
The Song's final chapter gathers up voices and images heard before. The woman wishes her beloved were like a brother, so that she could kiss him in public without shame, and bring him to her mother's house. For the third and last time she charges the daughters of Jerusalem not to awaken love until the time is right. Then a voice asks, as it did in chapter three, who is coming up from the wilderness, and this time it is the woman, leaning on her beloved.

Here the poem reaches its summit. Set me as a seal over your heart, she says, for love is as strong as death, a blazing flame that many waters cannot quench and no wealth can buy.

The last part of the chapter brings in new voices. The woman's brothers speak of their little sister and how they will guard her, and she answers that she is a wall, and has found peace in her beloved's eyes. She contrasts Solomon's great vineyard, rented out to tenants, with her own vineyard, which is hers to give. The Song ends with her beloved asking to hear her voice, and her reply, calling him to come away like a gazelle on the mountains of spices.
