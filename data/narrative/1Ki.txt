=== intro
First Kings opens in a bedroom in Jerusalem. King David, the warrior who united the tribes and took the city for his capital, is now old and cannot keep warm. Outside the palace, one of his sons is already holding a feast to celebrate his own accession. The question that hung over the last years of Second Samuel, who will sit on David's throne after him, is about to be answered.

The book follows directly from Samuel, and in the Hebrew Bible First and Second Kings were a single work, divided later into two scrolls. Together they cover about four hundred years, from David's death a little before nine hundred and seventy years before Christ to the fall of Jerusalem to Babylon. The author is not named. Jewish tradition linked the books with the prophet Jeremiah, but the account was completed during the exile, since its last scene takes place in Babylon, and whoever shaped it was looking back over the whole history of the kings. The writer drew on older records and names them from time to time, the book of the acts of Solomon and the chronicles of the kings of Israel and of Judah, sending readers there for details he chose to leave out.

That choice matters. Kings is not a complete political history. Some reigns that were important to the wider world are passed over in a few sentences, while others are told at length because of what they reveal about Israel's faithfulness to God. Each king is measured by a single question: did he do what was right in the eyes of the Lord, like his father David, or did he lead the people into the worship of other gods? Running through the book is the voice of the prophets, men who stand before kings with the word of the Lord, and whose predictions are later noted as fulfilled.

The first eleven chapters belong to Solomon, David's son by Bathsheba. He comes to the throne through a palace struggle, secures it with hard measures, and then asks God for wisdom to govern. The kingdom he inherits from David stretches from the Euphrates to the border of Egypt, and under Solomon it enjoys peace, trade and fame. His greatest work is the temple in Jerusalem, built with cedar from Lebanon and gold from distant lands, the house for the Lord's name that David was not permitted to build. The books of Proverbs, Ecclesiastes and the Song of Songs are linked with his name. But the same chapters also record the heavy labour that built his projects, his many foreign wives, and the turning of his heart in old age to their gods.

After Solomon's death, in chapter twelve, the kingdom breaks in two. The ten northern tribes reject his son Rehoboam and follow Jeroboam, a former official of Solomon's, and they keep the name Israel. Rehoboam is left with Judah and the small tribe of Benjamin, ruling from Jerusalem. From here the listener needs to keep two kingdoms in mind. In the south, Judah keeps the line of David, one dynasty from start to finish. In the north, Israel has no fixed royal house; its throne passes by assassination and coup from one family to another, and its capital moves from Shechem to Tirzah and finally to Samaria. Jeroboam sets up golden calves at Bethel and Dan, so that his people need not go to Jerusalem to worship, and the writer judges every northern king after him by whether he turned from the sins of Jeroboam.

The writer moves back and forth between the two kingdoms, dating each king's accession by the year of the king reigning in the other. It can feel like a tangle of names, but the pattern is steady: an opening note on when a king began to reign and how long, a verdict on his faithfulness, the events chosen for telling, and a closing note on his death and burial.

The last part of the book slows down dramatically. In the north, Omri founds a powerful dynasty and builds Samaria, and his son Ahab marries Jezebel, a princess of Sidon, who brings with her the worship of Baal. Against them stands the prophet Elijah, a rough figure from Gilead east of the Jordan, who appears without introduction and declares a drought. The contest between Elijah and the house of Ahab, on Mount Carmel and beyond, fills most of the closing chapters. The book ends with Ahab's death in battle against the Arameans and the reigns of the next kings in both kingdoms. Elijah's story, and the long decline of both kingdoms, will continue in Second Kings.

=== 1
Many years have passed since the end of Second Samuel. David is old and failing, and his servants bring a young woman from Shunem, in the north, named Abishag, to care for him. The king's weakness leaves the question of succession wide open. No rule yet decided which son would follow a king, and David has said nothing in public.

Amnon and Absalom are dead, and the eldest surviving son appears to be Adonijah, the son of Haggith. Like Absalom before him, he is handsome and ambitious, and he too acquires chariots, horsemen and fifty men to run ahead of him. The narrator adds that David never once corrected him. Adonijah has gathered powerful allies: Joab, the long-serving commander of the army, and Abiathar the priest, who has been with David since his fugitive days. Others stand apart, among them Zadok the priest, Benaiah the commander of the royal guard, and Nathan the prophet.

When Adonijah holds a great sacrificial feast at En-rogel, a spring just below Jerusalem, and leaves out these men and his brother Solomon, Nathan sees the danger at once. He goes to Bathsheba, Solomon's mother, with a plan. Within a single day, the city will hear two different celebrations.

=== 2
Solomon has been anointed at the Gihon spring and seated on his father's throne, and Adonijah, who clung to the horns of the altar for safety, has been sent home. Now David's life is ending. Like Moses and Joshua before him, he gives a final charge to his successor.

The first part of that charge looks back to the Law of Moses and to the promise God made to David's house. The second part is more personal and harder to hear. David reminds Solomon of unfinished business from the years of Second Samuel: Joab and the two commanders he murdered, Abner and Amasa; Barzillai of Gilead, who fed the king in exile; and Shimei, the Benjamite who cursed him on the road out of Jerusalem.

David dies and is buried in the City of David after reigning forty years. The rest of the chapter shows the new king making his position secure. Adonijah, Abiathar, Joab and Shimei each come to his attention in turn. Abiathar is sent away to his estate at Anathoth, a village just north of Jerusalem that will later be the home of the prophet Jeremiah, and the writer sees in his removal the fulfilment of the word spoken long ago against the house of Eli. By the chapter's close, the kingdom is firmly in Solomon's hand.

=== 3
Solomon's throne is secure, and now we see what kind of king he will be. The chapter begins with two notes that are easy to pass over. He makes a marriage alliance with Pharaoh, king of Egypt, a sign of how far Israel's standing has risen. And the people, along with Solomon himself, are still offering sacrifices at the high places, local hilltop shrines, because there is not yet a temple. The writer marks this as a shortcoming, and the high places will be measured against king after king in the chapters to come.

The greatest of these shrines is at Gibeon, a few miles north-west of Jerusalem, where, as Chronicles tells us, the old tabernacle of Moses' day then stood. There Solomon offers a thousand burnt offerings, and that night the Lord appears to him in a dream with an open invitation: ask for whatever you want. Solomon's answer, and God's response to it, set the tone for the opening years of his reign.

The chapter ends with a case brought before the new king by two women who live in the same house. It has become the most famous judgement in the Bible, and it shows all Israel that the wisdom Solomon asked for has been given.

=== 4
After the dream at Gibeon and the case of the two mothers, the writer steps back to show the shape of Solomon's government. This chapter reads like an administrative record, and in a sense it is one, but it also paints a picture of a kingdom at the height of its prosperity.

First comes the list of his chief officials. Some names carry over from David's court: Benaiah, now in command of the army, and Zadok the priest, whose son Azariah appears here as priest too. Two sons of a man named Nathan hold high office, quite possibly sons of the prophet who helped bring Solomon to the throne. Then come twelve district governors, each responsible for supplying the royal household for one month of the year. Their districts do not simply follow the old tribal boundaries, and Judah seems to be treated separately.

The second half of the chapter widens the view: a kingdom reaching from the Euphrates to the border of Egypt, a people eating and drinking and rejoicing, every man under his own vine and fig tree. It ends with Solomon's wisdom and fame, his three thousand proverbs and more than a thousand songs, and visitors coming from every nation to hear him.

=== 5
Solomon's kingdom is at peace, and he turns to the great work his father longed to do. In chapter seven of Second Samuel, the Lord told David that his son would build a house for his name. Now that son is on the throne.

The help he needs comes from the north. Hiram, king of Tyre, the wealthy Phoenician port on the Mediterranean coast, had been a friend of David and supplied cedar for his palace. The mountains of Lebanon behind Tyre and Sidon were famous across the ancient world for their great cedars, and the Phoenicians were the finest timber workers and seafarers of the age. Solomon proposes an arrangement, and Hiram responds warmly. The logs will be floated down the coast in rafts, and Solomon will pay in wheat and olive oil.

The rest of the chapter describes the workforce. Solomon conscripts tens of thousands of Israelites to work in Lebanon in monthly shifts, and many more to carry loads and quarry stone in the hills. The man in charge of this forced labour is Adoniram, and the resentment it stirred will one day help tear the kingdom apart.

=== 6
The timber is coming down the coast from Lebanon and the great stones are being cut in the quarries. Now the building begins. The writer marks the date with unusual care: the fourth year of Solomon's reign, the four hundred and eightieth year after Israel came out of Egypt. The temple is presented as the long-awaited end of the road that began at the exodus.

The measurements are given in cubits, a cubit being roughly the length of a forearm. The temple itself was not enormous, about thirty metres long and ten wide, because worshippers gathered in the courtyards outside. Its plan follows the pattern of the tabernacle in the wilderness, on a larger scale: a porch at the front, a main hall, and at the back a perfect cube, the inner sanctuary or Most Holy Place, where the ark of the covenant would rest beneath two great cherubim of gold-covered olive wood.

Inside, cedar panelling carved with gourds and flowers is overlaid with gold, so that not a single stone can be seen. In the middle of the building account comes a word from the Lord to Solomon, a reminder that what matters most is not the house but the obedience of the king who builds it. The work takes seven years.

=== 7
The temple is finished, but before the writer describes its furnishings he turns to Solomon's other building projects in Jerusalem. The contrast is quietly made: the temple took seven years, and the king's own palace took thirteen.

The palace complex had several parts. The largest was the House of the Forest of Lebanon, so named for its rows of cedar pillars, which seems to have served as a great hall and, as we later learn, an armoury. There was a colonnade, a Hall of Justice where Solomon sat to judge, his own residence, and a separate palace for his Egyptian queen.

Then the account returns to the temple and to a master craftsman in bronze, also called Huram, not the king of Tyre but a man of mixed descent, the son of a widow from the tribe of Naphtali and a father from Tyre. His work is described piece by piece: two great bronze pillars at the entrance, given the names Jachin and Boaz; an enormous bronze basin called the Sea, resting on twelve bronze oxen; and ten wheeled stands with basins. These bronze works will appear once more in the story, at the very end of Second Kings, and the listener may wish to remember them.

=== 8
The temple and all its furnishings are complete. Now Solomon gathers the elders of every tribe and family to Jerusalem for its dedication. It is the seventh month, the season of the autumn Feast of Tabernacles, when Israel remembered its years of living in tents in the wilderness.

The first task is to bring the ark of the covenant up from the City of David, the lower part of Jerusalem where David had placed it in a tent, to its new home on the higher ground to the north. The writer notes that the ark contains only the two stone tablets Moses placed in it at Horeb, the mountain also known as Sinai. When the ark is set in its place, something happens that recalls the day the tabernacle was completed in the wilderness, and the priests cannot continue their work.

Most of the chapter is given to Solomon's words: a blessing on the people, and then one of the longest prayers in the Bible, spoken with his hands spread out toward heaven. Solomon asks the Lord to hear whatever prayers are made toward this house, in war, drought, famine and plague, even from foreigners and even from captives in a distant land. Those who first read the finished book of Kings were themselves exiles, and they would have heard that last request with particular force.

=== 9
Some years have passed since the dedication. The temple and the palace are complete, after twenty years of building, and Solomon has done everything he set out to do. At this high point, the Lord appears to him a second time, as he did at Gibeon at the beginning of his reign.

The message answers Solomon's prayer. God has consecrated the house and will put his name there. But the promise comes with a solemn condition, addressed to Solomon and to his sons after him, and its warning is spelled out in vivid detail. The rest of the book of Kings will show how that warning came true.

The remainder of the chapter gathers notes on the wider reign. Solomon settles accounts with Hiram of Tyre by giving him twenty towns in Galilee, which do not please him. There is a record of the forced labour that built Solomon's projects, including the fortified cities of Hazor, Megiddo and Gezer, guarding the great roads through the land. Gezer, we learn, came to Solomon as a dowry when Pharaoh captured it and gave it to his daughter. And Solomon builds a fleet at Ezion-geber, at the head of the Gulf of Aqaba, which sails with Phoenician crews to a distant land of gold called Ophir, whose location is still uncertain.

=== 10
Solomon's fame is spreading far beyond Israel, and this chapter shows it at its height. It opens with a royal visitor: the queen of Sheba. Her kingdom was most likely in south-western Arabia, in what is now Yemen, a land grown rich on the trade in frankincense, myrrh and spices that travelled north by camel caravan. Some traditions place her in East Africa, across the Red Sea. Either way, she has come a very long way.

She comes, the writer says, to test Solomon with hard questions, and she brings a great caravan of gifts. What she sees in Jerusalem leaves her breathless. Her words in praise of Solomon's God are among the warmest spoken by any foreigner in the Old Testament, and Jesus would later recall her journey in his own preaching.

After her departure the chapter turns to Solomon's wealth: shields of beaten gold, an ivory throne with lions on its steps, ships that bring back gold, silver, ivory and apes, and horses and chariots traded from Egypt and from Kue, in what is now southern Turkey. The listener who knows the law of Moses may remember that it warned Israel's kings against gathering many horses from Egypt, many wives and great amounts of silver and gold. Two of those three are described here. The third comes next.

=== 11
The splendour of chapter ten is still fresh when this chapter opens with a single word that changes its direction: however. Solomon loved many foreign women. Pharaoh's daughter has been mentioned before; now we hear of wives from Moab, Ammon, Edom, Sidon and the Hittites, many of them no doubt political marriages sealing alliances. The law had warned that such marriages would turn Israel's heart to other gods, and in Solomon's old age that is exactly what happens. On the hill east of Jerusalem, facing the temple he built, he builds shrines for the gods of his wives.

The Lord's word to Solomon follows, announcing that the kingdom will be torn away, though not in his own lifetime, and not entirely. Then the writer introduces three adversaries. Two come from beyond Israel's borders: Hadad, a prince of Edom who had grown up in exile in Egypt, and Rezon, who seized power in Damascus. The third comes from within.

He is Jeroboam son of Nebat, a capable young man of the tribe of Ephraim, whom Solomon put in charge of the forced labour drawn from the northern tribes. On the road outside Jerusalem he meets a prophet, Ahijah of Shiloh, who is wearing a new cloak. What Ahijah does with it, and what he says, will shape the history of the next two centuries.

=== 12
Solomon is dead after forty years on the throne. In Judah his son Rehoboam's succession is not in doubt, but the northern tribes have yet to accept him. So Rehoboam goes north to Shechem, the ancient town in the hills of Ephraim where Joshua once gathered the tribes to renew their covenant with the Lord. The people's representatives meet him there, and with them comes Jeroboam, back from his refuge in Egypt.

They bring one request: lighten the heavy yoke your father laid on us. Rehoboam takes three days and consults two groups of advisers, the old men who served Solomon and the young men he grew up with. His answer divides the nation. Listeners who remember Sheba's rebellion in Second Samuel will recognise the cry that goes up in reply.

From this point on there are two kingdoms. In the north, the ten tribes keep the name Israel and make Jeroboam their king. In the south, Rehoboam rules Judah, with Benjamin, from Jerusalem. Jeroboam now faces a problem: the temple, and the great feasts, are in his rival's capital. His solution, set up at Bethel in the south of his kingdom and at Dan in the far north, will be called the sin of Jeroboam for the rest of the book, and his words to the people echo those once spoken over Aaron's golden calf at Sinai.

=== 13
Jeroboam is standing beside his new altar at Bethel, about to burn incense at the feast he has devised. Bethel was an ancient holy place, the spot where Jacob once dreamed of a stairway to heaven, and it lay only a few miles north of the border with Judah.

Into this scene comes a man of God from Judah, sent by the word of the Lord, and he speaks not to the king but to the altar. His message names a king of David's line who will one day come to this place. The name is Josiah, and the listener will meet him three hundred years later, near the end of Second Kings. The prophecy is confirmed at once by a sign, and Jeroboam himself is caught up in it.

The man of God has been given strict instructions about his journey home, and the rest of the chapter is one of the strangest stories in Kings. It involves an old prophet who lives in Bethel, a meal, a lie, and a lion on the road. It is a sobering account of what it means to carry the word of the Lord, and of how seriously that word is to be taken. The chapter ends by returning to Jeroboam, and to the verdict that will hang over his house.

=== 14
Jeroboam has been warned at his own altar, and he has not changed course. Now trouble comes to his own household. His son Abijah falls ill, and Jeroboam sends his wife, in disguise, to the one prophet he trusts to tell the truth: Ahijah, the old man from Shiloh who first promised him the kingdom. Jeroboam's court is now at Tirzah, a town in the hills north-east of Shechem, which will serve as the northern capital for the next few reigns.

Ahijah is nearly blind, but the Lord has told him who is coming. His message reaches beyond the sick boy to the house of Jeroboam, and to the whole northern kingdom, which will one day be scattered beyond the Euphrates. It is the first time Kings speaks of exile, two centuries before it came.

The second half of the chapter crosses the border to Judah. Here we see for the first time the pattern the writer will use for every reign from now on: the king's age, the length of his reign, his mother's name, a verdict on his faithfulness, some selected events, and his burial. Rehoboam's reign is summed up briefly, and it includes an invasion by Shishak, king of Egypt, the pharaoh who once sheltered Jeroboam, whose own inscription recording the campaign still survives.

=== 15
The writer now moves briskly between the two kingdoms, and it helps to name the kings as they come.

In Judah, Rehoboam is followed by his son Abijam, who reigns only three years. Then comes Asa, who reigns for forty-one, the first king of Judah to be praised for doing what was right in the eyes of the Lord, as David had done. He clears away idols and even removes his own grandmother from her position as queen mother. The high places, the writer notes, still remain.

In the north, Jeroboam's son Nadab reigns for two years before he is assassinated by Baasha, a man of the tribe of Issachar, while the army is besieging the Philistine town of Gibbethon. With this the pattern of the northern kingdom is set: one family seizes the throne from another, and Ahijah's words against Jeroboam's house come true. Meanwhile Baasha and Asa are at war. Baasha fortifies Ramah, only a few miles north of Jerusalem, and Asa turns for help to Ben-hadad, king of Aram in Damascus. It is the first of many times that the Arameans of Damascus will be drawn into Israel's affairs.

=== 16
Baasha now rules the northern kingdom from Tirzah, but his house will not last much longer than Jeroboam's. This chapter moves quickly through a time of turmoil in the north, while in Judah King Asa's long reign continues.

A prophet named Jehu son of Hanani brings Baasha a message very like the one Ahijah brought to Jeroboam. Baasha's son Elah reigns for two years and is murdered while drunk by Zimri, one of his chariot commanders. Zimri's reign lasts only seven days. The army proclaims its own commander, Omri, as king, and after a period of civil war against a rival named Tibni, Omri takes the throne.

Omri receives only a few verses, but he was one of the most important kings of the north. He moves the capital to a new city on a hill he buys: Samaria, which will remain Israel's capital until the end of the kingdom. For generations afterwards, Assyrian records called Israel the house of Omri. Then the chapter introduces his son Ahab, and with him Jezebel, the daughter of the king of Sidon, a devoted worshipper of the Phoenician storm god Baal. In Samaria Ahab builds a temple to Baal, and in the very next verse a prophet walks into the story.

=== 17
Ahab and Jezebel have brought the worship of Baal into the heart of Israel, with a temple in Samaria itself. Now, with no introduction beyond his name and his homeland, a prophet appears before the king. He is Elijah the Tishbite, from Gilead, the rough hill country east of the Jordan. His name means my God is the Lord, and his first words are a challenge.

Baal was worshipped as the god of storm and rain, the one who made the land fertile. Elijah announces that there will be neither dew nor rain except at his word. The contest between the Lord and Baal has begun, and it will be fought on Baal's own ground.

Then Elijah disappears from Ahab's sight. The Lord sends him first to hide by a brook east of the Jordan, and later, when it dries up, to a town called Zarephath on the coast near Sidon. That is Jezebel's homeland, the heart of Baal's territory, and there Elijah is taken in by a poor widow who is down to her last handful of flour. Jesus would later remind his hearers in Nazareth that, of all the widows in Israel, Elijah was sent to this one. The chapter tells of her household, and of a crisis that tests her faith in the man of God under her roof.

=== 18
The drought has lasted into its third year, and the famine in Samaria is severe. The word of the Lord comes to Elijah again: go and show yourself to Ahab, and I will send rain.

Before Elijah meets the king, he meets Obadiah, who manages Ahab's palace. He is not the prophet whose short book appears later in the Bible, but a court official who has quietly feared the Lord all his life and has risked his own safety to protect the Lord's prophets from Jezebel. Ahab and Obadiah are out searching the land for any grass to keep the royal horses alive, and Obadiah's fear when he sees Elijah shows how hard Ahab has been hunting him.

Then comes the most famous scene in Elijah's life. He calls for all Israel, and for the four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal, to gather on Mount Carmel, a long ridge running down to the Mediterranean near what is now Haifa. Carmel lay on the border between Israel and Phoenicia, and it may already have been a holy place for both. Elijah puts a simple question to the people about whom they will follow, and proposes a test. The chapter ends with clouds rising from the sea, and Elijah running ahead of Ahab's chariot to the royal city of Jezreel.

=== 19
After the fire on Carmel and the rain that ended the drought, it might seem that Baal's worship in Israel is finished. It is not. Ahab tells Jezebel what Elijah has done, and she sends him a message: by this time tomorrow, you will be dead.

Elijah runs. He goes south out of Israel altogether, through Judah to Beersheba on the edge of the desert, and then alone into the wilderness beyond. The man who stood against hundreds of prophets now lies down under a desert shrub and asks to die. What follows is gentle and unexpected, and it leads him on a journey of forty days and nights to Horeb, the mountain of God, also called Sinai, where the Lord once spoke to Moses. Like Moses before him, Elijah will stand on the mountain as the Lord passes by, though not in the way he might expect.

Elijah is given a new task, to anoint three men: a king for Aram, a king for Israel, and a prophet to succeed him. Their names will matter in Second Kings. The chapter ends with the prophet, a farmer named Elisha, ploughing in his father's fields, when Elijah passes by and throws his cloak around him.

=== 20
Elijah has gone to anoint his successor, and he steps out of the story for a while. The scene shifts to war. Ben-hadad, king of Aram in Damascus, marches south with a great army and thirty-two allied kings, probably local rulers under his authority, and lays siege to Samaria. Ben-hadad was a name borne by more than one king of Damascus, and it is not certain whether this is the same ruler whom Asa once paid to attack Israel.

Ben-hadad's demands begin as harsh and become humiliating, and Ahab, backed by his elders, finally refuses. Then something unexpected happens. A prophet of the Lord, unnamed, comes to Ahab with a promise of victory. Despite everything Ahab has done, the Lord has not abandoned Israel, and he intends Ahab to know who he is.

There are two campaigns in this chapter, the first at Samaria and the second a year later at Aphek, where the Arameans choose to fight on the plain because they believe Israel's God rules only the hills. The decisive moment, though, comes after the second battle, when Ahab must decide what to do with the defeated king of Damascus. The chapter ends with another prophet and another acted parable, of the kind Nathan once told David, and with Ahab going home to Samaria sullen and angry.

=== 21
Ahab has gone home sullen and angry after the prophet's rebuke, and the next chapter finds him in the same mood again, this time over something much smaller. Next to his palace in Jezreel, the royal city in the fertile valley north of Samaria, lies a vineyard belonging to a man named Naboth. Ahab wants it for a vegetable garden, and he makes what seems a fair offer.

Naboth's refusal is not stubbornness. In Israel, family land was an inheritance from the Lord, divided among the tribes in Joshua's day and meant to stay within the family for ever. Naboth will not sell what belongs to his fathers. Ahab accepts the answer, but sulks. Jezebel, raised in a royal court where a king's will was law, does not accept it at all, and she knows how to use Israel's own institutions to get what she wants.

The chapter is short and terrible, and it brings Elijah back into the story. He finds Ahab in the vineyard itself. The words he speaks there will be remembered, and fulfilled, in the chapters and years to come. Then Ahab responds in a way that surprises even the reader, and the Lord's answer to that response is the chapter's final word.

=== 22
Three years of peace have passed between Israel and Aram, the treaty Ahab made with Ben-hadad seemingly holding. The Bible does not mention it, but Assyrian records tell us that around this time Ahab and the king of Damascus fought side by side against the Assyrian king Shalmaneser at Qarqar in Syria. Assyria, the great power from the Tigris, will loom ever larger over the rest of Kings.

Now the old rivalry returns. The town of Ramoth-gilead, east of the Jordan, is still in Aramean hands, and Ahab wants it back. He has a new ally: Jehoshaphat, the son of Asa, king of Judah. After decades of war between the two kingdoms, the royal houses of Israel and Judah are now at peace, and as Second Kings will show, they have been joined by marriage. Jehoshaphat is willing to fight, but he wants first to hear from the Lord.

Four hundred prophets assure the kings of victory. Jehoshaphat asks whether there is not another prophet, and Ahab admits there is one, Micaiah son of Imlah, whom he hates. The chapter ends with a battle, the fulfilment of a prophecy, and brief notes on Jehoshaphat's reign in Judah and on Ahab's son Ahaziah, who now takes the throne in Samaria. His story, and Elijah's, continue directly in Second Kings.
