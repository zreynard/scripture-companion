=== intro
Second Peter is a farewell. The writer introduces himself as Simon Peter, a servant and apostle of Jesus Christ, and he says that he knows his death is near, as the Lord Jesus had made clear to him. The Gospel of John records that moment, beside the Sea of Galilee after the resurrection, when Jesus told Peter that one day others would lead him where he did not want to go, words John explains as a sign of the death by which Peter would glorify God. Early Christian tradition places that death in Rome, under the emperor Nero, in the middle of the sixties of the first century. This letter is Peter's last word before it came.

In the ancient world, a farewell speech was a recognised form. A great figure, near the end of life, gathered his followers to remind them of what he had taught, to warn them of dangers ahead, and to urge them to hold fast after he was gone. Jacob blessing his sons, Moses addressing Israel on the plains of Moab, Paul speaking to the elders of Ephesus: Scripture has several such scenes. Second Peter belongs with them. Its key word is remembering. Peter wants his readers to be able to recall these things at all times after his departure.

Towards the end he calls this his second letter to the same readers, a reference to First Peter, and so to the churches of Asia Minor. Its greeting is broad, addressed to all who have received a faith as precious as that of the apostles.

What prompted it is clear. False teachers have appeared, or soon will, among the very people Peter is writing to. They promise freedom, yet they live for appetite and greed, and they treat the moral teaching of the apostles with contempt. Most of all, they mock the promise that Jesus will return. Generations have died, they say, and everything continues as it always has.

The letter answers in three movements. The first chapter calls the readers to grow in a faith that shows itself in character, and grounds the apostles' message in what Peter himself saw and heard on the mountain where Jesus was transfigured. The second denounces the false teachers in fierce language, with examples of judgment drawn from Israel's Scriptures: fallen angels, the flood, Sodom and Gomorrah, and the prophet Balaam, and much of it runs closely parallel to the letter of Jude. The third answers the scoffers, insisting that God's apparent delay is patience, and looking ahead to the day of the Lord and a new heaven and a new earth. Near the end, Peter mentions the letters of our beloved brother Paul, some parts of which, he admits, are hard to understand.

=== 1
The opening words set the tone for the whole letter. Peter writes to people who share the same precious faith as the apostles, and he wants them to know what they have already been given: everything needed for life and godliness, and promises great enough to make them partakers of the divine nature.

From there he builds a ladder of qualities, each added to the last, beginning with faith and ending with love. Without them, he warns, faith grows short-sighted and forgetful.

Then the letter becomes personal. Peter explains why he keeps reminding them of what they already know. His tent, as he calls his body, will soon be folded away, and he wants them to remember after he has gone. That leads him back to the mountain where he, James and John saw Jesus in his glory and heard the voice of the Father calling him his beloved Son. The apostles' message is no clever myth, he says; they were eyewitnesses. The chapter ends with the prophetic Scriptures, a lamp shining in a dark place until the day dawns, and the reminder that their message came from God.

=== 2
Peter has just spoken of the true prophets, who spoke from God. Now he turns to their counterfeits. Israel had false prophets, he says, and the church will have false teachers, men who slip in destructive ideas, deny the Master who bought them, and exploit people out of greed.

Much of this chapter closely resembles the letter of Jude. Peter draws a series of examples from Israel's earliest history to show that God knows how to judge the wicked and rescue the godly: the angels who sinned, the ancient world swept away in the flood while Noah and his family were kept safe, and the cities of Sodom and Gomorrah reduced to ashes while Lot was rescued. He then compares the false teachers to Balaam son of Beor, the prophet in the book of Numbers who was hired to curse Israel and was rebuked by his own donkey.

The language that follows is some of the harshest in the New Testament. These teachers are springs without water and mists driven by a storm. They promise freedom while being slaves themselves. The chapter ends with two blunt proverbs about returning to the mud.

=== 3
With the false teachers exposed, Peter turns to the claim that lies at the heart of their mockery. This is his second letter to them, he says, and both are reminders of what the prophets foretold and the apostles passed on.

The scoffers ask where the promise of Christ's coming is, since everything carries on as it has from the beginning. Peter answers from Genesis. The world was not always unchanged; by God's word it was formed out of water, and by water it was overwhelmed in the flood. The same word now holds the present world for a day of fire. He reminds them that, with the Lord, a day is like a thousand years and a thousand years like a day, an echo of the ninetieth psalm, and that what looks like slowness is patience, giving time for repentance.

The chapter looks ahead to the day of the Lord coming like a thief, and beyond it to a new heaven and a new earth. In his closing words Peter mentions his beloved brother Paul, warns against those who twist his letters, and ends with praise to Christ.
