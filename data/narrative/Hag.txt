=== intro
With Haggai the Twelve cross the great divide of Israel's history. Zephaniah spoke while the temple of Solomon still stood. Between his book and this one, Jerusalem has fallen to Nebuchadnezzar, the temple has been burned, and the people of Judah have spent a lifetime in exile in Babylon. Now Babylon itself has gone, and the Persians rule from the Aegean to the borders of India. The listener has heard this part of the story in the book of Ezra, and Haggai belongs inside it.

In five hundred and thirty-nine before Christ Cyrus the Persian captured Babylon. The next year he issued a decree allowing the exiles to return home and rebuild the house of the Lord in Jerusalem. Some tens of thousands went back. Within a short time they rebuilt the altar and laid the foundation of the new temple, and Ezra records that the old men who remembered Solomon's temple wept aloud while the young shouted for joy. Then the work stalled. Neighbouring peoples opposed it and complained to the Persian court, harvests were poor, and the returned exiles turned to rebuilding their own homes and livelihoods. For some sixteen years the foundation lay untouched.

Haggai's book is dated with a precision unusual in the prophets. All four of his messages fall within a few months of the second year of Darius king of Persia, five hundred and twenty before Christ. Darius had come to the throne after a violent struggle, and the first years of his reign were filled with revolts across the empire. It was a time when thrones seemed to shake, and Haggai's language reflects it. The book of Ezra names him, together with Zechariah, as the prophets whose words got the building moving again.

Two leaders stand at the centre of the book. Zerubbabel son of Shealtiel is the governor of Judah, a province of the Persian empire, and he is a descendant of King Jehoiachin, the king of Judah taken captive to Babylon. In him the royal line of David survives. Joshua son of Jehozadak is the high priest, the man called Jeshua in the book of Ezra. Haggai addresses them both, and the remnant of the people with them.

Of Haggai himself nothing is known beyond his title, the prophet. He speaks plainly and practically. Where earlier prophets condemned idolatry and injustice, Haggai's concern is neglect: the people say the time has not come to rebuild the Lord's house, while they live in panelled houses of their own. Yet his book is more than a building appeal. It looks beyond the modest temple taking shape to a day when the Lord will shake the nations and fill his house with glory.

Haggai's prophecy had results that can be measured. Within weeks the work resumed, and four years later, in the sixth year of Darius, the second temple was completed and dedicated. It would stand, enlarged and rebuilt by Herod the Great, until the time of Jesus.

=== 1
The first message is dated to the first day of the sixth month in the second year of Darius, late summer of five hundred and twenty before Christ. It was the New Moon, a day when people gathered for worship, and the word goes to Zerubbabel the governor and Joshua the high priest.

The people have been saying that the time has not yet come to rebuild the house of the Lord. The Lord's reply turns their words back on them: is it a time for you to live in panelled houses, while this house lies in ruins? Twice Haggai tells them to consider their ways. Their harvests are thin, and their wages vanish as if into a purse with holes. The drought on their fields, he says, is bound up with the neglect of the Lord's house.

What follows is rare in the prophets. The leaders and the people listen, and they obey. The Lord stirs the spirit of Zerubbabel, Joshua and the whole remnant, and little more than three weeks later the work begins.

=== 2
Three more messages follow over the next months. The first comes on the last day of the Feast of Tabernacles, the festival at which Solomon had once dedicated his temple. A few of the oldest people had seen that temple before the Babylonians burned it, more than sixty years earlier, and to them the new building seems like nothing. The Lord tells them to be strong and work, for his Spirit remains among them. He will shake the nations, and the glory of this house will be greater than the former.

Two months later Haggai asks the priests for a ruling on ritual purity. Holiness does not pass from one thing to another by touch, but defilement does, and so it has been with the people's work. Yet from this day, the Lord says, he will bless them.

The same day a final word goes to Zerubbabel alone: the Lord will make him like his signet ring. Jeremiah had once said that even if King Jehoiachin, Zerubbabel's forebear, were the signet ring on the Lord's hand, he would pull him off. Now the image is reversed.
