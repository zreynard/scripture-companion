=== intro
This letter comes from a man whose story the listener already knows well. Simon was a fisherman from Galilee, working the lake with his brother Andrew, when Jesus called him and gave him a new name, Peter, which means rock. He was there for almost everything in the Gospels: the miracles, the mountain of transfiguration, the garden of Gethsemane. He was also the disciple who, on the night of the arrest, three times denied knowing Jesus, and who was restored by the risen Lord beside the same lake where he had first been called. In the book of Acts, Peter preaches at Pentecost, leads the young church in Jerusalem, survives prison, and opens the door to the first Gentile believers in the house of Cornelius. Then Acts lets him slip from view, and the story follows Paul instead.

Early Christian tradition fills in something of what came next. Peter travelled beyond Judea, and in the end he came to Rome, where he was put to death under the emperor Nero, probably in the middle of the sixties of the first century. This letter seems to come from those last years. At its close Peter sends greetings from the church in Babylon. The great city of Mesopotamia was by then largely a ruin, and most readers have understood the name as a veiled way of speaking of Rome, the capital of a world power, just as the book of Revelation will later do. He mentions two companions. Silvanus, the Silas who travelled with Paul, either helped to write the letter or carried it, and Mark, whom Peter calls his son, is the John Mark of Acts, later remembered as the writer of the Gospel that draws on Peter's preaching. Some scholars think the letter was written by a follower in Peter's name after his death, but the letter presents itself as Peter's own, written by an old apostle to churches under strain.

It is addressed to believers scattered across five regions of Asia Minor, in what is now Turkey: Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia and Bithynia. Together they cover a huge sweep of country from the Black Sea coast through the central highlands to the Aegean, and the order in which they are named may trace the route the letter's messenger was to follow. Paul had planted churches in Galatia and Asia, but he was once prevented from entering Bithynia, and how the gospel reached the rest is not recorded. Acts does mention visitors from Pontus, Cappadocia and Asia among the crowd in Jerusalem who heard Peter preach on the day of Pentecost.

Peter calls his readers the elect who are exiles of the Dispersion. That was the language of the Jewish people scattered among the nations, but most of these readers seem to have been Gentiles. Peter speaks of the empty way of life they inherited from their ancestors, of the time they once spent doing what pagans do, and of a time when they were not a people at all. He takes the titles of Israel and gives them to these new believers. They are foreigners and exiles now, not because they have moved house, but because their faith has made them strangers in their own towns.

That is the heart of their situation. They have stopped joining in the feasts, the drinking and the temple worship that bound ancient society together, and their neighbours are offended. They are slandered as evildoers, insulted for the name of Christ, and in some cases mistreated by masters or husbands who do not share their faith. There is no sign yet of an empire-wide campaign against them. Their suffering is the daily hostility of the street, the household and the marketplace, though Peter warns of a fiery trial that has come upon them.

Into that situation Peter writes what he calls a brief letter of encouragement. It opens with praise for the living hope God has given through the resurrection of Jesus, an inheritance kept safe in heaven. From there it moves to the kind of life that hope produces: holiness, love within the community, and honourable conduct among outsiders. Peter speaks to citizens and rulers, slaves and masters, wives and husbands, always with an eye on how the watching world will see them. Running through it all is the example of Christ, who suffered unjustly and did not retaliate, and whose suffering was followed by glory. Peter quotes the Old Testament often, especially Isaiah and the Psalms, and his words recall the Gospel scenes he himself witnessed.

In the order of the New Testament, the two letters bearing Peter's name follow James, and they stand among the general letters, written to believers across a wide region rather than to one church. This first letter is the voice of the apostle who once failed under pressure, now strengthening others who are facing pressure of their own.

=== 1
Peter opens with a greeting that already tells his readers who they are: chosen by God, and yet living as exiles across the provinces of Asia Minor. Then, before saying a word about their troubles, he breaks into praise.

The opening paragraph is a single long outpouring in Greek, and its subject is hope. God has given these believers new birth through the resurrection of Jesus, and an inheritance that cannot perish or fade, kept for them in heaven. Their present trials are real, but Peter compares them to the fire that refines gold, and he reminds them that the Old Testament prophets searched and longed to understand the salvation they now possess. Even angels long to look into these things.

From that hope Peter draws a call to live differently. He quotes the command God gave Israel in Leviticus, be holy, because I am holy, and speaks of their redemption not with silver or gold but with the blood of Christ, a lamb without blemish. The chapter ends with words from Isaiah: all flesh is like grass, but the word of the Lord stands forever.

=== 2
Chapter one ended with the enduring word of God. Peter now pictures his readers as newborn babies, craving that word as an infant craves milk.

Then the image changes to building. Jesus is the living stone, rejected by people but chosen by God, and believers are living stones built into a spiritual house. Peter weaves together three passages from Isaiah and the Psalms about a cornerstone and a stone that makes people stumble. He had quoted one of them years before, standing before the council in Jerusalem, and his own name, rock, gives the image a personal edge. He goes on to give these mostly Gentile believers the titles once spoken over Israel at Mount Sinai: a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people belonging to God.

The second half turns to life among outsiders who slander them. Peter speaks of honouring the emperor and his governors, and then addresses household slaves, who made up a large part of many early churches, some of them serving harsh masters. His model for suffering unjustly is Christ himself, and he describes the crucifixion in words drawn from Isaiah's song of the suffering servant.

=== 3
Peter continues to speak to those in difficult households, and now he turns to wives. In the Roman world a wife was expected to follow her husband's religion, so a woman who had become a believer while her husband had not was in an awkward and exposed position. Peter hopes such husbands may be won over without a word, by the conduct of their wives, and he points back to Sarah and the holy women of Israel's past. A shorter word to husbands follows.

He widens his address to everyone, quoting a psalm of David about seeking peace and keeping the tongue from evil, and he takes up the question of suffering for doing good. Believers should be ready to explain their hope to anyone who asks, gently and with respect.

The chapter ends with one of the most debated passages in the New Testament. Speaking of Christ's death and resurrection, Peter mentions his proclamation to the spirits in prison who disobeyed in the days of Noah, and he draws a comparison between the flood and baptism. Interpreters have read these lines in several ways. The chapter closes with Christ ascended, and every power subject to him.

=== 4
Chapter three ended with Christ, who suffered and is now exalted. Peter takes that as his starting point: since Christ suffered in his body, his followers should arm themselves with the same resolve.

He describes, frankly, the life his readers have left behind, the drinking parties, the revels and the idolatry that filled the social calendar of a Roman town. Their old friends are surprised that they no longer join in, and they respond with abuse. Peter reminds his readers that those friends will give an account to God, who judges the living and the dead.

Then he speaks of life inside the church, with the end of all things near: prayer, deep love, hospitality without grumbling, and gifts used in service. A brief burst of praise follows, ending in amen, and some have wondered whether the letter once paused here.

The last section returns to suffering with new intensity. Peter speaks of a fiery trial, and of suffering as a Christian, one of only three places in the New Testament where that name, first used of the believers in Antioch, appears.

=== 5
The letter draws to a close with a personal appeal. Peter addresses the elders of the churches, the leaders responsible for each congregation, and he speaks not as a commanding apostle but as a fellow elder and a witness of the sufferings of Christ. His charge to them is to shepherd the flock of God willingly and humbly, as examples rather than masters. It is hard not to hear an echo of the morning beside the Sea of Galilee when the risen Jesus told Peter to feed his sheep.

He then turns to the younger members of the church, and to everyone, urging humility. Like James, he quotes the line from Proverbs that God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble. He pictures the devil as a roaring lion on the prowl, and reminds his readers that believers across the world are suffering as they are.

The closing greetings are brief. Peter names Silvanus, through whom he has written, sends greetings from the church in Babylon and from Mark, whom he calls his son, and ends with a word of peace.
