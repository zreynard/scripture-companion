=== intro
The book of Daniel opens with a defeat. In the third year of Jehoiakim, king of Judah, Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon comes to Jerusalem, and some of the sacred vessels of the temple are carried off to the treasury of a Babylonian god. With them go a number of young men from Judah's royal family and nobility, taken to be trained for service in the court of their conqueror. One of them is Daniel.

The year is about six hundred and five before Christ. Nebuchadnezzar, the crown prince of Babylon, had just defeated the Egyptian army at Carchemish on the Euphrates, and within months he would be king. Babylon was now the great power of the Near East, heir to the Assyrian empire it had helped to destroy. This first deportation from Judah was small, a matter of hostages and promising youths. A larger one would follow eight years later, when the prophet Ezekiel was taken, and in five hundred and eighty-six the city and temple would be destroyed. Daniel, then, belongs to the same generation as Ezekiel, and Ezekiel appears to mention him by name. But where Ezekiel lived among the exiles by a canal in the countryside, Daniel lived in the capital itself, at the centre of imperial power.

The book follows him for about seventy years, through the reigns of several kings. Nebuchadnezzar, the most powerful ruler of his age, dominates the early chapters. Then comes Belshazzar, the last prince of Babylon, who was the son and co-regent of King Nabonidus, though Daniel speaks only of him. Then, after Babylon falls to the Medes and Persians in five hundred and thirty-nine, the scene shifts to the new Persian empire, with a ruler called Darius the Mede, whose identity in other records is still debated, and Cyrus, the Persian king who allowed the exiles to return home. Daniel is last seen in the third year of Cyrus, an old man still in Babylon.

The book falls into two halves. The first six chapters are stories, told about Daniel and his three friends, Hananiah, Mishael and Azariah, better known by their Babylonian names Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego. They are stories of the court: a test of diet, a king's dream that no one else can interpret, a golden statue and a blazing furnace, a king driven mad, a feast interrupted by writing on the wall, and a den of lions. In each, faithful Jews living in a foreign land face pressure to compromise, and in each the God of Israel shows himself greater than the kings of the earth.

The last six chapters are visions, told by Daniel himself. He sees four great beasts rising from the sea, a ram and a goat, a heavenly messenger who explains the seventy weeks, and finally a long revelation of wars between kings of the north and the south. These visions survey the empires that will follow Babylon, and they look ahead to a time of great suffering for God's people and to the final establishment of God's own kingdom. Some of the kingdoms are named in the text itself: Babylon, Media and Persia, and Greece. The visions are dated within Daniel's own lifetime, and they are not all in order with the stories. The first two visions come from the reign of Belshazzar, before the events of chapter five.

The book has another curious feature. It is written in two languages. It begins in Hebrew, switches to Aramaic, the international language of the empire, in the middle of chapter two, and returns to Hebrew at chapter eight. The reason is not certain, but the Aramaic section covers the chapters most concerned with the nations and their kings.

Its date and authorship are debated. The traditional view holds that the book comes from Daniel himself in the sixth century before Christ, while many modern scholars date it, or its final form, to the second century, during the persecution of the Jews under the Greek king Antiochus the Fourth, whose reign the later visions describe in detail. The book's message is the same either way: the kingdoms of this world rise and fall, but the Most High rules over the kingdom of mankind.

In the Hebrew Bible, Daniel is placed among the Writings rather than the Prophets. In Christian Bibles it follows Ezekiel as the last of the major prophets. Its influence reaches far into the New Testament. Jesus speaks of the abomination of desolation spoken of by the prophet Daniel, and his favourite way of referring to himself, the Son of Man, comes from Daniel's seventh chapter. The book of Revelation draws on Daniel's visions throughout.

=== 1
Daniel and the other young captives have arrived in Babylon, and the opening chapter shows what their new life will demand. The young men from Judah are placed under Ashpenaz, the chief of the court officials, and given a three-year course in the language and literature of the Chaldeans, the learned class of Babylon, whose studies included astronomy, omens and divination. They are to eat from the king's own table, and then serve in the palace.

Four are named: Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael and Azariah. Each Hebrew name contains a reference to the God of Israel, and each is replaced with a Babylonian one. Daniel becomes Belteshazzar, a name that seems to call on the protection of a Babylonian god, and his friends become Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego.

Daniel accepts the new name and the new education, but he draws a line at the king's food and wine. The text does not say exactly why; the food may have been forbidden by the law of Moses or offered to idols. What follows is a quiet test, lasting ten days. The chapter closes by noting that Daniel remained in royal service until the first year of King Cyrus, more than sixty years later.

=== 2
Nebuchadnezzar is now king, in the second year of his reign, and he is troubled by dreams. Dreams were taken seriously as messages from the gods, and Babylon had a whole class of experts to interpret them. The king summons them, but he sets an impossible condition. They must tell him not only what the dream means, but what the dream was.

When the astrologers reply, the book itself changes language. From the middle of this chapter to the end of chapter seven, Daniel is written in Aramaic, the common language of the Near East.

The experts protest that no one but the gods could do what the king asks, and the furious king orders all the wise men of Babylon to be executed, including Daniel and his friends. Daniel asks for time, and the four pray together for mercy from the God of heaven. That night the mystery is revealed. The dream concerns a great statue made of different metals, and a stone cut out without human hands, and it looks far beyond Nebuchadnezzar's own reign to what will happen in the latter days.

=== 3
Nebuchadnezzar has acknowledged Daniel's God as the God of gods, and Daniel's three friends have been made administrators in the province of Babylon. This chapter carries no date, and Daniel himself does not appear in it. It belongs to his three friends, known here only by their Babylonian names.

The king sets up a golden statue, sixty cubits high and six wide, about ninety feet tall, on the plain of Dura in the province of Babylon. The text does not say whom it represented. Every official in the empire is summoned to its dedication, and the lists of officials and instruments, repeated again and again, give the scene the ponderous grandeur of a state occasion. When the music sounds, everyone must fall down and worship. The penalty for refusal is the blazing furnace.

Some of the astrologers see their opportunity and report that certain Jews have refused. Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego are brought before the furious king, and their reply is one of the most famous answers in the Bible. Their God is able to deliver them, they say, but even if he does not, they will not serve the king's gods.

=== 4
This chapter is unusual in the Bible. It is written as a royal proclamation, issued by Nebuchadnezzar himself to all the peoples of the earth, and most of it is told in his own words. He begins by praising the Most High God, and then explains why.

He was at ease in his palace, at the height of his power, when another dream alarmed him. Only Daniel could interpret it. Nebuchadnezzar describes a great tree reaching to the sky, sheltering every creature, and a holy messenger from heaven, a watcher, who orders it to be cut down, leaving only a stump bound with iron and bronze. The one represented by the tree will live like an animal until seven periods of time pass over him.

Daniel is troubled by what it means, and with good reason, because the tree is the king. He urges Nebuchadnezzar to turn from his sins and show mercy to the oppressed. A year later, walking on the roof of his palace, the king looks out over Babylon and boasts of the great city he has built by his own power. He had indeed rebuilt Babylon on a magnificent scale. While the words are still in his mouth, a voice speaks from heaven.

=== 5
Some time has passed since the stories of Nebuchadnezzar. He died in five hundred and sixty-two before Christ, and after several short reigns the throne passed to Nabonidus, a king who spent years away from his capital, leaving his son Belshazzar to rule in Babylon. Belshazzar is called here the son of Nebuchadnezzar, which in the language of the time could mean his successor or descendant. Daniel, now probably in his eighties, seems to have been forgotten at court.

The scene is a great banquet for a thousand nobles. In the background, though the chapter does not pause to say so, the armies of Cyrus the Persian are closing in on Babylon. Belshazzar, drinking, orders the gold and silver vessels from the temple in Jerusalem to be brought in, the vessels of the book's opening verses, and the guests drink from them as they praise their gods.

Then the fingers of a human hand appear and write on the plastered wall of the palace. None of the king's wise men can read it. The queen, probably the queen mother, remembers Daniel. The king offers him the reward of third ruler in the kingdom, perhaps because Nabonidus and Belshazzar held the first two places. What happens next is over before the night is out.

=== 6
Babylon has fallen, and a new empire rules. The kingdom has passed to Darius the Mede, a figure not yet identified with certainty in other ancient records; some think he was a governor appointed by Cyrus, or Cyrus himself under another title. The new administration has a hundred and twenty satraps, or provincial governors, with three senior administrators over them. Daniel, now very old, is one of the three, and the king plans to set him over the whole kingdom.

His rivals look for something to accuse him of and find nothing. So they turn to his faith. They persuade the king to sign a decree that for thirty days no one may pray to any god or man except the king, and, in accordance with the law of the Medes and Persians, it cannot be changed. The penalty is the den of lions.

Daniel learns of the decree and goes home. In his upper room the windows open towards Jerusalem, as Solomon had once asked that the exiles might pray towards the city, and three times a day he kneels and prays, as he always has. The king soon finds he has been trapped by his own law.

=== 7
Here the stories of the court give way to visions, and the book steps back in time. The first vision comes in the first year of Belshazzar, before the feast of chapter five, while Babylon still ruled. Daniel now speaks in his own voice.

The four winds of heaven stir up the great sea, and four beasts rise from it, each unlike the others: a lion with eagle's wings, a bear, a leopard with four wings and four heads, and a fourth beast, terrifying and unlike any animal, with iron teeth and ten horns. Among its horns a little horn appears, with eyes and a boastful mouth.

Then the scene moves to heaven. Thrones are set in place, the Ancient of Days takes his seat, and the books are opened. And one like a son of man comes with the clouds of heaven and is given an everlasting kingdom. The beasts, Daniel is told, are four kings or kingdoms, echoing the statue of chapter two. Interpreters have identified the four in different ways, most often as Babylon, the Medes and Persians, Greece and Rome, or as Babylon, Media, Persia and Greece. Jesus would take up the figure of the son of man coming with the clouds when he stood on trial before the high priest.

=== 8
Two years after the vision of the four beasts, in the third year of Belshazzar, Daniel has a second vision. At this point the book returns from Aramaic to Hebrew, and it stays in Hebrew to the end.

In the vision Daniel finds himself at Susa, by the Ulai canal, in the land of Elam, east of Babylon. Susa would later become a royal capital of the Persian empire, the setting of the book of Esther and of Nehemiah's service at court.

He sees a ram with two horns, one longer than the other, charging west, north and south. Then a goat with a single great horn comes from the west, so fast it does not touch the ground, and shatters the ram. This time the angel Gabriel, named here for the first time in the Bible, explains the vision, and names the kingdoms. The ram is the kings of Media and Persia, the goat is Greece, and its great horn is the first king, widely understood as Alexander the Great, whose empire split into four after his early death. From one of the four comes a small horn that grows great and attacks the sanctuary. Most interpreters see in him Antiochus the Fourth, the Greek king who profaned the temple in Jerusalem in the second century before Christ.

=== 9
The scene moves forward again, to the first year of Darius the Mede, the year Babylon fell to the Persians, around five hundred and thirty-nine before Christ. The den of lions belongs to this same period, and so does the decree of Cyrus, recorded in Ezra, allowing the exiles to go home.

Daniel has been reading the scrolls of the prophet Jeremiah, who had written that the desolation of Jerusalem would last seventy years. Daniel was taken to Babylon some sixty-six years earlier. The seventy years are nearly over. So he turns to God in prayer, with fasting, sackcloth and ashes, and the prayer that follows takes up most of the chapter. It is a confession for the whole nation, acknowledging that the exile came because Israel would not listen to the prophets, and it appeals only to God's mercy and the honour of his name.

While he is still praying, at the hour of the evening sacrifice, though no sacrifice has been offered in Jerusalem for decades, Gabriel comes to him again. He brings a message about seventy weeks, or seventy sevens, decreed for Daniel's people and the holy city, reaching on to an anointed one and to a time of desolation. Few passages in the Bible have been interpreted in so many different ways.

=== 10
The final vision of the book fills the last three chapters, and this one sets the scene for it. It is the third year of Cyrus, king of Persia, around five hundred and thirty-six before Christ. By now the first exiles have gone home to Judah, as the book of Ezra tells, and have begun to rebuild. Daniel, an old man, has stayed behind. This is the last date in his life that the book records.

After three weeks of mourning and fasting, standing on the bank of the great river Tigris with some companions, he sees a man dressed in linen, his face like lightning and his voice like the roar of a crowd. The others do not see him, but terror falls on them and they run away. Daniel is left alone, and his strength drains from him.

The figure touches him and tells him that his prayer was heard from the first day, but that he was delayed for twenty-one days by the prince of the kingdom of Persia, until Michael, one of the chief princes, came to help. It is a rare glimpse of conflict in the unseen world behind the kingdoms of the earth. The messenger has come to tell Daniel what will happen to his people in the latter days.

=== 11
The heavenly messenger now delivers his message, one of the most detailed passages of prediction in the Bible. It names no kings after the Persians, but describes them clearly enough that their outline can be traced.

It begins with the kings of Persia, and a mighty king who arises after them, whose kingdom is divided towards the four winds. Then it follows two of the kingdoms that emerged from that division, the king of the South and the king of the North. In the history of the following centuries these were the Ptolemies, the Greek dynasty that ruled Egypt, and the Seleucids, who ruled from Syria. The land of Judah, which the text calls the Beautiful Land, lay between them and was repeatedly fought over.

Then a despicable person seizes the throne of the North by intrigue. He is generally identified as Antiochus the Fourth, already glimpsed as the small horn of chapter eight, who turns against the holy covenant, desecrates the temple, abolishes the daily sacrifice and sets up the abomination of desolation, while those who know their God stand firm. The closing verses of the chapter are harder to match with known events, and interpreters differ over whether they still describe Antiochus or look beyond him to the end of the age.

=== 12
The last vision of the book reaches its conclusion. After the rise and fall of kings, the messenger turns to what lies at the end of it all.

Michael, the great prince who watches over Daniel's people, will arise. There will be a time of distress unlike any before it, but those whose names are written in the book will be delivered. And many who sleep in the dust of the earth will awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt. It is the clearest statement of a resurrection of the dead anywhere in the Old Testament.

Daniel is told to seal up the book until the time of the end. Then, standing by the river, he hears one heavenly figure ask the man in linen how long it will be until these things are fulfilled. The answer is a time, times and half a time, followed by numbers of days, and Daniel admits that he does not understand. The book's final words are addressed to him personally. He is to go his way, to rest, and to rise to receive his inheritance at the end of the days.
