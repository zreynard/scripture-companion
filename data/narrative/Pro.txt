=== intro
The book of Proverbs takes us out of the stream of Israel's story and into its classrooms. There are no battles here, no journeys and no prophets confronting kings. Instead we hear a father speaking to his son, a teacher gathering sayings for his students, and a voice calling out at the city gates. This is the wisdom of ancient Israel, distilled into lines short enough to remember and sharp enough to stay with you.

The opening words name Solomon son of David, king of Israel. Solomon reigned in Jerusalem in the tenth century before Christ, the high point of the united kingdom, when Israel was at peace with its neighbours and trade flowed in from Tyre, Arabia and Egypt. The book of Kings remembers him above all for his wisdom. When he became king he asked God for a discerning heart to govern the people, and God granted it. Kings records that he spoke three thousand proverbs, that he could talk of trees and animals, birds and fish, and that people came from every nation to hear him, among them the queen of Sheba. Proverbs gathers part of that legacy.

But it is not all Solomon, and the book itself says so. Proverbs is an anthology, built up over generations, and it marks its own seams. After the opening instruction comes a heading, the proverbs of Solomon, and a long collection of single sayings. Later we meet a section headed the sayings of the wise, then a further collection of Solomon's proverbs copied out by the men of Hezekiah king of Judah, who lived more than two centuries after Solomon. The last two chapters bring in voices from outside Israel's royal line altogether: the words of a man named Agur, and the words of King Lemuel, which his mother taught him.

The setting of much of this material is the royal court. Kings in the ancient world kept scribes and counsellors, and young men being trained for public life were schooled in the art of living well: how to speak, when to keep silent, how to handle money, how to stand before a ruler. Egypt and Mesopotamia had their own long traditions of wisdom writing, often framed as a father's teaching to his son, and Israel's wise men knew something of them. One section of Proverbs bears a close resemblance to an Egyptian text called the Instruction of Amenemope. What sets Israel's wisdom apart is its starting point, announced in the first chapter: the fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge. Skill in living is never separated here from reverence for the God of Israel.

The first nine chapters form a gateway to the book. They are not short sayings but longer speeches, most of them opening with the words my son. A father, with the mother's teaching standing beside his own, urges his son to choose the right road while he is young. Two figures dominate these chapters. One is Wisdom herself, pictured as a noble woman who calls out in the streets and squares, who claims to have been with God before the world was made, and who invites the simple to her table. The other is her rival: the smooth-tongued woman who leads young men astray, and at last a figure called Folly, who sets up her own house and calls out to the same passers-by. The son must decide whose voice he will follow.

From chapter ten the style changes completely. Now come the proverbs proper, usually two lines long, in the parallel form of Hebrew poetry. The second line may echo the first, contrast with it, or carry it further. They are observations about how life generally goes, the harvest that follows hard work, the trouble that follows a careless tongue, and they are meant to be weighed rather than treated as promises. Some seem to contradict each other on purpose, and the reader is left to judge which one fits the moment. The subjects range freely and often without obvious order: speech, work, money, anger, friendship, family, justice, the king, and above all the difference between the wise and the fool.

The book ends with a portrait. Its final poem praises a wife of noble character, and it is an acrostic, each line beginning with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet. The book that began with Wisdom calling in the streets closes with wisdom lived out in a household.

In our Bibles Proverbs stands among the poetical books, after the Psalms and before Ecclesiastes and the Song of Songs, two other works linked with Solomon. Together with Job they make up what is often called the wisdom literature of the Old Testament. The listener will not find a storyline to follow from chapter to chapter here. What Proverbs offers instead is a voice, patient and searching, teaching a young man how to walk through the world.

=== 1
The book opens with a title and a statement of purpose. These are the proverbs of Solomon son of David, king of Israel, and the opening lines pile up the words for what they offer: wisdom, discipline, insight, prudence, discretion. The book is meant for the young and the inexperienced, whom it calls the simple, but even the wise are invited to listen and grow wiser. Then comes the motto that stands over everything that follows: the fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge.

The first speech begins with the words that will open speech after speech in these chapters: listen, my son. The father's opening warning is about the company his son keeps. A gang of young men is pictured, promising easy plunder and a share of the spoils if he will join them in an ambush.

The chapter closes with a new voice. Wisdom herself appears as a woman standing at the busiest places in the city, at the gates and in the squares, and she speaks with the urgency of a prophet to those who will not listen.

=== 2
After Wisdom's warning in the streets, the father speaks again, and his tone is now one of patient encouragement. This chapter is a single long sentence in Hebrew, built on a chain of conditions: if you accept my words, if you call out for insight, if you search for it like silver or hidden treasure, then you will understand the fear of the Lord.

The search is the son's, but the gift is God's. The father insists that it is the Lord who gives wisdom, and that wisdom, once it enters the heart, becomes a guard.

The second half of the chapter names two dangers that wisdom will rescue the son from. The first is men whose words are twisted and who love the crooked paths. The second is introduced here for the first time and will return again and again in these chapters: the forbidden woman, a stranger with seductive words, who has left the husband of her youth and forgotten the covenant of her God.

=== 3
The father continues his instruction, and this chapter contains some of the best known lines in the book. He begins with the promise that his teaching will bring length of days and peace, and calls on his son to keep loving devotion and faithfulness bound around his neck and written on his heart.

From there the speech moves through a series of short appeals, each with its reason attached: trust in the Lord rather than your own understanding, honour him with the firstfruits of your harvest, and do not resent his discipline, for he corrects the one he loves as a father does a son.

At the centre of the chapter is a hymn in praise of Wisdom, more precious than silver or gold, a tree of life to those who hold her fast. The father reminds his son that by wisdom the Lord founded the earth. The chapter ends on practical ground, with how to treat a neighbour: do not withhold what is owed, do not plot against the one who lives trustingly beside you.

=== 4
The father now widens the circle. He addresses his sons, plural, and for the first time he tells them something of his own childhood. When he was a boy, tender and the only child of his mother, his own father sat him down and taught him: get wisdom, get understanding. So the instruction we are hearing is itself an inheritance, passed from grandfather to father to son.

The rest of the chapter is built around the picture of two roads. The way of the wicked is dark, its travellers so given to wrongdoing that they cannot sleep until they have caused someone to fall. The path of the righteous is like the first gleam of dawn, growing brighter until full day.

The father closes by turning to the son's own person, his heart, his mouth, his eyes and his feet. Guard your heart above all, he says, for from it flow the springs of life.

=== 5
The father returns to the danger he named in chapter two, and this time he gives it a whole speech. The forbidden woman's lips drip honey and her words are smoother than oil, but the end of the road with her is bitter. Her steps lead down to Sheol, the realm of the dead.

The father speaks with unusual frankness about what such a path costs a young man: his strength spent on strangers, his wealth enriching another man's house, and at the end of his life a groan of regret that he never listened to his teachers.

Then the speech turns from warning to delight. Using the language of water, a man's own cistern and his own fountain, the father urges his son to find his joy in the wife of his youth, and to be captivated by her love always. The chapter ends with a reminder that the Lord sees every path a man takes.

=== 6
This chapter interrupts the father's long concern with the adulteress to address three other dangers of daily life, each in a short, vivid sketch.

The first is rash financial promises. To put up security for a neighbour's debt was to place yourself in his hands, and the father urges his son to go at once and get himself released, without sleeping until it is done. The second is laziness, and here the son is sent to watch the ant, which needs no overseer to gather its food in summer. The third is the troublemaker, a worthless man who signals with his eyes, feet and fingers while he plots in his heart. A numbered saying follows, six things that the Lord hates, seven that are detestable to him, a form found elsewhere in ancient wisdom.

Then the father returns to his main warning. He calls his teaching a lamp and a light that will keep his son from another man's wife, and he speaks plainly about the jealousy of a wronged husband, which no gift can buy off.

=== 7
The father's warnings about the adulteress reach their climax in this chapter, and this time he tells a story. It begins with a scene: the father standing at the window of his house, looking down through the lattice at the street below as evening falls.

He sees a young man, one of the simple, lacking judgment, strolling down the road towards a particular corner. A woman comes out to meet him, dressed to attract, loud and restless. She has a ready speech: she has made her peace offerings and paid her vows, her bed is spread with coloured linen from Egypt and perfumed with myrrh, and her husband has gone away on a long journey with a bag of money.

The father describes the young man's response with a run of images from the hunt and the slaughterhouse. The story is meant to be watched from a safe distance, so that the son never has to learn its ending for himself.

=== 8
After the darkness of the street corner at twilight, the scene changes to broad daylight. Wisdom stands again on the heights beside the road, at the crossroads and the city gates, and this time she speaks at length, in one of the great poems of the Old Testament.

She begins as a public teacher. Her words are true and straight, worth more than silver and gold, and by her, she says, kings reign and rulers make just laws. Then her speech reaches further back than any human teacher could. Wisdom claims that the Lord brought her forth before his works of old, before the mountains were settled or the springs were flowing. She was there when he marked out the heavens and set a boundary for the sea, at his side like a master craftsman, rejoicing in his world and delighting in humanity.

The poem has had a long history of interpretation. Many early Christian writers read it alongside the New Testament's language about Christ as the Word through whom all things were made. Within Proverbs, Wisdom ends where she began, calling on her hearers to listen and live.

=== 9
The opening section of the book comes to its close with two invitations, set side by side.

First, Wisdom has built her house and carved out its seven pillars. She has prepared meat and mixed her wine, set her table, and sent her servant girls out to call from the highest places in the city: whoever is simple, come in here. Then, at the end of the chapter, comes her rival, the woman named Folly. She is loud and knows nothing. She too sits at the door of her house on the heights of the city, and she calls out to the passers-by with exactly the same words. Her bread is eaten in secret, and her guests, the chapter says, are in the depths of Sheol.

Between the two banquets stands a short collection of sayings about the mocker and the wise, and at its centre the book's motto returns once more: the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom. The simple have heard both voices. With the next chapter, the speeches give way to the proverbs themselves.

=== 10
A new heading stands at the top of this chapter: the proverbs of Solomon. The father's long speeches are over, and in their place comes the first great collection of single sayings, running to the middle of chapter twenty-two. Each proverb is usually a pair of lines, complete in itself, and in these early chapters the second line most often turns against the first with a but. The wise and the foolish, the righteous and the wicked, the diligent and the lazy are set side by side and left for the listener to compare.

The very first saying keeps a thread from the opening chapters: a wise son brings joy to his father, a foolish son grief to his mother. Then the subjects come thick and fast, with the mouth and the lips returning again and again.

=== 11
Solomon's collection continues in the same contrasting pairs. Several sayings in this chapter look beyond the individual to the community around him. A city rejoices when the righteous prosper and is torn down by the mouth of the wicked, and a nation falls for lack of guidance.

The marketplace is here too. In a world without standard coinage, goods were weighed against stones carried in a merchant's bag, and the chapter opens with the Lord's verdict on dishonest scales. Later come sayings about generosity and hoarding, including the farmer who holds back his grain to drive up the price.

Every so often a single picture leaps out of the steady rhythm, none more memorable than the gold ring in a pig's snout.

=== 12
The sayings in this chapter keep close to the ground, to the farmer working his land, the hunter who will not roast his game, the man who cares for the life of his animal. Diligence and laziness are measured by what they bring in at the end of the season.

The chapter returns repeatedly to speech: the honest witness and the false one, rash words that pierce like a sword, the tongue of the wise that heals, the lying lip that lasts only a moment. There is also a brief line about the wife of noble character, who is her husband's crown. The phrase will return at the very end of the book, where it opens a whole poem.

=== 13
The opening saying of this chapter could have come from the first nine chapters: a wise son heeds his father's discipline. Much of what follows has the feel of a household, with sayings about inheritance passed to grandchildren, about the rod of correction, and about the company a young man keeps, since he who walks with the wise becomes wise.

The chapter also observes money with a clear eye. Some pretend to be rich and have nothing, others pretend to be poor and have great wealth. Dishonest gain dwindles, while what is earned by steady work grows.

Among the sayings is one of the book's most humane lines, about hope deferred, which makes the heart sick.

=== 14
This chapter opens with a wise woman building her house, an echo of Wisdom building her house back in chapter nine, while the foolish woman tears hers down with her own hands. The sayings that follow range from the farmyard, where an empty stall means a clean manger but no harvest, to the law court and the royal palace.

Some of the proverbs here look inward with unusual tenderness. The heart knows its own bitterness, and no stranger shares its joy. Even in laughter the heart may ache. Others look outward to the poor, and one of them makes a striking claim: whoever oppresses the poor shows contempt for their Maker. The chapter closes with the king and the nation, subjects that will grow more prominent as the collection goes on.

=== 15
The contrasting pairs continue, and the name of the Lord is heard more often in this chapter than in any before it in the collection. His eyes are in every place, watching the evil and the good. Even Sheol and Abaddon, the depths of the realm of the dead, lie open before him, and how much more the human heart.

A new form also becomes noticeable here, the better than saying, which weighs one thing against another: better a little with the fear of the Lord than great wealth with turmoil, better a dish of vegetables where there is love than a fattened ox with hatred.

The chapter begins with the power of a gentle answer and ends with humility, which comes before honour.

=== 16
Something shifts in Solomon's collection here. For six chapters the sayings have mostly set opposites against each other. From this point the second line more often echoes or extends the first, and sayings on a single subject begin to gather together.

The chapter opens with a cluster about the Lord and human plans. People make their plans and weigh their own motives, but it is the Lord who weighs the heart and directs the steps. Then comes a cluster about the king, his words, his anger and his favour, which is like a rain cloud in spring.

Among the sayings that follow is the familiar warning that pride goes before destruction. The chapter ends where it began, with human plans and divine purpose, in the casting of lots.

=== 17
Much of this chapter is set within the household and among friends, and it begins with a scene that every family understands: better a dry crust eaten in peace than a house full of feasting and strife. A quarrel, the chapter warns, is like letting out water, hard to stop once it starts.

The courts are never far away either. Several sayings concern the bribe, observed with a realist's eye, and the judge who acquits the guilty or condemns the innocent.

The fool is everywhere in these lines, sometimes with grim humour: better to meet a bear robbed of her cubs than a fool in his folly. And the chapter ends with a saying about silence, which even a fool can use to seem wise.

=== 18
Words and their power dominate this chapter. The words of a man's mouth are deep waters. The whispers of a gossip go down like choice morsels into the inmost parts. Life and death, one saying claims, are in the power of the tongue.

Other sayings come from the world of disputes and lawsuits, where the first to state his case seems right until someone else comes forward to question him, and where the casting of lots could settle a quarrel between powerful opponents.

Two neighbouring sayings make a pointed pair. The name of the Lord is a strong tower where the righteous run to safety. A rich man's wealth is his fortified city, but only, the next line adds, in his imagination. The chapter closes on friendship and on the friend who stays closer than a brother.

=== 19
The poor man and his friends are a recurring subject in this chapter. Wealth brings many friends, but the poor are deserted even by their own brothers, and one saying pictures a poor man pursuing them with pleas while they vanish. Against that, a single line stands out: kindness to the poor is a loan to the Lord, and he will repay it.

The household returns as well, with the foolish son, the quarrelsome wife compared to the constant dripping of a leaking roof, and the prudent wife who is a gift from the Lord. The king's anger is like the roar of a lion. And near the end, for a moment, the voice of the opening chapters is heard again, speaking directly: if you stop listening to instruction, my son, you will stray.

=== 20
The chapter opens with strong drink, personified as a mocker and a brawler, and much of what follows concerns how easily people deceive others and themselves. The buyer in the marketplace cries worthless, worthless, and then goes off boasting of his bargain. Unequal weights and measures appear twice, both detestable to the Lord. And one saying asks plainly who can claim to have a pure heart.

The king is prominent again. He sits on his throne to judge, sifting out evil with his eyes, and a wise king drives the threshing wheel over the wicked, an image from the harvest floor, where grain was crushed and separated from its husk.

Running through it all is a note of humility about human understanding. A man's steps are from the Lord, so how can anyone understand his own way?

=== 21
The opening picture of this chapter comes from the irrigated fields of the ancient world. The king's heart, for all his power, is like a channel of water in the Lord's hand, and he directs it wherever he pleases. The sayings that follow keep returning to the same theme of the Lord's oversight: he weighs the heart, and he values justice more than sacrifice.

There are lighter touches. Twice in this chapter a man concludes that it would be better to live on a corner of the roof, or out in the desert, than to share a house with a quarrelsome wife.

The final lines draw a boundary around all human wisdom. No wisdom, understanding or counsel can stand against the Lord. The horse is made ready for battle, but victory belongs to him.

=== 22
The first part of this chapter completes the long collection of Solomon's proverbs that began in chapter ten. It includes some of the best known sayings in the book, on the value of a good name, on the rich and the poor who share the same Maker, and on training a child in the way he should go.

Then, partway through the chapter, the voice changes. A new heading introduces the words of the wise, and a teacher addresses his pupil directly again, much as the father did in the opening chapters. He says he has written thirty sayings, so that his student may give a sound answer to those who sent him, words that belong to the training of young men for service as officials and messengers.

This section has well known parallels with an Egyptian work, the Instruction of Amenemope, which is also arranged in thirty parts. Israel's teacher draws on that tradition while grounding everything in trust in the Lord. Its counsel is concrete: do not rob the poor, do not move the ancient boundary stone, and notice the skilled worker who will one day stand before kings.

=== 23
The words of the wise continue, and the setting is plainly the world of the court and the well-to-do. The chapter opens with table manners at a ruler's banquet, where the young official is told to watch himself and his appetite, since that food may not be what it seems. Then comes a warning about wearing oneself out for riches, which grow wings and fly off like an eagle.

The closest parallels with the Egyptian Instruction of Amenemope end early in this chapter, with a second warning about boundary stones and the fields of the fatherless, whose Redeemer is strong.

After that the teacher speaks more personally, addressing my son again and longing for the day his pupil's wisdom will make his parents glad. The chapter closes with a satirical portrait of a drunkard, so dizzy he feels like a man lying at the top of a ship's mast, and already wondering when he can wake up for another drink.

=== 24
The words of the wise run on into the first part of this chapter, still addressed to a son and still in short units of two or three sayings. They return to subjects the teacher has raised before: do not envy the wicked, rely on sound counsel when you go to war, fear the Lord and the king. One saying refuses the easy excuse of the bystander. Rescue those being led away to death, it says, and do not claim that you knew nothing, for the One who weighs hearts knows.

Then a brief new heading appears: these also are sayings of the wise. This small appendix concerns fairness in court, honest testimony and the temptation to repay a wrong in kind.

It ends with a little scene told in the first person. The teacher walks past the field of a lazy man and finds it overgrown with thorns, its stone wall fallen down, and draws from it the lesson first heard back in chapter six, a little sleep, a little slumber, and poverty arrives like a robber.

=== 25
A fresh heading opens the last major collection in the book. These are more proverbs of Solomon, it says, copied by the men of Hezekiah king of Judah. Hezekiah reigned in Jerusalem about seven hundred years before Christ, in the days of the prophet Isaiah and the Assyrian threat, and the book of Kings remembers him as a reforming king. Here we glimpse his scribes at work, gathering older sayings attributed to Solomon and preserving them. This collection runs to the end of chapter twenty-nine.

Its opening sayings belong to the court: the glory of kings is to search out a matter, and a wise official does not push himself forward in the king's presence. After that come many comparisons drawn from nature and daily life, like apples of gold in settings of silver, like snow at harvest time, like cold water to a weary soul. Among them is the counsel to feed a hungry enemy, which the apostle Paul would later quote in his letter to the Romans.

=== 26
Hezekiah's collection continues with three tightly grouped clusters of sayings, each devoted to one familiar character.

The first is the fool. A string of comparisons, most of them biting, describes what it is like to honour a fool, to send a message by him, or to hear a proverb in his mouth. Two sayings placed side by side seem to contradict each other, do not answer a fool according to his folly, then answer a fool according to his folly, and the listener is left to weigh when each one applies.

Next comes the slacker, with his excuses about the lion in the road, turning in his bed like a door on its hinges. The third cluster concerns the troublemaker, the gossip and the flatterer, whose gracious speech is like a glaze covering a cheap clay pot.

=== 27
This chapter in Hezekiah's collection turns often to friendship and neighbourly life. The faithful wounds of a friend are set against the kisses of an enemy, and a neighbour nearby is better than a brother far away. One saying pictures the man who greets his neighbour too loudly, too early in the morning, and finds his blessing counted as a curse. As iron sharpens iron, says another, so one person sharpens another.

There is a brief address from a father to his son, the first in some time, and at the end of the chapter something unusual: a short poem about the shepherd's life. Know the state of your flocks, it says, for riches do not last forever, and the lambs and the goats will clothe and feed the household year after year.

=== 28
The tone of Hezekiah's collection shifts here back towards the contrasting pairs of the earlier chapters, the righteous set against the wicked. The opening line is one of the most vivid: the wicked flee when no one pursues, but the righteous are as bold as a lion.

Much in this chapter concerns the life of a whole people and those who govern it. A land in rebellion has many rulers. A wicked ruler over a helpless people is like a roaring lion or a charging bear. When the wicked rise to power, people go into hiding.

The law of God is mentioned several times here, more than anywhere else in the book. Alongside it is a saying that stands out for its directness, that whoever conceals his sins will not prosper, but whoever confesses and renounces them will find mercy.

=== 29
This is the last chapter of the collection gathered by Hezekiah's men, and it continues the concerns of the one before it. Again and again the sayings ask what happens to a people when the righteous flourish and when the wicked rule. A king who judges the poor fairly will see his throne established, while a ruler who listens to lies will find all his officials corrupt.

Household discipline, the angry man, the hasty speaker and the proud man all appear once more. Near the end comes a saying about the fear of man, which is a snare, and trust in the Lord, which sets a person safely on high.

The final line sums up the collection's long contrast in a single observation: the righteous and the wicked each find the other detestable. After this, the book turns to two new voices.

=== 30
The book now introduces a speaker we know nothing about apart from this chapter. These are the words of Agur son of Jakeh, addressed to someone named Ithiel.

Agur's voice is unlike anything heard so far. He opens in weariness and humility, confessing that he has not learned wisdom, and asks a series of questions about the One who rules the wind and the waters and has established the ends of the earth. He makes a short prayer for neither poverty nor riches.

Then he turns to the numerical sayings that are his hallmark, three things, then four. They are sayings of wonder and observation: things that are never satisfied, things too wonderful to understand, small creatures that are exceedingly wise, and creatures stately in their stride.

=== 31
The book's final chapter brings two voices. The first is King Lemuel, who passes on what his mother taught him. Her advice to her son is the counsel of a queen mother to a ruler: do not waste your strength on women, keep away from strong drink, and open your mouth for those who have no voice.

Then comes the poem that brings the whole book to a close, the portrait of the wife of noble character. It is an acrostic of twenty-two lines, each beginning with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet, from first to last.

The woman it describes is industrious and shrewd. She works with wool and flax, buys a field and plants a vineyard, trades with merchants, cares for the poor, and speaks with wisdom. In her the wisdom personified in the opening chapters takes on a human face, and the book ends with her praised at the city gates, where Wisdom first raised her voice.
