=== intro
The first three Gospels have so much in common that they are often called the Synoptic Gospels, from a Greek word meaning seen together. John stands apart. It begins not in Bethlehem or at the Jordan, but before creation itself: in the beginning was the Word. Most of what it records appears nowhere else. There is no Sermon on the Mount, no parable of the sower, no casting out of demons. Instead there are long conversations, with a Pharisee by night, with a Samaritan woman at a well, with crowds and critics in the temple courts, and an extended farewell to the disciples on the night before the cross.

The Gospel does not name its author. It says that it rests on the testimony of an unnamed figure called the disciple whom Jesus loved, who leans against Jesus at the last supper, stands at the cross, and runs to the empty tomb, and its closing lines say that this disciple wrote these things. From the second century the church identified him as John, the son of Zebedee, one of the twelve, who was said to have lived to a great age in Ephesus, the leading city of the Roman province of Asia. Irenaeus, a bishop who as a young man had known Polycarp, a disciple of John, is an early witness to that tradition. Scholars have long discussed whether the beloved disciple was the apostle or another early follower, and whether others helped to give the book its final shape. Its own last verses hint at such a circle: we know that his testimony is true. It is usually thought to be the last of the four Gospels to be written, toward the end of the first century.

John tells his readers plainly why he wrote: so that they may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing they may have life in his name. He often explains Hebrew and Aramaic words, Rabbi, Messiah, Siloam, and Jewish customs and feasts, which suggests that many of his first readers lived far from Judea. Yet the book is steeped in the Scriptures and the worship of Israel, and it knows the geography of Jerusalem and the land in close detail. It also reflects a painful separation, for it speaks of believers being put out of the synagogue. John often uses the phrase the Jews for the religious authorities in Jerusalem who oppose Jesus, rather than for the Jewish people as a whole, to which Jesus himself and every one of his first disciples belonged.

The other Gospels concentrate on Galilee, with a single climactic journey to Jerusalem at the end. John moves back and forth. Jesus goes up to Jerusalem again and again for the great festivals of the Jewish year, and the calendar gives the book much of its structure. There is a Passover near the beginning, an unnamed feast, a second Passover in Galilee, the autumn Feast of Tabernacles, the winter Feast of Dedication, and finally the last Passover, when he dies. Because John mentions three Passovers, it is from his Gospel that the common idea of a ministry of about three years comes, though the exact length is uncertain. Again and again Jesus' words take up the images of the festival he is attending: the bread of the Passover, the water and light of Tabernacles.

John calls Jesus' miracles signs, because they point beyond themselves. He records only a handful of them, seven by the usual count before the resurrection, from water turned into wine at a wedding to a dead man called out of his tomb. Alongside them come the sayings that begin with the words I am: I am the bread of life, the light of the world, the good shepherd, the resurrection and the life, the way and the truth and the life, the true vine. Those words carried a deep resonance for Jewish hearers, because I am echoes the name God gave himself to Moses at the burning bush. John's vocabulary is simple and his ideas profound. He writes about light and darkness, life and death, truth and falsehood, believing and seeing, and his characters often misunderstand Jesus, taking literally what he means in a deeper sense.

The book moves in two great halves. After a prologue, the first twelve chapters follow Jesus' public ministry, his signs and his debates with the authorities, with growing belief on one side and hardening opposition on the other. Through these chapters runs a refrain, that his hour has not yet come. Then, as the last Passover approaches, Jesus says that the hour has come. The second half slows dramatically. Five chapters are given to a single evening, as Jesus washes his disciples' feet, speaks with them at length, and prays for them. Then come the arrest in a garden across the Kidron valley, the hearings before Annas and Caiaphas and Pilate, the crucifixion, and the first day of the week, when Mary Magdalene finds the tomb empty. A final chapter returns to the Sea of Galilee, with a breakfast on the shore and a conversation between the risen Jesus and Peter.

Readers who know the other Gospels will find John both familiar and new. The cast is the same: John the Baptist, Peter and Andrew, the sons of Zebedee, Caiaphas, Pilate. But figures who are names elsewhere come alive here, Philip, Thomas and Nathanael, Nicodemus, Martha, Mary and Lazarus of Bethany. And at every turn John invites his readers to consider the question that the prologue answers from the start: who is this man, and where has he come from?

=== 1
John's Gospel opens with words that deliberately recall the first line of Genesis: in the beginning. Before anything was made, the Word was with God, and the Word was God. The Greek term, logos, was familiar both to Jews, who spoke of God's word and wisdom at work in creation, and to Greek thinkers, who used it of the reason that ordered the universe. John gives it a meaning neither had imagined: the Word became flesh and dwelt among us. This opening passage, the prologue, is like an overture, introducing light and darkness, witness, belief and glory, all of which return later.

Into the prologue steps John the Baptist, though here he is simply called John, and his role is to be a witness. The narrative then begins at Bethany beyond the Jordan, where priests and Levites sent from Jerusalem question him about who he is. John does not describe the baptism of Jesus itself. Instead, over several days, the Baptist points to Jesus as the Lamb of God, and his own disciples start to follow. Andrew brings his brother Simon. Philip, from Bethsaida, brings Nathanael, who is doubtful that anything good can come from Nazareth.

=== 2
On the third day, a wedding takes place at Cana, a village in the hills of Galilee. Jesus has come with his new disciples, and his mother is there. Village weddings could last for days, and running out of wine would have been a lasting embarrassment for the bridegroom's family. Standing nearby are six large stone jars, each holding twenty to thirty gallons, kept for the Jewish rites of purification. What happens next John calls the first of Jesus' signs, the miracles that reveal who he is.

After a short stay in Capernaum, Jesus goes up to Jerusalem for the Passover, the first of three Passovers that John mentions. In the temple courts, where cattle, sheep and doves were sold for sacrifice and money was changed for the temple tax, he makes a whip of cords and drives the traders out. The other Gospels place a similar scene in the last week of his life; interpreters differ on whether there were two such occasions or John has placed one for his own reasons. The authorities demand a sign, and Jesus speaks of destroying the temple and raising it in three days. Herod's rebuilding had already been going on for forty-six years.

=== 3
Jesus is still in Jerusalem, and many have been impressed by his signs. Now a visitor comes to him at night. Nicodemus is a Pharisee and a leader of the Jews, very likely a member of the ruling council, and he addresses Jesus respectfully as a teacher come from God. John does not say why he comes at night, but darkness and light matter in this Gospel.

The conversation turns quickly to the need to be born again. The Greek word can mean both again and from above, and Nicodemus takes it in the most literal way. Jesus speaks of wind and Spirit, and recalls the bronze snake that Moses lifted up in the wilderness, from the book of Numbers. Then comes one of the most quoted sentences in the Bible, about God's love for the world. Exactly where Jesus' words end and John's own reflection begins is unclear, since ancient writing had no quotation marks.

The second half of the chapter moves to the Judean countryside, where Jesus' disciples are baptising. John the Baptist is still at work nearby, at Aenon near Salim, and has not yet been imprisoned. When his disciples complain that the crowds are going to Jesus, he gives his answer with an image from a wedding.

=== 4
Jesus leaves Judea to return to Galilee, and he takes the direct route north through Samaria. The Samaritans were descended from the old northern kingdom of Israel and the peoples settled among them by the Assyrians. They accepted only the five books of Moses and worshipped on Mount Gerizim, where their temple had stood until a Hasmonean king destroyed it. Jews and Samaritans had kept apart, often bitterly, for centuries.

At midday, tired from the road, Jesus sits by Jacob's well near the town of Sychar, close to ancient Shechem and within sight of Mount Gerizim, on land that Jacob had once owned. A Samaritan woman comes to draw water alone, and Jesus asks her for a drink. For a Jewish teacher to speak with her at all was extraordinary, as both she and his returning disciples recognise. Their conversation moves from well water to living water, and from her own life to the long dispute over where God should be worshipped.

After two days among the Samaritans, Jesus returns to Cana. A royal official, probably in the service of Herod Antipas, comes from Capernaum, some distance away by the lake, to plead for his dying son. This is the second sign.

=== 5
Some time later there is another feast, which John does not name, and Jesus goes up to Jerusalem again. Near the Sheep Gate, north of the temple, is a pool called Bethesda, surrounded by five covered colonnades. Archaeologists have uncovered a pair of large pools on that site, with porticoes around and between them. The sick lie there in great numbers, waiting for the water to be stirred.

One man has been an invalid for thirty-eight years. Jesus tells him to pick up his mat and walk, and he does. But it is the Sabbath, and carrying a mat counts as work. The authorities challenge the man, and then Jesus himself.

Jesus' reply, my Father is working until now, and I am working, makes matters far worse. His hearers understand him to be making himself equal with God, and from this point, John says, they seek to kill him. The rest of the chapter is a long answer, as if in a courtroom, about the Son's relationship to the Father and the witnesses who support his claims: John the Baptist, the works he does, the Father himself, and the Scriptures, including the writings of Moses.

=== 6
The scene moves back to Galilee, to the far side of the lake, which John also calls the Sea of Tiberias, after the city Herod Antipas had built on its western shore and named for the emperor. It is spring, and the Passover is near. A large crowd has followed Jesus up onto the hillside.

The feeding of the five thousand is the only miracle before the resurrection told in all four Gospels, and John adds details of his own: Philip's reckoning that two hundred denarii would not buy enough bread, and Andrew's discovery of a boy with five barley loaves, the bread of the poor. Afterwards the crowd wants to seize Jesus and make him king, and he withdraws. That night the disciples, rowing across to Capernaum in a strong wind, see him walking on the water.

The next day, in the synagogue at Capernaum, the crowd catches up with him, and the rest of the chapter is a long discussion about bread. They remember the manna in the wilderness, and Jesus speaks of himself as the bread of life. His words are hard to accept, and many disciples turn away. Jesus asks the twelve whether they will leave too, and Peter answers for them.

=== 7
Six months have passed since the Passover of the previous chapter. Jesus has kept to Galilee, because the authorities in Judea want to kill him. Now the Feast of Tabernacles is near, the great autumn festival at the end of the harvest, when Jews lived for a week in shelters made of branches to recall Israel's years in the wilderness. Jerusalem would be crowded with pilgrims.

Jesus' brothers, who do not yet believe in him, urge him to go up and show himself. He refuses, but later goes up privately, and halfway through the feast he begins to teach openly in the temple courts. The city is divided about him, and the debates turn on his learning, on the Sabbath healing from chapter five, and on where the Messiah would come from.

Later Jewish tradition describes how, during this feast, water was carried in procession from the Pool of Siloam and poured out at the altar. On the last and greatest day, Jesus stands and cries out, if anyone is thirsty, let him come to me. The chief priests send officers to arrest him, and they come back empty-handed. Nicodemus, who came to Jesus by night, speaks up cautiously for him.

=== 8
The chapter begins with the story of a woman caught in adultery, brought before Jesus in the temple courts by scribes and Pharisees who want to trap him. This passage, which begins with the last line of chapter seven, is missing from the earliest manuscripts of John, and some later ones place it elsewhere. Many scholars think it is an authentic early story about Jesus that found its place here later. It is included in the reading that follows.

Then the teaching in the temple resumes, apparently still in the setting of the feast. According to the same later tradition, great lamps were lit in the temple courts during Tabernacles, and Jesus, teaching near the treasury, declares, I am the light of the world. What follows is the sharpest debate in the Gospel so far. Jesus speaks of truth that sets people free, and his hearers protest that they are Abraham's descendants and have never been slaves of anyone. The argument turns on who their true father is.

The exchange rises to a final claim: before Abraham was born, I am. Those words echo the name God revealed to Moses, and the listeners understand them. They pick up stones to throw at him, but Jesus slips away from the temple.

=== 9
Still in Jerusalem, Jesus and his disciples pass a man who has been blind from birth. The disciples ask a question that reflects a common belief of the time, that suffering must be the result of someone's sin: was it this man's, or his parents'? Jesus answers by repeating what he said at the feast, that he is the light of the world.

He makes mud, puts it on the man's eyes, and sends him to wash in the Pool of Siloam, at the southern end of the city. John notes that the name means sent. The man comes back seeing. Once again the healing takes place on the Sabbath, and making mud counted as work.

What follows is told almost like a trial, in a series of scenes. Neighbours argue about whether this is really the same man. The Pharisees question him, then his parents, who are afraid to say much, because anyone who confessed Jesus as the Christ was to be put out of the synagogue. Then they question the man again. As the pressure grows, so does his boldness. The chapter ends with Jesus finding him, and with words about who is truly blind.

=== 10
There is no break between the previous chapter and this one. Jesus is still speaking to the Pharisees who questioned the man born blind, and he turns to an image every Judean knew: a sheepfold, a shepherd, and sheep that know his voice. The Scriptures had long pictured Israel's leaders as shepherds and condemned those who failed the flock. Jesus calls himself the gate for the sheep and the good shepherd, who lays down his life for them. Once again his listeners are divided.

Then John moves the story forward two or three months. It is winter, and the Feast of Dedication is being held in Jerusalem. This is Hanukkah, the festival that remembers the cleansing and rededication of the temple by Judas Maccabeus and his brothers, after it had been defiled by the Syrian king Antiochus. Jesus is walking in Solomon's Colonnade, a covered walkway along the eastern side of the temple courts.

The leaders surround him and demand that he tell them plainly whether he is the Christ. His answer ends with the words, I and the Father are one, and again they take up stones. Jesus withdraws across the Jordan, to the place where John had first baptised, and many there believe in him.

=== 11
Jesus is staying across the Jordan when a message arrives from Bethany, a village less than two miles from Jerusalem on the far side of the Mount of Olives. Lazarus is ill. He and his sisters, Martha and Mary, are close friends, and John says plainly that Jesus loved them. Yet he waits two more days before setting out, and his disciples are alarmed that he means to go back to Judea at all, so soon after he was nearly stoned. Thomas, called Didymus, the twin, speaks up with grim loyalty.

By the time Jesus arrives, Lazarus has been in the tomb for four days. Each sister meets Jesus with the same words of grief. To Martha he says, I am the resurrection and the life. At the tomb, a cave with a stone across its entrance, the shortest verse in the Bible records that Jesus wept.

By the usual count this is the seventh and greatest of the signs before the cross, and its consequences are immediate. The chief priests and Pharisees call a meeting of the Sanhedrin, fearing that Rome will take away their temple and their nation. Caiaphas, the high priest that year, gives his counsel, and from that day they plan to kill Jesus. Jesus withdraws to a town called Ephraim, near the wilderness, as the Passover approaches.

=== 12
Six days before the Passover, Jesus returns to Bethany, and a dinner is given in his honour, with Lazarus at the table and Martha serving. Mary pours a pint of costly perfume of pure nard on Jesus' feet and wipes them with her hair. Matthew and Mark tell of an anointing at Bethany by an unnamed woman; John names her, and adds that Judas, who kept the money bag, was a thief. The chief priests now plan to kill Lazarus too.

The next day Jesus enters Jerusalem. Only John mentions that the crowds carried palm branches, which had been a symbol of Jewish national victory since the days of the Maccabees. They hail him as the King of Israel.

Then some Greeks who have come up to worship at the feast ask Philip if they may see Jesus. This is the moment Jesus has spoken of since Cana. The hour has come, he says, and he speaks of a grain of wheat that must fall into the ground and die. A voice comes from heaven. The chapter closes the first half of the Gospel, with John's reflection, drawn from Isaiah, on why so many did not believe, and with Jesus' last public words.

=== 13
The second half of the Gospel begins, and the pace slows almost to a halt. Chapters thirteen to seventeen all belong to one evening, the last before Jesus' death. John says the meal took place before the Passover Feast, and unlike the other Gospels he does not describe the bread and the cup. How his timing fits with theirs, in which the meal is the Passover itself, has been much discussed.

During supper, Jesus gets up, ties a towel around his waist and washes his disciples' feet. On dusty roads in sandals, feet got filthy, and washing them was a job for the lowest servant. Peter at first refuses to allow it.

Then he becomes troubled and says that one of them will betray him. Here, for the first time, John mentions the disciple whom Jesus loved, reclining next to him. Guests at a formal meal lay on couches around a low table, so he can lean back and ask Jesus quietly who it is. Judas takes the piece of bread Jesus offers and goes out, and John adds four words: and it was night. Jesus then gives his disciples a new commandment, and warns Peter that before the rooster crows he will deny him three times.

=== 14
Judas has gone out into the night, and Jesus is alone at the table with the eleven. He has just told them that he is going where they cannot follow, and that Peter will deny him. They are shaken. This chapter begins what is often called the farewell discourse, which runs through chapter sixteen and closes with the prayer of chapter seventeen.

Jesus begins by telling them not to let their hearts be troubled, and speaks of his Father's house with its many rooms. The talk moves forward through the questions of individual disciples, each of whom reveals how little they yet understand. Thomas says they do not know the way, Philip asks Jesus to show them the Father, and another Judas, not Iscariot, asks why Jesus will reveal himself to them and not to the world.

For the first time Jesus promises another Advocate, the Holy Spirit. The Greek word, Paraclete, means one called alongside to help, and it could be used of a counsel for the defence. The chapter ends with the words, get up, let us go on from here. Yet the teaching continues for three more chapters, perhaps as they walk, and readers have long wondered how the scene unfolds from this point.

=== 15
Jesus is still speaking to the eleven on the last night. He now uses one of the oldest images of Israel in the Scriptures, the vine. Psalms and prophets had pictured God's people as a vine he planted and tended, which too often bore no good fruit. According to the historian Josephus, a great golden vine decorated the entrance to the sanctuary of Herod's temple. Jesus says, I am the true vine, and his Father is the vinedresser, and he describes the disciples as branches, who can bear fruit only by remaining in him.

From the vine he moves to love. He has loved them as the Father has loved him, and he calls them no longer servants but friends. The greatest love, he says, is to lay down one's life for one's friends, words spoken only hours before his death.

The mood then darkens. The world that has hated him will hate them too, and he tells them to expect it. The chapter closes with a second promise of the Advocate, the Spirit of truth, who will testify about him, as the disciples themselves will, because they have been with him from the beginning.

=== 16
The farewell discourse continues, and Jesus speaks frankly about what lies ahead for his followers. They will be put out of the synagogues, and a time will come when those who kill them will think they are serving God. He says he is telling them now, so that when it happens they will remember.

The disciples are filled with grief at the thought of his going away, but Jesus insists it is for their good, because only then will the Advocate come. He describes what the Spirit will do, both in the world and among the disciples, guiding them into all truth.

Then Jesus speaks of a little while, in which they will not see him, and then another little while, in which they will. The disciples whisper to one another that they do not know what he means. He answers with the image of a woman in labour, whose anguish turns to joy when the child is born. At last they think they understand, and say so. Jesus warns them that the hour is coming when they will be scattered and leave him alone. His last words to them before he prays are, take courage; I have overcome the world.

=== 17
The teaching is finished. Jesus lifts his eyes to heaven and prays aloud, with the disciples listening. This is the longest prayer of Jesus recorded in the Gospels, and since the sixteenth century it has often been called his high priestly prayer, because in it he intercedes for his people, as the high priest did for Israel.

It opens with the words, Father, the hour has come. That hour has been anticipated throughout the Gospel, from the wedding at Cana onward. Jesus asks the Father to glorify the Son, speaks of the glory he had before the world existed, and says that he has completed the work he was given to do.

The prayer then widens in stages. He prays for the disciples whom the Father has given him, who must remain in the world after he leaves it, that they will be kept and made holy by the truth. Then he prays for those who will believe through their message in times to come, that they may all be one. These are the last words Jesus speaks to his Father before he goes out to the garden where he will be arrested.

=== 18
Jesus leaves the city with his disciples and crosses the Kidron valley, east of the temple, to a garden on the slopes of the Mount of Olives. The other Gospels call it Gethsemane. Judas knows the place well, because Jesus often met there with his disciples, and he arrives by night with a detachment of soldiers and officers from the chief priests and Pharisees, carrying lanterns, torches and weapons. Jesus steps forward to meet them.

John's account of the night that follows differs in detail from the others. Jesus is taken first to Annas, a former high priest and the father-in-law of Caiaphas, who remained a powerful figure, and only then to Caiaphas himself. Peter follows, and is let into the courtyard through another disciple who is known to the high priest. John interweaves Peter's denials beside the charcoal fire with Jesus' questioning inside.

Early in the morning, Jesus is brought to the Praetorium, the headquarters of Pontius Pilate. The Jewish leaders will not go inside, to avoid becoming ritually unclean before the Passover, so Pilate moves back and forth, questioning Jesus inside and speaking with the accusers outside. His conversation with Jesus turns on kingship and truth.

=== 19
The crowd has called for Barabbas instead of Jesus. Now Pilate has Jesus flogged, and the soldiers mock him with a crown of thorns and a purple robe. Pilate brings him out and presents him to the crowd.

The chief priests then add a religious charge: Jesus has claimed to be the Son of God. Pilate becomes more afraid. When they warn him that releasing Jesus would make him no friend of Caesar, the threat is serious, for a governor accused of tolerating a rival king could lose everything. Pilate takes his seat on the judgement seat at the Stone Pavement, called Gabbatha. John notes that it is the day of Preparation for the Passover, about the sixth hour, around noon, when the Passover lambs were about to be slaughtered in the temple.

Carrying his own cross, Jesus goes out to Golgotha, the Place of the Skull. The sign above him is written in Hebrew, Latin and Greek. Near the cross stand his mother and other women, and the disciple whom Jesus loved. Because the next day is a High Sabbath, the bodies must come down before sunset. Joseph of Arimathea and Nicodemus, both secret followers until now, lay Jesus in a new tomb in a nearby garden.

=== 20
It is the first day of the week, still dark, and Mary Magdalene comes to the tomb. The other Gospels mention other women with her, but John follows Mary alone. She sees that the stone has been rolled away and runs to tell Simon Peter and the disciple whom Jesus loved. The two men run to the tomb, and John records their race, what they see inside, and the moment the other disciple believes.

Mary stays outside, weeping. What follows is one of the most personal scenes in the Gospels. She mistakes the risen Jesus for the gardener, until he speaks her name.

That evening the disciples are gathered behind locked doors, for fear of the authorities, when Jesus comes and stands among them. Thomas, one of the twelve, is not there, and he refuses to believe their report unless he can see and touch the wounds himself. A week later, Jesus comes again. Thomas's response is the fullest confession of faith in the Gospel. The chapter closes with John's statement of why he has written, and many scholars believe it was originally the book's ending.

=== 21
The book seemed to have reached its conclusion, but a further chapter follows, a kind of epilogue. The scene is back in Galilee, beside the lake, here called the Sea of Tiberias. Seven disciples are together: Simon Peter, Thomas, Nathanael from Cana, the sons of Zebedee, and two others. Peter decides to go fishing, and they spend the night on the water and catch nothing.

At dawn a figure stands on the shore and calls out to them. The scene recalls an earlier miraculous catch that Luke records at the start of Peter's call, and John gives the number of fish exactly. On the shore Jesus has made a charcoal fire. The only other charcoal fire in the Gospel burned in the high priest's courtyard on the night Peter denied him.

After breakfast Jesus asks Peter three times whether he loves him, and gives him a charge to care for his sheep. He also speaks of how Peter will die; early tradition holds that Peter was crucified in Rome. When Peter asks about the beloved disciple, Jesus' answer gives rise to a rumour, which the book corrects. The Gospel ends with a word about the disciple who wrote these things, and about how much more could have been told.
