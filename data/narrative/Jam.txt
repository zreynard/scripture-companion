=== intro
The letter of James opens with a name and almost nothing else: James, a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ. He does not say which James he is, and he does not need to. Several men of that name appear in the New Testament. James the son of Zebedee, one of the twelve, was put to death by King Herod in Jerusalem little more than a decade after the crucifixion. The James whom the early church remembered as the author of this letter is another man altogether: the brother of Jesus.

His is a remarkable story. He grew up in the same household in Nazareth, one of four brothers named in the Gospels, alongside Joseph, Simon and Judas, the Jude of a later letter. Christians have understood that family tie in different ways, but the New Testament simply calls him the Lord's brother. During Jesus' ministry, John's Gospel says, his brothers did not believe in him. Then, after the resurrection, Paul records that the risen Jesus appeared to James, and from that point everything changes. In the book of Acts, James becomes the leading figure of the church in Jerusalem. When Peter escapes from prison, he asks that word be sent to James. When the apostles meet to decide whether Gentile believers must keep the law of Moses, it is James who sums up the matter. Paul calls him one of the pillars of the church. Later tradition remembered him as James the Just, a man of such constant prayer that his knees grew hard as a camel's. The Jewish historian Josephus records his death in the early sixties of the first century: in a gap between Roman governors, the high priest had him stoned.

The letter is addressed to the twelve tribes of the Dispersion. That was the name for the Jewish communities scattered beyond the land of Israel, in Syria, Egypt, Asia Minor, Mesopotamia and Rome itself. James is most likely writing to Jewish believers in Jesus living among them, perhaps including those Acts describes as scattered from Jerusalem after the death of Stephen, though some read the phrase as a picture of the whole church, God's people living away from home. The word he uses for their gathering is synagogue. These are ordinary people, many of them poor, some of them labourers whose wages have been withheld, harassed by wealthy men who drag them into court. Within the community there are quarrels, flattery of the rich, careless talk, and traders who plan their profits as if tomorrow were theirs to command.

When it was written is uncertain. If James the Lord's brother wrote it, it must come from before his death, and some scholars place it as early as the forties, which would make it among the very first Christian writings to survive. Others date it later, and some doubt that James himself was the author. What is clear is the world it comes from: Jewish, rooted in the Scriptures of Israel, and close to the earliest days of the church.

It reads less like a personal letter than a collection of wisdom. There are no greetings to friends, no news, no travel plans, and no farewell. Instead there are short, sharp sayings and vivid pictures, in the tradition of Proverbs: a wave tossed by the wind, a horse turned by a bit, a ship steered by a small rudder, a forest set ablaze by a spark, a farmer waiting for the rain. Commands come thick and fast. And although James names Jesus only twice, his words are full of echoes of his brother's teaching, especially the Sermon on the Mount: blessing for those who endure, treasure eaten by moths, a yes that means yes.

The letter does not build a single argument from start to finish. Its subjects circle and return: trials and the wisdom to face them, rich and poor, hearing and doing, the power of the tongue, quarrels, pride and humility. It opens with joy in the middle of trials. It moves through its most famous passage, on faith that shows itself in deeds, a passage readers have long set beside the letters of Paul. It speaks of the tongue and of two kinds of wisdom, of friendship with the world and of plans made without God. And it closes with a fierce warning to the rich, a call to patience until the Lord comes, and a word about prayer for the sick, ending abruptly with a brother brought back from wandering.

James stands first in a group of letters the early church called general or catholic letters, meaning that they were written not to one congregation but to believers more widely. Peter, John and Jude follow. Of all of them, James sounds most like the Old Testament prophets and wise men, a voice from the first generation of believers in Jerusalem, speaking to scattered communities about what it means to live out their faith.

=== 1
After a single line of greeting to the scattered tribes, James goes straight to the hardest thing in his readers' lives: trials of many kinds. In Greek there is a link between the two sentences that English loses, as the ordinary word of greeting gives way at once to a call to consider it pure joy.

The chapter moves the way a wisdom teacher moves, from one saying to the next, often joined by a single word. Trials lead to perseverance, perseverance to maturity, and the thought of lacking anything leads to asking God for wisdom. Brief pictures flash past: a wave driven by the wind, a wild flower withering in the scorching sun. Then James turns from the trials that test from outside to the temptations that pull from within, and insists that these do not come from God.

The last part gathers around the word of God, received and then acted on. James's picture of a man who glances in a mirror and forgets his own face introduces a concern that will run through the whole letter. The chapter closes with a short definition of true religion, and it points to the care of orphans and widows.

=== 2
James now walks his readers into their own gathering. Two visitors arrive at the same moment. One wears a gold ring and fine clothes; the other is poor and shabbily dressed. Who is offered a seat and who is told to stand is a scene James's readers would have recognised. They have been honouring the wealthy, the very people who oppress them and haul them before the courts.

Against this James sets what he calls the royal law, the command from Leviticus to love your neighbour as yourself, and a reminder that the law cannot be kept selectively.

That leads into the passage for which the letter is best known. James imagines someone who claims to have faith but has nothing to show for it, who wishes a hungry brother well and sends him away empty. He calls such faith dead, and he brings two witnesses from Israel's Scriptures: Abraham offering Isaac on the altar, and Rahab of Jericho, who sheltered the spies. James quotes the same verse from Genesis that Paul uses when he writes of faith, and readers have weighed the two together ever since. His own target is plain: a faith that never moves beyond words.

=== 3
Talk of faith that is only words leads James to words themselves. He begins with a caution to those who would be teachers in the community, since teachers work with their tongues, and they will be judged more strictly for it.

Then comes a string of images from everyday life. A small bit in a horse's mouth turns the whole animal. A small rudder steers a great ship through strong winds. A small spark sets a whole forest alight. Every kind of creature has been tamed, but no one can tame the tongue. The same mouth blesses God and curses people made in his likeness, and James asks whether a spring can pour out fresh water and salt, or a fig tree bear olives.

Back in chapter one, James told his readers to ask God for wisdom and to bridle their tongues. Both concerns return here together. The chapter ends by setting two kinds of wisdom side by side, one earthly, bred of jealousy and selfish ambition, the other from above, pure and peace-loving. The bitter rivalry he describes is about to come into the open.

=== 4
The jealousy and selfish ambition James has just described have consequences, and now he names them: conflicts and quarrels within the community. He traces them back to desires at war inside people, to craving what they cannot have, and to prayers that are really requests for more pleasure.

His language becomes sharp and urgent. He calls his readers adulteresses, an image the prophets of Israel had used for a people unfaithful to God, and he sets friendship with the world against friendship with God. Then, quoting the book of Proverbs, he reminds them that God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble. A rapid run of commands follows, calling them to submit to God, resist the devil, draw near and humble themselves, and to stop slandering and judging one another.

The chapter ends with a new audience. Come now, James says, and he turns to the merchants among them, the kind of travelling traders found in every Jewish community of the Dispersion, who speak confidently of going to a city for a year and making a profit. He reminds them that their lives are like a morning mist.

=== 5
James's words to confident merchants turn into something much stronger. Again he says come now, and this time he addresses the rich, with the voice of an Old Testament prophet. Their stored wealth is rotting, their clothes moth-eaten, their gold corroded. The wages they held back from the labourers who harvested their fields are crying out, and the cries have reached the Lord of Hosts. Like the prophets who denounced nations that would never hear them, James speaks over his readers' heads to the wealthy who exploit them.

Then he turns back to his brothers, the poor and the wronged, and calls them to patience until the Lord's coming. His picture is the farmer in the land of Israel, waiting for the autumn and spring rains. He offers the prophets, Job and Elijah as examples of endurance and of prayer.

The final paragraphs are practical and gentle: plain speech without oaths, prayer in trouble and song in gladness, and elders called to pray over the sick and anoint them with oil. James closes not with greetings but with one last concern, for any who wander from the truth.
