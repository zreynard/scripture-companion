=== intro
Obadiah is the shortest book in the Old Testament, a single chapter of twenty-one verses, and it is addressed to a single nation, Edom. Of the prophet himself nothing is known beyond his name, which means servant of the Lord. Several men called Obadiah appear elsewhere in the Old Testament, but none can be identified with him.

Edom's story begins in Genesis. Its people were descended from Esau, the twin brother of Jacob, and the rivalry between the brothers, which began in the womb, never really ended between the nations that came from them. Edom's homeland lay south and east of the Dead Sea, a rugged country of red sandstone mountains and deep gorges, its towns perched on heights that seemed beyond any attacker's reach. Its people grew rich on the trade routes that passed through their land. In the days of Moses, Edom refused Israel passage on the way to Canaan. David later conquered it, and in the centuries that followed it rebelled, was subdued, and rebelled again.

Obadiah speaks of a particular day. Foreigners entered Jerusalem's gates, carried off its wealth and cast lots for the city, and Edom stood by and gloated, joined in the looting, and cut off the fugitives as they fled. Most scholars connect this with the destruction of Jerusalem by the Babylonians in five hundred and eighty-six before Christ. Other parts of the Old Testament remember Edom's part in that disaster with lasting bitterness: a psalm recalls the Edomites crying, tear it down, and Lamentations and Ezekiel both pronounce judgement on them. Some have suggested an earlier occasion, such as a raid on Jerusalem in the ninth century recorded in Chronicles, and the date cannot be fixed with certainty. If the later setting is right, Obadiah belongs with Jeremiah and Ezekiel, in the years of the exile, long after Amos, who comes before him in the Twelve. Several of his lines are very close to an oracle about Edom in the book of Jeremiah.

The vision moves from Edom's pride and fall, to the wrong it did its brother, and then out to the Day of the Lord for all the nations, ending with Mount Zion restored and the kingdom belonging to the Lord.

In the centuries after Obadiah, the Edomites were gradually pushed out of their mountains by Arab peoples, and settled in the south of Judah, in a region later called Idumea. Herod the Great, king of Judea when Jesus was born, came from an Idumean family.

=== 1
The vision opens in the middle of events. A messenger has gone among the nations, calling them to battle against Edom. Then the Lord speaks to Edom itself, secure in the clefts of the rocks. Though it nests among the stars, he says, he will bring it down.

Then the reason is given, and it is personal: the violence done to your brother Jacob. In a run of urgent commands, do not gloat, do not loot, do not stand at the crossroads to cut off the fugitives, the prophet replays the day of Jerusalem's fall as if it were still happening. As you have done, the Lord says, it will be done to you. The closing lines map the lands the returning exiles will possess, from the Negev in the south to Zarephath on the Phoenician coast.
