=== intro
Jude is the last of the short general letters before Revelation, and it comes from another member of Jesus' own family. The writer calls himself Jude, a servant of Jesus Christ and a brother of James. His name is Judas, the Greek form of Judah and a common Jewish name, and English translations usually call him Jude to distinguish him from Judas Iscariot.

The James he mentions is James the brother of the Lord, the leader of the church in Jerusalem and the writer of the letter of James. So Jude too was one of Jesus' brothers. The Gospels name four of them, James, Joseph, Simon and Judas, and record that during Jesus' ministry they did not believe in him. After the resurrection his brothers appear among the believers in Jerusalem, and Paul mentions the Lord's brothers travelling in the work of the gospel. Jude does not call himself the brother of Jesus. He describes himself instead as his servant, and identifies himself through James, the better known of the two. A story from the second century tells of Jude's grandsons, farmers with hands hardened by work, brought before the emperor Domitian and questioned about Christ's kingdom.

His readers knew the Jewish traditions well, and many of them were Jewish believers. The letter belongs to the second half of the first century, and it is closely related to Second Peter, since a long passage appears in both in similar form.

The occasion is urgent. Jude explains that he had meant to write a letter about the salvation he and his readers share, but news has forced him to change course. Certain men have slipped into the community unnoticed. They turn the grace of God into an excuse for immorality, reject authority, and deny Jesus Christ as Master and Lord. They take part in the church's love feasts, the shared meals at which believers gathered, and use them for their own ends.

Jude is a vivid writer who likes to group things in threes. He recalls three examples of judgment from Israel's history, and three figures from the Old Testament who went the wrong way: Cain, Balaam and Korah. He piles up pictures from the natural world to describe the intruders: hidden reefs, clouds without rain, fruitless trees, wild waves, wandering stars. He also draws on two Jewish writings outside the Old Testament, quoting a prophecy attributed to Enoch and referring to a dispute between the archangel Michael and the devil over the body of Moses.

Yet the letter does not end in denunciation. It turns to the readers themselves, calls them to build one another up and to show mercy to those who waver, and closes with one of the most beautiful words of praise in the New Testament.

=== 1
After a brief greeting to those called and kept by God, the letter turns at once to its reason for being. Jude had intended to write about the salvation he and his readers share, but he has heard that intruders have crept in among them, and he must urge them instead to contend for the faith.

He reminds them of what they already know: the generation that came out of Egypt and perished in the wilderness, angels who abandoned their place, Sodom and Gomorrah. Then he describes the intruders in a rapid stream of images, setting them in the line of Cain, Balaam and Korah, and quoting Enoch's prophecy of the Lord coming in judgment.

In the final verses Jude speaks directly and tenderly to his beloved readers, and the letter ends with a soaring word of praise to the God who is able to keep them from stumbling.
