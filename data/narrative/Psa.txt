=== intro
Until now the Bible has mostly told a story. The book of Psalms does something different. It gathers up the voices of the people inside that story and lets us hear them speaking to God. Here are a hundred and fifty poems, the songs and prayers of ancient Israel, written over many centuries and collected into a single book. Its Hebrew title means praises. Our word psalm comes through Greek, from a word for a song sung to the plucking of strings.

The poems span a remarkable stretch of history. One is credited to Moses, which takes us back to the wilderness. Many belong to the age of David and the kingdom he founded, about a thousand years before Christ. Others mourn the destruction of Jerusalem by the Babylonians, and at least one remembers the exiles weeping beside the rivers of Babylon. So the collection grew slowly, through the years of the kings, the exile and the return, and it reached its final shape after the temple was rebuilt. It became the songbook of the second temple, and later of the synagogue and the church.

Most psalms open with a short heading, which the Bible we are reading counts as part of the first verse. These superscriptions are very old. Some name a writer. Some give musical directions: for the choirmaster, with stringed instruments, to a tune with a name such as The Doe of the Dawn. Some use old technical words, such as Maskil, Miktam and Shiggaion. The word Selah appears often within the poems, marking a pause or an interlude. And a handful of headings, all about David, link a psalm to a moment in his life: running from Saul, hiding in a cave, confronted by the prophet Nathan, fleeing his own son Absalom. Most psalms carry no such note, and where Scripture is silent about an occasion, it is wise not to invent one.

David's name stands over more psalms than any other, about half the collection. He was remembered as Israel's singer: the shepherd boy who played the lyre for King Saul, and the king who brought the ark to Jerusalem with music and dancing and appointed Levites to sing before God. Asaph was one of the chief musicians David appointed, and his descendants went on singing at the temple for generations. The sons of Korah were a family of Levites, descended from the Korah who rebelled against Moses, and later served as gatekeepers and singers at the temple. Two psalms are linked with Solomon, one with Moses, one with Heman and one with Ethan, both called Ezrahites. Many psalms are anonymous.

The psalms come in recognisable kinds. The most common is the lament, a cry for help from someone sick, slandered, hunted or abandoned, or from the whole nation in defeat. Laments complain honestly, plead with God, and very often turn, sometimes abruptly, to trust and praise. There are hymns praising God as creator and king, and songs of thanksgiving for a rescue already received. Royal psalms concern Israel's king: his coronation, his wedding, his battles, and God's promise to the house of David. Wisdom psalms reflect on the two ways of life and the puzzle of why the wicked prosper. Near the end of the book come fifteen short songs of ascents, sung by pilgrims going up to Jerusalem for the great festivals.

All of this is poetry, and Hebrew poetry has its own music. It does not rhyme. Instead it works by parallelism: one line makes a statement, and the next echoes it, deepens it, or sets it against its opposite. The Lord is my shepherd; I shall not want. Because the rhythm lies in the thought rather than the sound, it survives translation remarkably well. A few psalms are built as acrostics, each line or stanza beginning with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet. One small thing helps the listener too. Where English Bibles print the LORD, the Hebrew has God's personal name, the name he revealed to Moses at the burning bush.

The collection is arranged in five books, like the five books of Moses. Each of the first four closes with a short blessing of God, and the last psalm is one great closing burst of praise. Psalms one and two stand at the entrance as a gateway: one about the person who delights in God's law, the other about the king God has set on Zion. Book One is almost entirely David's, and full of laments. Book Two opens with the sons of Korah and returns to David. Book Three, darker in tone, belongs largely to Asaph and ends with a grieving question about what has become of God's promise to David's line. Book Four answers with a prayer of Moses and a series of psalms declaring that the Lord reigns. Book Five gathers songs of pilgrimage and thanksgiving, and the whole book ends in a cascade of hallelujahs.

Israel sang these psalms in the temple courts, with lyres and harps, cymbals and trumpets, at the festivals and the daily sacrifices, and prayed them at home and in exile far from Jerusalem. Jesus knew them by heart, quoted them often, and prayed them from the cross. They have been the prayer book of God's people ever since, and each one is meant to be heard whole, in its own voice.

=== 1
The Psalter opens not with a prayer but with a portrait. Psalm one has no heading and no named author. It is a wisdom psalm, describing two kinds of people and the two roads they travel.

The first is the blessed man, a word that means something like truly happy, or well placed in life. He is defined by what he refuses and by what he loves: the law of the Lord, which he turns over in his mind day and night. The word is Torah, meaning instruction, the whole of God's teaching, not a list of rules alone. Such a person is like a tree beside streams of water, rooted, green and fruitful.

The wicked, by contrast, are chaff, the husks blown away when grain is tossed into the wind on the threshing floor. Placed at the front of the book, this short psalm quietly invites every reader to come to the psalms that follow as the first kind of person.

=== 2
The second psalm stands beside the first as the other half of the Psalter's gateway. Psalm one began with the word blessed; Psalm two ends with it. But where the first looked at a single life, this one looks at the nations.

It is a royal psalm, set at a coronation in Jerusalem. In the ancient world a new king's accession was a dangerous moment, when subject peoples might try to break free. Here the kings of the earth plot against the Lord and his anointed one, in Hebrew his messiah. The psalm has no heading, though in the book of Acts the early believers speak of it as David's.

Listen for the changing voices: the rebellious rulers, heaven's laughter, God declaring that he has installed his king on Zion, the king announcing God's decree, you are my son, and finally a warning to the rulers to submit. The New Testament returns to this psalm again and again when it speaks of Jesus as God's Son.

=== 3
Here, for the first time, a psalm carries a heading, and it takes us into one of the darkest chapters of David's life: when he fled from his son Absalom.

The second book of Samuel tells the story. Absalom spent years winning the hearts of the people, then had himself proclaimed king at Hebron and marched on Jerusalem. David left the city, climbing the Mount of Olives barefoot and weeping, and crossed the Jordan. On the way a man of Saul's family pelted him with stones and cursed him, as though God had finally abandoned him.

That is the taunt this psalm repeats: God will not deliver him. Against it David sets God as a shield around him, the one who lifts his head. Then comes a remarkably simple line. He lies down, sleeps, and wakes again, sustained by the Lord. Listen too for the word Selah, which appears here for the first time.

=== 4
The day is ending in Psalm four. Its last line finds David lying down to sleep in peace, and it has long been paired with the morning psalm before it. Its heading brings the first of many musical directions: for the choirmaster, with stringed instruments.

David appeals to God, who has relieved his distress before, and then turns, unusually, to speak to the people around him, men who drag his honour through the mud and chase after lies. He tells them to search their hearts on their beds and be still.

The scene widens. Many are asking who will show them any good, and David answers with a prayer that echoes the old priestly blessing, asking God to let the light of his face shine. The joy God has given him, he says, is greater than the joy of a harvest when grain and new wine abound.

=== 5
Psalm four ended at bedtime; Psalm five begins at daybreak. David lays out his plea before God and waits to see what God will do. The heading asks for flutes.

This is a lament, though a composed one, setting two kinds of people side by side before God. On one side stand the boastful, the liars and the violent, whose throats are open graves. On the other stands the psalmist, who can enter God's house only because of God's own loving devotion.

That phrase will be heard again and again in the psalms, so it deserves a word. The Hebrew is hesed, the loyal, committed love God has bound himself to show his people. The Berean Standard Bible calls it loving devotion; others say steadfast love or mercy.

=== 6
The voice in Psalm six belongs to a man at the end of his strength. He is gravely ill, and fears that his suffering is God's discipline. His bones ache, and night after night he floods his bed with tears. Church tradition counted it the first of seven penitential psalms.

The heading's Sheminith, meaning the eighth, is a musical direction. Listen for the cry how long, O Lord, how long, which will return often in the psalms, and for the reason the psalmist gives God for saving him: in Sheol, the shadowy realm of the dead, no one gives him praise.

Then, without warning, the mood changes. He orders his enemies away, because the Lord has heard his weeping. He says it three times in quick succession, and the prayer that began in tears ends in confidence.

=== 7
The heading of Psalm seven calls it a Shiggaion, one of the old musical terms of the Psalter. It names a cause, the words of Cush, a Benjamite. Who Cush was, Scripture never says. We know only that David had bitter enemies in Saul's tribe of Benjamin, and that this man had evidently been accusing him.

The psalm is the plea of someone falsely charged, hunted as if by a lion. David swears an oath of innocence, calling disaster down on himself if he has really done the wrong he is accused of.

Then the scene becomes a courtroom. God is summoned to take his seat above the assembled nations, the righteous judge who searches hearts and minds. By the close, the wicked man has dug a pit and fallen into it himself, and David gives thanks to the Lord Most High.

=== 8
After seven psalms of struggle comes the first pure hymn of praise. Psalm eight celebrates God's glory in creation, and the surprising place he has given human beings within it. The heading mentions the Gittith, a musical term whose name recalls the Philistine city of Gath.

The same exclamation opens and closes the psalm: how majestic is your name in all the earth. Between them the poet stands under the night sky. He looks at the moon and stars, the work of God's fingers, and the vastness of it makes him ask what man is, that God should care for him.

The answer echoes the first chapter of Genesis. God has crowned human beings with glory and honour and set them over his works, the flocks and herds, the wild animals, the birds and the fish. The letter to the Hebrews later reads these lines in the light of Jesus.

=== 9
Psalm nine is a song of thanks, and it belongs closely with Psalm ten. Hidden in the Hebrew is an acrostic, its stanzas beginning with successive letters of the alphabet, which starts here and runs on, somewhat broken, into the next psalm. The ancient Greek translation treated the two as one.

The heading names a tune, The Death of the Son. God is praised as a righteous judge on his throne, who has overthrown hostile nations and remembers the cry of the afflicted, and Zion appears as the place where he dwells.

Yet the mood is not simply triumphant. Midway through, the singer asks for mercy, still pressed by enemies, and the psalm closes not with a victory song but with a prayer that the nations may learn they are only men. Near the end, Higgaion and Selah appear together, calling for a pause to reflect.

=== 10
Psalm ten has no heading of its own, and it carries on the acrostic begun in Psalm nine. But the confidence of that psalm gives way here to a question sufferers have asked in every age: why, O Lord, do you stand far off?

Much of the psalm is a portrait of the wicked man. He is proud, greedy and secure, sure that God never notices. Then the picture turns predatory. He lurks near the villages like a lion in a thicket, waiting to drag off the helpless in his net.

Against that portrait the psalmist calls on God to rise and remember the fatherless and the oppressed. By the end the opening question has its answer, not as an explanation but as a declaration: the Lord is King forever and ever, and he hears the desire of the humble.

=== 11
A short song of trust, Psalm eleven begins with a scene. Someone is urging David to run: flee like a bird to your mountain. The wicked have their arrows on the string, they say, and if the foundations are crumbling, what can the righteous do?

David does not dispute the danger. Instead he lifts his eyes. The Lord is in his holy temple, the Lord is on his heavenly throne, and his eyes are examining everyone, the righteous and the wicked alike. The fire and sulphur that will rain on the wicked recall the judgement on Sodom in Genesis.

In a few lines the psalm captures the choice between panic and refuge, and it closes with a quiet and hopeful promise: the upright will see his face.

=== 12
Psalm twelve laments a whole society. The psalmist looks around and the faithful seem to have vanished. What is left is a world of talk: flattering lips, double hearts, and people who boast that their tongues will carry them, since no one can master them.

Then, at the centre, God himself speaks. Because the poor are plundered and the needy groan, he says, he will now arise. Words of God set within a lament appear several times in the psalms, and they may reflect a moment in worship when a divine answer was declared.

The psalmist responds that the Lord's words, unlike the liars', are flawless, like silver refined in a furnace, like gold purified seven times. Yet nothing is neatly resolved. The last line looks again at the world as it still is, where the wicked strut about and vileness is prized.

=== 13
Few laments are as short as Psalm thirteen, or as clear. In six verses it travels the whole path so many psalms take, from complaint, to petition, to trust.

It opens with four questions, each beginning how long: how long will God forget, how long will he hide his face, how long must the psalmist wrestle with sorrow, how long will his enemy prevail. The psalm is left open for any troubled person to take up.

Then comes a plea: look on me, answer me, give light to my eyes before I sleep in death. And finally the turn, marked by a single word, but. Nothing outward has changed yet. What has changed is where the psalmist rests his weight, on God's loving devotion, and the last line is a song.

=== 14
The fool says in his heart, there is no God. The Hebrew word for fool here does not mean someone lacking intelligence. It describes a person who is morally senseless, who lives as though God does not matter and will never call anyone to account.

The Lord looks down from heaven, searching for anyone who understands and seeks him, and finds no one who does good. The apostle Paul quotes these lines in his letter to the Romans. Yet the psalm also speaks of evildoers devouring God's people like bread, and of God standing with the righteous.

This poem appears again, almost word for word, as Psalm fifty-three. Here it closes with a longing that the salvation of Israel would come out of Zion, and that the Lord would restore his captive people, so that Jacob might rejoice and Israel be glad.

=== 15
Who may abide in your tent? Who may dwell on your holy mountain? Psalm fifteen begins with a question asked at the threshold of God's house. The tent recalls the tabernacle of the wilderness years, and the tent David pitched on Zion for the ark of the covenant.

It is often called an entrance psalm, with one voice asking and another answering. The answer says nothing about sacrifice or ritual. It describes character: someone who walks with integrity, speaks truth from the heart, does not slander his neighbour, keeps his word even when it costs him, lends without interest and refuses a bribe.

Such a person, the psalm says, will never be shaken. Psalm twenty-four will ask almost the same question in a grander setting.

=== 16
Psalm sixteen is called a Miktam of David. It is a song of trust, and its central image comes from the land of Israel itself.

When the tribes entered Canaan under Joshua, the land was divided by lot and marked out with boundary lines. The tribe of Levi received no land; the Lord himself was their inheritance. David takes up that language. The Lord is his portion and his cup, his boundary lines have fallen in pleasant places, and his inheritance is delightful. Alongside that joy he refuses any part in the worship of other gods.

The last lines reach beyond this life. God will not abandon him to Sheol, or let his holy one see decay. On the day of Pentecost, the apostle Peter quoted these words in Jerusalem and applied them to the resurrection of Jesus.

=== 17
Headed simply a prayer of David, Psalm seventeen is the plea of an innocent man under attack. He asks God to hear a just case, offered from lips without deceit. God has tested his heart, even at night, and found no evil.

His claim is not to be sinless, but to be in the right in this conflict. His enemies have tracked him down and surrounded him, eyes fixed on throwing him to the ground, like a young lion crouching in ambush.

At the centre come two of the tenderest images in the book. He asks to be kept as the apple of God's eye, the pupil that anyone instinctively protects, and hidden in the shadow of his wings, like a bird sheltering its young. The prayer closes at waking, with the singer satisfied in the presence of God.

=== 18
Psalm eighteen is the longest psalm in Book One, and one of the few whose heading tells a story. David sang it on the day the Lord delivered him from all his enemies and from the hand of Saul. The same song appears, with small differences, near the end of the second book of Samuel, where it looks back across his whole life: the years as a fugitive, the wars that followed, his rise to the throne.

It is a royal thanksgiving in great movements. First comes the cry for help from a man caught in the cords of death. Then God answers, and his coming is overwhelming: earthquake, smoke and fire, darkness, thunder, hail and lightning, God riding a cherub on the wings of the wind. It recalls the Lord's descent on Mount Sinai.

After the rescue, David reflects on God's faithfulness to the faithful. Then the song becomes a warrior's hymn, as God trains his hands for battle, sets his feet on the heights and makes him head of nations. The last line names David and promises loving devotion to his descendants forever.

=== 19
Psalm nineteen moves through two great subjects and then turns inward. It begins with the sky. The heavens declare the glory of God, without words or voice, yet their message reaches the whole earth. The sun is a bridegroom leaving his chamber, a champion eager to run his course. In this first part the poet uses the general Hebrew word for God.

Then he begins speaking of the law, and the name changes. Now it is the LORD, God's personal covenant name, repeated again and again. His law, testimony, precepts and commandments are praised in balanced lines, more precious than gold and sweeter than honey. The God who speaks silently through creation has spoken plainly to Israel.

Finally the psalmist looks at himself, asking to be cleansed from hidden faults and kept from wilful sin. His closing prayer, that the words of his mouth and the meditation of his heart may please God, has been prayed by countless readers since.

=== 20
Psalms twenty and twenty-one belong together. Both are royal psalms, and they read like two moments in one event: a prayer before battle, and thanks after it.

Here the king is about to march out to war, and the people pray for him. May the Lord answer him in the day of trouble, send help from the sanctuary in Zion, remember his offerings and grant the desire of his heart. Kings offered sacrifices before setting out on a campaign.

Halfway through, a single voice speaks: now I know that the Lord saves his anointed. Then the famous contrast. Some trust in chariots and some in horses, the great weapons of the age, but Israel trusts in the name of the Lord. The last cry belongs to the whole congregation: O Lord, save the king.

=== 21
Psalm twenty asked God to grant the king his heart's desire. Psalm twenty-one announces that he has. The prayer before battle has become thanksgiving after it, and again it is the gathered people speaking to God about their king.

They celebrate what God has given him: rich blessings, a crown of pure gold, long life, splendour and joy in God's presence. At the centre stands the reason for it all. The king trusts in the Lord, and through the loving devotion of the Most High he will not be shaken.

Then the address turns, either to the king or to God himself, and the tone grows fierce, as enemies are seized, consumed as if in a furnace and put to flight. In the final line the congregation lifts its praise back to God, for his strength.

=== 22
My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? Psalm twenty-two opens with the most desolate words in the collection. The heading names a tune, The Doe of the Dawn, and attributes the psalm to David.

It is a lament, and an extreme one. Onlookers mock the sufferer, sneering that he trusted in the Lord, so let the Lord rescue him. Enemies surround him like the strong bulls of Bashan, the rich cattle country east of the Jordan, then like roaring lions, then like a pack of dogs. His strength is poured out like water, his bones are on display, and those around him divide his garments and cast lots for his clothing.

The Gospels record that Jesus cried out the opening words of this psalm from the cross, and the evangelists saw in it the mockery of the crowd and the soldiers gambling for his clothes. But the psalm does not end where it begins. Two-thirds of the way through it turns sharply, with God's answer, and the cry becomes praise, spreading from the singer's brothers to all Israel, to the ends of the earth, and to a people yet unborn.

=== 23
After the depths of Psalm twenty-two comes the most familiar psalm of all. It is a song of trust attributed to David, who spent his youth as a shepherd in the hills around Bethlehem, guarding his father's flocks from lions and bears.

In the ancient Near East, kings were often called the shepherds of their people. Here the king himself becomes the sheep. The Lord leads him to green pastures and quiet waters, restores him, and guides him along right paths. Even the valley of the shadow of death holds no terror, because the shepherd is with him, with rod and staff, the club that drives off predators and the crook that guides.

Midway through, the picture changes from open country to a banquet. God becomes the host who prepares a table, anoints his guest's head with oil and fills his cup to overflowing, while enemies look on. At the last, the guest is at home in the house of the Lord.

=== 24
The earth is the Lord's, and the fullness thereof. So begins Psalm twenty-four, a song for a procession, declaring that the whole world belongs to the God who founded it upon the seas. Then, like Psalm fifteen, it asks who may climb the hill of the Lord, and answers: the one with clean hands and a pure heart.

In the final section the worshippers stand at the city gates. One voice calls on the ancient doors to lift up their heads, so that the King of Glory may come in. Another asks, who is this King of Glory? The exchange is repeated, more grandly the second time, until the answer rings out: the Lord of Hosts, commander of the armies of heaven.

=== 25
A prayer for guidance and forgiveness, Psalm twenty-five is carefully built as an acrostic, each verse in the Hebrew beginning with the next letter of the alphabet, a pattern of order and completeness that cannot survive translation.

The psalmist is lonely and afflicted, pressed by enemies, but his deepest concern is God's ways. Again and again he asks to be shown, taught and led. He asks God not to remember the sins of his youth, but to remember him according to his loving devotion, for the Lord is good and upright, and therefore shows sinners the way.

Guidance, confession and pleas for rescue weave in and out of one another, as they so often do in life. When the alphabet is complete, one more line is added, widening the prayer from one person to the nation: redeem Israel, O God, from all its distress.

=== 26
Vindicate me, O Lord, for I have walked with integrity. Like Psalms seven and seventeen, Psalm twenty-six is not a claim to sinlessness. It is the appeal of someone who fears being swept away with the wicked, and asks God to examine his heart and see the difference.

He names the company he refuses to keep: the deceitful, the hypocrites, the mob of evildoers, men whose right hands are full of bribes. And he names the place he loves, the house where God dwells, where his glory resides.

At the centre is a picture of worship. He washes his hands in innocence, a sign of being clean of guilt, and goes around God's altar, singing aloud of his wonderful works. By the last line he stands on level ground, blessing the Lord in the congregation.

=== 27
The Lord is my light and my salvation, whom shall I fear? Psalm twenty-seven begins with ringing confidence. Even if an army camps around him and war breaks out, David says, his heart will not give way.

Beneath that confidence lies a single longing. One thing he asks above all: to dwell in the house of the Lord, to gaze on his beauty and seek him in his temple. There God will hide him in his shelter and set him high upon a rock.

Halfway through, the tone changes sharply. Now he is pleading: hear me, do not hide your face, do not turn me away. False witnesses are rising against him, breathing violence. Yet the trust of the opening holds. He is sure he will see the Lord's goodness in the land of the living, and he ends by urging himself, or his hearers, to wait for the Lord.

=== 28
Psalm twenty-eight travels the lament's familiar road, from distress to thanksgiving, in a short space. It opens with a fear of silence. If God does not answer, David says, he will be like those going down to the Pit, the grave.

He prays with his hands raised toward God's holy sanctuary, the inner room where the ark of the covenant rested. His fear is of being dragged away with the wicked, who speak peace to their neighbours with malice in their hearts.

Then, with no explanation, prayer becomes praise. Blessed be the Lord, for he has heard. As often in David's psalms, the final lines widen from the king's own need to the people he leads. The Lord is the strength of his people and a stronghold for his anointed, and David asks him to shepherd Israel and carry them forever.

=== 29
A thunderstorm rolls through Psalm twenty-nine. The hymn opens in heaven, calling the heavenly beings to give the Lord the glory due his name, and then brings the storm down to earth.

Seven times we hear the voice of the Lord, like peals of thunder. The storm comes in over the many waters and sweeps across the land. It shatters the cedars of Lebanon, makes the mountains skip like calves, Sirion among them, an old name for Mount Hermon, and shakes the wilderness of Kadesh.

Israel's Canaanite neighbours credited the storm to their god Baal, but here the thunder is the voice of the Lord. Meanwhile in his temple all cry, glory. The storm passes into calm, with the Lord enthroned over the flood as king forever, blessing his people with peace.

=== 30
The singer of Psalm thirty has been brought back from the edge of death, and this is his song of thanks. Its heading reads: a song for the dedication of the temple, of David.

The psalm itself is intensely personal. Grown secure in prosperity, the singer told himself he would never be shaken. Then God hid his face, and sickness brought him near the Pit. He remembers his prayer: can the dust praise you?

God heard and healed him, and the psalm is full of reversals: weeping that lasts for a night and joy in the morning, mourning turned into dancing, sackcloth stripped away and replaced with gladness.

=== 31
In grave danger, the singer of Psalm thirty-one keeps returning to God as his rock and refuge. Its best-known line comes early: into your hands I commit my spirit. According to Luke's Gospel, Jesus spoke these words as he died.

The psalm swings more than once between distress and confidence. The singer is wasting away with grief, a disgrace to his neighbours, avoided by friends in the street, forgotten like a dead man, a broken pot. He hears whispering all around, and a phrase the prophet Jeremiah would later use many times: terror on every side. Against all this he sets a simple affirmation. My times are in your hands.

Near the end he gives thanks for God's loving devotion shown to him in a city under siege, then turns outward, urging all God's people to be strong and take heart.

=== 32
Psalm thirty-two is called a Maskil, a song of instruction. It celebrates the joy of being forgiven, and church tradition counted it the second of the penitential psalms. Paul quotes its opening in Romans as David's description of that blessing.

It begins with two beatitudes for the one whose sins are covered. Then the psalmist tells his story. While he kept silent about his sin, he wasted away under God's heavy hand, his strength dried up as in summer heat. When at last he confessed, God forgave. The sin is never named.

In the second half God himself speaks, promising to instruct and guide, and warning against being like a stubborn horse or mule that must be steered with bit and bridle. Then comes a call to the righteous to rejoice.

=== 33
Psalm thirty-two closed with a call to the righteous to shout for joy. Psalm thirty-three, which has no heading, takes up that call and turns it into a full hymn of praise.

It begins with music: harp and ten-stringed lyre, a new song played skilfully with shouts of joy. The reasons for praise are on the largest scale. The Lord made the heavens by his word and the stars by the breath of his mouth. He gathers the waters of the sea and stores up the deep. He spoke, and it came to be.

From creation the psalm moves to history. The plans of the nations come to nothing, but the Lord's purposes stand forever. No king is saved by the size of his army, and a warhorse is a vain hope. The congregation waits quietly for the Lord, their help and shield, asking that his loving devotion rest on them.

=== 34
The heading of Psalm thirty-four takes us back to David's years on the run from Saul: when he pretended to be insane before Abimelech. The first book of Samuel tells the story. David fled into Philistine territory, to Gath, the home town of Goliath. There he was recognised, and in fear for his life he acted the madman, scrawling on the gate and letting saliva run down his beard, until the king sent him away. In Samuel the king is called Achish. Abimelech was a royal title among the Philistines, much as Pharaoh was in Egypt.

The psalm is a thanksgiving, and like Psalm twenty-five an acrostic. David invites others to magnify the Lord with him, because this poor man called and the Lord heard. Then he becomes a teacher, calling his hearers children. Listen for the invitation taste and see that the Lord is good, and the promise that the Lord is near to the brokenhearted.

=== 35
Contend with my opponents, O Lord. The man who prays Psalm thirty-five is under attack from people he once treated as friends. He asks the Lord to fight for him like a warrior, with shield, spear and javelin. Then the scene becomes a law court, where hostile witnesses accuse him of things he knows nothing about.

What wounds him most is their ingratitude. When they were sick, he wore sackcloth and fasted for them, grieving as if for a brother or his own mother. Now that he has stumbled, they gather in glee, mocking him like jesters at a feast and crying, aha, aha.

The psalm moves through three rounds of complaint and prayer, each ending with a promise of praise. Its pleas for his enemies' shame are hard to hear, but they come from someone who has handed the matter to God rather than taking revenge himself.

=== 36
Psalm thirty-six sets two portraits side by side. Its heading calls David the servant of the Lord, as Psalm eighteen did, and it opens with an oracle in his heart about the wicked.

The wicked man has no fear of God before his eyes. He is too pleased with himself to see his own sin, and he lies on his bed plotting evil.

Then, without transition, the psalmist lifts his eyes, and the language soars. God's loving devotion reaches to the heavens and his faithfulness to the clouds, his righteousness is like the highest mountains and his judgements like the great deep. People shelter in the shadow of his wings, feast in his house and drink from his river of delights. With you is the fountain of life, he says, and in your light we see light. A short prayer for protection follows, and a glimpse of evildoers fallen, unable to rise.

=== 37
Less a prayer than a body of counsel, Psalm thirty-seven is a wisdom psalm and the longest of David's acrostics. Its speaker says he was once young and now is old.

Its subject troubled Israel's wise men: what should the faithful do when the wicked prosper? The answer begins with its opening words, do not fret. The success of the wicked is brief. They wither like grass and vanish like smoke, while those who wait for the Lord will inherit the land. That phrase recurs throughout, recalling God's promise to Israel's ancestors, and Jesus echoes it in the Sermon on the Mount: the meek will inherit the earth.

Because the psalm follows the alphabet, it moves less by argument than by return, circling its theme from many angles. Listen for its gentle commands: trust in the Lord, delight in him, commit your way to him, be still, and wait.

=== 38
Psalm thirty-eight opens almost exactly as Psalm six did, and church tradition counted it the third of the penitential psalms. Its heading adds the words for remembrance: this is a prayer that brings the sufferer's case before God.

The psalmist is ill, and he links his suffering directly to his own sin. His wounds fester, his back burns with pain, his heart pounds and the light has gone from his eyes. His friends and family keep their distance, while enemies lay snares.

His response is striking. Like a deaf man who cannot hear and a mute who cannot answer, he refuses to argue with his accusers and waits instead for the Lord. He confesses his iniquity openly. There is no turn to praise here, only a closing plea for God to come quickly, the Lord his Saviour.

=== 39
The heading of Psalm thirty-nine names Jeduthun, one of the three leaders of music David appointed, alongside Asaph and Heman. The psalm is among the most personal and sombre in the book.

It begins with silence. The psalmist resolved to muzzle his tongue in front of the wicked, but holding it in only made his distress burn hotter, until at last he spoke. His words are for God alone: show me my end, let me know how fleeting my life is.

What follows is a meditation on the brevity of life. A lifetime is a few handbreadths. Each person is a mere breath, a passing shadow who heaps up wealth without knowing who will gather it. He calls himself a foreigner dwelling with God, a stranger like his fathers. Unusually, the psalm finds no resolution, and its last words ask God to look away for a while, so that he may smile again before he is gone.

=== 40
Psalm forty begins where many laments end, with the answer already received. I waited patiently for the Lord, David says, and he heard my cry. God lifted him from the pit and the miry clay, set his feet upon a rock, and put a new song in his mouth.

That song becomes testimony before the great assembly. Within it comes a passage about sacrifice. God did not want sacrifices and offerings, David says, but opened his ears, and he answered, here I am, I delight to do your will. The letter to the Hebrews places these lines on the lips of Christ.

Then the mood darkens. Troubles beyond counting surround him again, his sins have overtaken him, and his heart fails. The psalm that began in thanksgiving ends in urgent prayer: O my God, do not delay. This closing section will reappear almost word for word as Psalm seventy.

=== 41
Psalm forty-one brings Book One to its close. It begins as Psalm one did, with the word blessed, but here the blessing rests on the one who cares for the poor, whom the Lord will sustain on his sickbed.

Then the psalmist speaks of his own illness. He confesses his sin and asks for healing. Meanwhile his enemies wait for him to die. Visitors feign sympathy at his bedside, then go out and spread word that he will never rise again. Worst of all, a close friend, one who shared his bread, has lifted his heel against him. In John's Gospel, Jesus quotes this line at the Last Supper, speaking of his betrayer.

After a final word of confidence comes a line that belongs not to this psalm alone but to the whole first book: blessed be the Lord, the God of Israel, from everlasting to everlasting. Amen and Amen.

=== 42
With Psalm forty-two, Book Two of the Psalter begins, and with it a run of psalms belonging to the sons of Korah, the family of Levite singers. From here until well into Book Three, the poems usually call God simply God, rather than using his personal name, the LORD.

Psalms forty-two and forty-three form one poem, bound by a refrain sung three times: why are you downcast, O my soul? Put your hope in God. The singer is far from Jerusalem, in the land of the Jordan and the peaks of Hermon in the far north, where the river's headwaters pour down from the mountain. He remembers leading crowds in festive procession to the house of God. Now he thirsts for God as a deer pants for streams of water, while people taunt him, where is your God?

Each time the refrain returns, he answers his own despair.

=== 43
Psalm forty-three has no heading, and it carries on the poem begun in Psalm forty-two. The same exile is speaking, still far from God's house, still mourning under the enemy's oppression.

Now, though, lament turns into request. He asks God to plead his case against an ungodly nation and deceitful men. Then comes the heart of this final stanza, a prayer that God would send out his light and his truth, like guides, to lead him back to the holy mountain where God dwells.

He pictures the homecoming: going up to the altar of God, to God his greatest joy, and praising him with the harp. The despair has not yet lifted. But when the refrain sounds for the third and last time, it rings with the hope of that return.

=== 44
A whole nation is lamenting in Psalm forty-four, and it begins by remembering. The fathers have told how God drove out the nations and planted Israel in the land, not by their own swords, but by his right hand. The people still trust him. They do not rely on bow or sword; they boast in God all day long.

Then comes the shock. God has rejected them. The army has been routed, the people scattered among the nations, sold off as cheaply as sheep and made a laughingstock. What makes it so striking is its protest. Israel has not forgotten God or turned to other gods, and still this has happened. For your sake we face death all day long, they say, a line Paul quotes in Romans. The last words are a bold cry for God to wake up.

=== 45
Psalm forty-five is unlike any other in the collection. Its heading calls it a love song, to the tune of The Lilies, and it was written for a royal wedding.

The poet tells us his heart is stirred and his tongue is the pen of a skilled writer. Then he addresses the king, praising his beauty and gracious speech, and his majesty as a warrior riding out for truth and justice. In one remarkable line the throne is addressed as the throne of God, enduring forever. The letter to the Hebrews quotes these verses of the Son of God.

The scene moves to the bride, a princess from another land, urged to forget her people and her father's house. Gifts arrive from Tyre, the wealthy Phoenician port. She is led into the palace in gold-embroidered robes, and the king is promised sons who will be princes in the land.

=== 46
God is our refuge and strength. Psalm forty-six is a song of confidence when the whole world seems to be collapsing, and centuries later it inspired Martin Luther's hymn A Mighty Fortress Is Our God. The heading's Alamoth, meaning young women, is another musical direction.

The psalm has three stanzas, and the second and third end with a refrain: the Lord of Hosts is with us, the God of Jacob is our fortress. First, nature itself is in upheaval, mountains toppling into the sea. Then the scene shifts to the city of God, which in reality had no great river, but here is gladdened by streams and steadied by God's presence while nations rage and kingdoms fall.

Finally the listener is invited to come and see the Lord ending wars, breaking bows and burning shields. Then God himself speaks: be still, and know that I am God.

=== 47
Clap your hands, all you peoples. Psalm forty-seven is a hymn to God as king of all the earth, and it is full of noise.

At its centre is a dramatic picture. God has gone up amid shouts of joy, the Lord with the sound of the horn, the ram's horn that sounded at Israel's great occasions and at the crowning of kings. It may echo a procession in which the ark of the covenant, the symbol of God's throne, was carried up to Zion. Then the call to sing praises rings out again and again.

The psalm looks to the nations as much as to Israel. God has chosen Jacob's inheritance, but he reigns over every people. In the final scene the nobles of the nations gather as the people of the God of Abraham, recalling the promise that through Abraham all the families of the earth would be blessed.

=== 48
Jerusalem itself is the subject of Psalm forty-eight, a song of Zion celebrating the city of the great King. It completes a trio of psalms about God's reign among his people.

It praises the height and beauty of Mount Zion, likening it to Zaphon, the sacred mountain of the north in Canaanite myth, the home of Baal. For Israel the true holy mountain is Zion, and its true defence is God within her citadels.

Then a story is told in quick strokes. Kings joined forces and advanced on the city. They saw it, were astounded and fled in terror, seized by pains like a woman in labour, while the great ships of Tarshish were wrecked by an east wind. No particular siege is named. In the final lines the worshippers are invited to walk around Zion, count her towers and study her ramparts, so that they can tell the next generation that this God is their God forever.

=== 49
Hear this, all you peoples. Psalm forty-nine is a wisdom psalm, and it summons everyone, high and low, rich and poor. The singer promises to set out a riddle to the music of the lyre.

The riddle is wealth. Why should the faithful fear when rich and ruthless men surround them? Because money cannot buy what matters most. No one can pay God a ransom to escape death. Wise and foolish alike die and leave their riches to others. They may name their lands after themselves, but the grave becomes their lasting home. Twice the refrain returns: a man without understanding, for all his wealth, is like the beasts that perish.

Death is pictured as a shepherd herding the proud into Sheol. But in the middle comes one of the clearest flashes of hope beyond death in the Old Testament: God will redeem my life from Sheol, for he will surely take me to himself.

=== 50
Psalm fifty brings in a new name. It is the first psalm of Asaph, one of the chief musicians David appointed, and the only one of his psalms found outside Book Three.

The scene is a courtroom on the grandest scale. God shines out from Zion, with fire before him and a tempest around him, and summons heaven and earth as witnesses while he judges his own people. Then he speaks, at length.

His first charge is not that Israel has neglected its sacrifices, but that it has misunderstood them. Every animal of the forest is his already, the cattle on a thousand hills. He does not need to be fed. What he wants is thanksgiving, kept vows, and a people who call on him in trouble. His second charge is sharper, aimed at those who recite his covenant while befriending thieves and slandering their own brothers.

=== 51
Few psalm headings carry as much weight as this one: a Psalm of David, when Nathan the prophet came to him after his adultery with Bathsheba. The second book of Samuel tells the story. While his army was at war, David took Bathsheba, the wife of one of his soldiers, Uriah the Hittite. When she became pregnant, he arranged for Uriah to be killed in battle and married her. Then the prophet Nathan came to the king with a story about a rich man who stole a poor man's only lamb, and when David burned with anger, Nathan said to him, you are the man.

Psalm fifty-one is the prayer the heading links with that moment, and it became the best known of the penitential psalms. David makes no excuses. He pleads for mercy on the ground of God's loving devotion alone, and asks to be purified with hyssop, the plant used in Israel's rites of cleansing. He asks for far more than pardon: a clean heart, a renewed spirit, the joy of salvation restored. In its final lines the prayer turns from the king to the city, asking God to build up the walls of Jerusalem.

=== 52
The heading of Psalm fifty-two recalls one of the ugliest episodes of David's years on the run. Fleeing from Saul, David came to the town of Nob, where the priest Ahimelech, not knowing he was a fugitive, gave him bread and the sword of Goliath. One of Saul's men was there that day, his chief herdsman, Doeg the Edomite. He reported what he had seen, and when Saul's own guards refused to strike the priests, Doeg killed them himself, eighty-five in all, and then the people of their town.

The psalm is addressed to a mighty man who boasts of evil, whose tongue is like a sharpened razor, and it foretells his uprooting. Beside him it sets a very different picture: the psalmist as an olive tree flourishing in the house of God, trusting in God's loving devotion forever.

=== 53
Psalm fifty-three will sound familiar. It is almost the same poem as Psalm fourteen, beginning again with the fool who says in his heart there is no God. The heading adds a musical term, Mahalath.

Two differences stand out. The first reflects this part of the Psalter: where Psalm fourteen used the personal name of the Lord, this version says God. The second comes near the end. Instead of God's shelter for the poor, it describes enemies seized with dread and the bones of those who besieged God's people scattered, so that the old psalm now celebrates a deliverance from attack.

=== 54
Psalm fifty-four is short and urgent, and its heading takes us back again to the years when Saul was hunting David. The Ziphites lived in the wilderness of Judah, in the barren hills south-east of Hebron. The first book of Samuel tells how they went to Saul, reported that David was hiding among them, and offered to hand him over. Saul closed in until the two were on opposite sides of the same mountain, and only news of a Philistine raid called him away. The Ziphites would betray David a second time.

The psalm speaks of strangers rising against him, ruthless men without regard for God. In a few verses David moves from a cry for rescue to confidence that God is his helper, and promises a freewill offering for a deliverance he already speaks of as done.

=== 55
At the centre of Psalm fifty-five is a wound: betrayal by a close friend. The psalmist is shaking with fear, the terrors of death upon him, and his first impulse is escape. If only he had wings like a dove, he would fly away and stay in the wilderness, far from the storm.

The city around him is full of violence and strife, oppression and deceit in its streets. Then he says what hurts most. It is not an enemy who taunts him, but a companion, someone he walked with among the crowds going up to the house of God. That friend has broken his covenant, his words smoother than butter, with war in his heart.

The psalm swings between anguish and trust, and among its harsh words lies a line that has steadied many since: cast your burden on the Lord, and he will sustain you.

=== 56
Psalm fifty-six belongs to the same dangerous episode as Psalm thirty-four. The heading says it was written when the Philistines seized David in Gath, the city where he had sought refuge from Saul and found himself recognised instead. Its tune has a haunting name: A Dove on Distant Oaks.

David describes enemies who hound him all day long, twisting his words, lurking and watching his steps. Twice a refrain breaks through the fear: in God I trust, I will not be afraid, what can man do to me?

Between them comes a tender image. God has counted David's wanderings, and David asks him to keep his tears in a bottle and record them in his book. Nothing he has suffered has gone unnoticed. Vows of thanksgiving follow, and the hope of walking before God in the light of life.

=== 57
Psalm fifty-seven is set when David fled from Saul into the cave. Two caves feature in his story in the first book of Samuel: the cave of Adullam, where he first took refuge and gathered a band of outcasts, and a cave near En-gedi by the Dead Sea, where Saul himself walked in unaware, and David, hidden in the darkness, cut off a corner of his robe rather than kill him.

This is the first of several psalms set to the tune Do Not Destroy. David takes shelter in the shadow of God's wings while he lies among lions, men whose teeth are spears. A refrain divides the psalm: be exalted, O God, above the heavens.

In the second half the mood lifts. His heart is steadfast, and he will wake the harp and lyre and awaken the dawn. These closing lines reappear in Psalm one hundred and eight.

=== 58
Psalm fifty-eight confronts rulers who are supposed to dispense justice and do the opposite. The charge is plain. In their hearts they devise injustice, and with their hands they deal out violence.

David describes them as liars from birth, venomous as a cobra that stops its ears against the snake charmer. Then come some of the fiercest prayers in the Psalter. He asks God to break their teeth, to make them vanish like water draining away, like a slug dissolving as it goes, like a stillborn child that never sees the sun.

These are hard words, from someone who has watched the powerful get away with cruelty. But he leaves the matter in God's hands, and what he most wants the world to see is this: there is surely a God who judges the earth.

=== 59
The heading of Psalm fifty-nine takes us to the night David escaped from his own house. In the first book of Samuel, Saul's jealousy has turned murderous, and he sends men to watch the house and kill David in the morning. David's wife Michal, Saul's daughter, lets him down through a window and puts an idol in his bed, covered with a garment, to buy him time.

The psalm pictures enemies who return each evening, snarling like dogs and prowling around the city, sure that no one hears them. Against them stands God, who laughs at them, and whom David calls again and again his strength and his fortress.

Two refrains shape the poem, one about the prowling dogs and one in praise of God's strength. By its last lines night has given way to morning, and David is singing of God's loving devotion.

=== 60
A defeat lies behind Psalm sixty, a lament for the nation, and its heading sets it in David's wars. He was fighting the Aramean kingdoms to the north, Aram-naharaim in upper Mesopotamia and Aram-zobah north of Damascus, while in the south Joab struck down twelve thousand Edomites in the Valley of Salt. The second book of Samuel records these campaigns as victories, but the psalm suggests that along the way came a serious setback. It speaks of the land shaken and torn open, and of the people staggering as if drunk.

At the centre God speaks from his sanctuary, claiming the whole land as his own: Shechem and the valley of Succoth, Gilead and Manasseh, Ephraim as his helmet and Judah as his sceptre. Hostile neighbours become household objects. Moab is his washbasin, and on Edom he tosses his sandal. The psalm ends looking toward Edom's fortified city, and its second half reappears in Psalm one hundred and eight.

=== 61
From the ends of the earth I call to you, whenever my heart grows faint. Psalm sixty-one is a short prayer from someone who feels a long way off. The heading names David.

He asks to be led to the rock that is higher than he is, and to dwell in God's tent forever, under the shelter of his wings. He remembers that God has heard his vows and given him the inheritance of those who fear his name.

Then the prayer turns to the king: his long life, and his reign before God for generations, guarded by loving devotion and faithfulness. It closes with a promise to keep his vows day after day.

=== 62
Quiet trust fills Psalm sixty-two, whose heading again names Jeduthun. One small Hebrew word recurs throughout, translated alone or surely. In God alone my soul finds rest. He alone is my rock and my salvation.

Around that stillness there is real danger. Enemies are trying to topple David from his high position, as though pushing over a leaning wall or a tottering fence. With their mouths they bless; inwardly they curse.

The psalmist speaks first to his own soul, telling it to rest in God, then to the people, urging them to pour out their hearts before him. On the scales, human beings high and low weigh less than a breath, and riches are no foundation. A closing saying sounds like the voice of the wise: God has spoken once, twice I have heard it, that power belongs to God, and loving devotion too.

=== 63
A Psalm of David, when he was in the Wilderness of Judah: the dry, broken country that falls away from the hills of Judah to the Dead Sea. David knew it as a fugitive from Saul, and again when he fled Jerusalem during Absalom's revolt.

The setting shapes the language. In a dry and weary land without water, David's soul thirsts for God. He remembers seeing God's power and glory in the sanctuary, and lies awake through the watches of the night, thinking of him.

Few psalms of longing are warmer. God's loving devotion, he says, is better than life itself. Only in the final lines does he turn to his enemies, who will fall by the sword, before the psalm ends with the king's joy.

=== 64
The enemies in Psalm sixty-four are not soldiers but schemers. They sharpen their tongues like swords and aim bitter words like arrows, shooting at the innocent from cover. They plot in secret, congratulating themselves on a perfect plan that no one will see.

The turn comes as a striking reversal of that image. God will shoot his own arrows at them, suddenly, and their own tongues will bring them down. Those who see it will shake their heads, and all humanity will fear and ponder what God has done.

The psalm is brief, and it leaves the righteous rejoicing and taking refuge in the Lord.

=== 65
After a run of laments, Psalm sixty-five is pure thanksgiving. It begins in Zion, where praise awaits God and vows are being kept, where sins are atoned for and people are brought near to dwell in his courts.

Then the view widens. The God who answers prayer in his temple is also the hope of the farthest seas. He set the mountains in place and stilled the roaring waves and the tumult of the nations.

The last part is one of the loveliest pictures of the land of Israel in the Bible. God waters the earth, soaks its furrows and softens it with showers, and crowns the year with his bounty. The wilderness pastures overflow, the hills are robed with joy, the meadows are covered with flocks and the valleys with grain, until the whole landscape seems to shout and sing.

=== 66
Make a joyful noise to God, all the earth! Psalm sixty-six has no named author, and it begins as a hymn for every nation.

The reason given is Israel's history. Come and see what God has done, the psalm says, and it recalls the day he turned the sea into dry land and his people passed through the waters on foot. Then it speaks of a later ordeal, when God tested his people and refined them like silver, laid burdens on their backs and let men ride over their heads, and brought them through fire and water into abundance.

Halfway through, the voice changes from we to I. A single worshipper comes to the temple with burnt offerings, to keep the vows he made in his distress, and invites all who fear God to hear what God has done for him.

=== 67
Psalm sixty-seven opens with words every Israelite would know. May God be gracious to us and bless us, and make his face shine upon us. They echo the blessing the priests pronounced over Israel in the book of Numbers.

But here the blessing has a purpose that reaches beyond Israel: that God's ways may be known on earth and his salvation among all nations. A refrain rings out twice, let the peoples praise you, O God, let all the peoples praise you, and at the centre the nations are pictured singing for joy under his just rule.

The final lines mention a harvest gathered in. Israel's blessing spreads outward, until all the ends of the earth fear him.

=== 68
Psalm sixty-eight is one of the longest and most difficult psalms in the book. Its Hebrew is often obscure and its scenes change quickly, but its movement is clear. It is a great processional hymn, celebrating God's march from Sinai to Zion.

It opens with words very close to the cry Moses raised whenever the ark of the covenant set out in the wilderness: God arises, his enemies are scattered. God is praised as father of the fatherless and defender of widows, and the psalm remembers how he went before Israel through the desert, when the earth shook at Sinai and rain refreshed his weary people. Kings fled before them, and the women at home divided the spoils.

Then the scene moves to the mountains. The many-peaked mountains of Bashan, east of the Jordan, look on in envy, because God has chosen the hill of Zion for his dwelling. He ascends on high, leading captives in his train, a line Paul quotes in Ephesians of the ascended Christ.

Near the end the procession itself enters the sanctuary, singers first, then musicians, among girls playing tambourines, followed by the princes of the tribes, little Benjamin leading them. The kingdoms of the earth are summoned to sing to God.

=== 69
Save me, O God, for the waters are up to my neck. Psalm sixty-nine is a long lament that begins with the image of drowning. The psalmist is sinking in deep mud with no footing, hoarse from crying out.

His enemies hate him without cause, and he has become a stranger even to his own family. Much of what he suffers comes from his loyalty to God. Zeal for your house has consumed me, he says, and the insults aimed at God have fallen on him. When he fasted and wept, he became a joke at the city gate and a song for drunkards. When he looked for comfort, they gave him gall for food and vinegar for his thirst.

After Psalm twenty-two, this is among the psalms the New Testament quotes most. John's Gospel recalls the line about zeal for God's house when Jesus clears the temple, and the Gospels tell of sour wine offered to Jesus on the cross. The psalm also holds some of the Psalter's strongest prayers against enemies, before it ends in praise and the promise that God will rebuild the cities of Judah.

=== 70
Psalm seventy is a short, urgent prayer, and it has been heard before. It repeats, with small changes, the closing section of Psalm forty, where a song of thanksgiving gave way to a fresh cry for help. Here that cry stands alone. Like Psalm thirty-eight, its heading calls it a psalm to bring remembrance.

The same plea opens and closes it: hurry, O God, to help me, do not delay. Between those cries the psalmist asks that those who mock him with their aha, aha be turned back in shame, and that all who seek God rejoice. He calls himself simply poor and needy.

As with Psalm fifty-three, this version tends to say God where the earlier one used the Lord's personal name.

=== 71
Do not cast me off in my old age; do not forsake me when my strength fails. Psalm seventy-one has no heading, and it reads like a continuation of the urgent prayer before it, from someone who is now old.

Much of its language will sound familiar. The opening lines echo Psalm thirty-one, and its pleas for God to hurry recall Psalms forty and seventy. This is someone who has learned the psalms and made them his own.

What gives it its warmth is the sense of a whole lifetime. God has been his hope since his youth, and he has leaned on him since birth. Enemies now say God has abandoned him, but he looks back on many troubles and believes God will revive him again. His great desire is to live long enough to tell the next generation of God's power, and he takes up harp and lyre to praise the Holy One of Israel.

=== 72
Book Two closes with Psalm seventy-two, whose heading names Solomon. It is a prayer for the king, the son of a king, describing the reign Israel longed for.

Above all, the king is to be just. He is to defend the afflicted, save the children of the needy and crush the oppressor, because their blood is precious in his sight. His reign is pictured as rain on mown grass, bringing peace and plenty.

The prayer reaches far beyond Israel's borders. It asks that he rule from sea to sea and from the Euphrates to the ends of the earth, that the kings of Tarshish, Sheba and Seba bring him gifts, and that all nations be blessed through him, an echo of God's promise to Abraham.

Then comes a doxology, followed by a short editorial note: thus conclude the prayers of David son of Jesse. More psalms of David follow later, so this marks the end of an earlier collection.

=== 73
With Psalm seventy-three, Book Three of the Psalter begins. It opens a group of eleven psalms bearing the name of Asaph, and the tone of this whole book is darker and more searching than what has gone before.

This is a wisdom psalm that tells the story of a faith nearly lost. It begins with the conclusion: surely God is good to Israel. Then the singer confesses that his feet had almost slipped. He had envied the arrogant. They seemed healthy, comfortable and untroubled, mocking God and growing rich, while he kept his heart pure and was afflicted every morning. It all seemed in vain.

The turning point comes when he enters God's sanctuary and sees where the path of the wicked ends. What follows is one of the most beautiful confessions in the book: whom have I in heaven but you, and on earth I desire no one besides you.

=== 74
An unthinkable disaster lies behind Psalm seventy-four, a lament for the whole community. The enemy has broken into God's sanctuary, hacked at its carved panels with axes and hatchets, and burned it to the ground. Every place where God met his people in the land has been destroyed. There are no signs, no prophet, and no one knows how long it will last.

The enemy is not named, but the scene matches what the book of Kings records of the Babylonians, who burned Jerusalem and Solomon's temple early in the sixth century before Christ. The heading, a Maskil of Asaph, carries the name of the guild of singers descended from him, still serving centuries after David.

Amid the ruins the singer remembers who God is: his King from ancient times, who divided the sea, crushed the heads of Leviathan, the monster of the deep, and set the boundaries of the earth. The psalm closes with an urgent plea for God to rise and defend his cause.

=== 75
Psalm seventy-four pleaded with God to rise up. Psalm seventy-five, another psalm of Asaph set to the tune Do Not Destroy, opens with the congregation giving thanks, because his name is near.

Then God himself speaks. He will choose the appointed time and judge with fairness. When the earth and all its people quake, he is the one who holds its pillars steady. He warns the proud not to boast and the wicked not to lift their horns, the horn being an ancient image of strength.

Exaltation comes from neither east nor west nor the desert, but from God, who brings one down and lifts another up. In the Lord's hand is a cup of foaming wine, an image the prophets would use for his judgement, and the wicked must drain it to the dregs. God has the last word: the horns of the wicked will be cut off, and those of the righteous lifted high.
