=== intro
Until now the Bible has mostly told a story. The book of Psalms does something different. It gathers up the voices of the people inside that story and lets us hear them speaking to God. Here are a hundred and fifty poems, the songs and prayers of ancient Israel, written over many centuries and collected into a single book. Its Hebrew title means praises. Our word psalm comes through Greek, from a word for a song sung to the plucking of strings.

The poems span a remarkable stretch of history. One is credited to Moses, which takes us back to the wilderness. Many belong to the age of David and the kingdom he founded, about a thousand years before Christ. Others mourn the destruction of Jerusalem by the Babylonians, and at least one remembers the exiles weeping beside the rivers of Babylon. So the collection grew slowly, through the years of the kings, the exile and the return, and it seems to have reached its final shape after the temple was rebuilt. It became the songbook of the second temple, and later of the synagogue and the church.

Most psalms open with a short heading, which the Bible we are reading counts as part of the first verse. These superscriptions are very old. Some name a writer. Some give musical directions: for the choirmaster, with stringed instruments, to a tune with a name such as The Doe of the Dawn. Some use technical words whose meaning was lost long ago, such as Maskil, Miktam and Shiggaion. The word Selah appears often within the poems, probably marking a pause or an interlude, though no one now knows exactly how it was used. And a handful of headings, all about David, link a psalm to a moment in his life: running from Saul, hiding in a cave, confronted by the prophet Nathan, fleeing his own son Absalom. Most psalms carry no such note, and where Scripture is silent about an occasion, it is wise not to invent one.

David's name stands over more psalms than any other, about half the collection. He was remembered as Israel's singer: the shepherd boy who played the lyre for King Saul, and the king who brought the ark to Jerusalem with music and dancing and appointed Levites to sing before God. The Hebrew phrase of David can mean by David, but also for him or belonging to his collection, so readers weigh it with care. Asaph was one of the chief musicians David appointed, and the psalms bearing his name probably include the work of singers who followed him. The sons of Korah were a family of Levites, descended from the Korah who rebelled against Moses, and later served as gatekeepers and singers at the temple. Two psalms are linked with Solomon, one with Moses, one with Heman and one with Ethan, both called Ezrahites. Many psalms are anonymous.

The psalms come in recognisable kinds. The most common is the lament, a cry for help from someone sick, slandered, hunted or abandoned, or from the whole nation in defeat. Laments complain honestly, plead with God, and very often turn, sometimes abruptly, to trust and praise. There are hymns praising God as creator and king, and songs of thanksgiving for a rescue already received. Royal psalms concern Israel's king: his coronation, his wedding, his battles, and God's promise to the house of David. Wisdom psalms reflect on the two ways of life and the puzzle of why the wicked prosper. Near the end of the book come fifteen short songs of ascents, probably sung by pilgrims going up to Jerusalem for the great festivals.

All of this is poetry, and Hebrew poetry has its own music. It does not rhyme. Instead it works by parallelism: one line makes a statement, and the next echoes it, deepens it, or sets it against its opposite. The Lord is my shepherd; I shall not want. Because the rhythm lies in the thought rather than the sound, it survives translation remarkably well. A few psalms are built as acrostics, each line or stanza beginning with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet. One small thing helps the listener too. Where English Bibles print the LORD, the Hebrew has God's personal name, the name he revealed to Moses at the burning bush.

The collection is arranged in five books, perhaps echoing the five books of Moses. Each of the first four closes with a short blessing of God, and the last psalm is one great closing burst of praise. Psalms one and two stand at the entrance as a gateway: one about the person who delights in God's law, the other about the king God has set on Zion. Book One is almost entirely David's, and full of laments. Book Two opens with the sons of Korah and returns to David. Book Three, darker in tone, belongs largely to Asaph and ends with a grieving question about what has become of God's promise to David's line. Book Four answers with a prayer of Moses and a series of psalms declaring that the Lord reigns. Book Five gathers songs of pilgrimage and thanksgiving, and the whole book ends in a cascade of hallelujahs.

Israel sang these psalms in the temple courts, with lyres and harps, cymbals and trumpets, at the festivals and the daily sacrifices, and prayed them at home and in exile far from Jerusalem. Jesus knew them by heart, quoted them often, and prayed them from the cross. They have been the prayer book of God's people ever since, and each one is meant to be heard whole, in its own voice.

=== 1
The Psalter opens not with a prayer but with a portrait. Psalm one has no heading and no named author. It is a wisdom psalm, describing two kinds of people and the two roads they travel.

The first is the blessed man, a word that means something like truly happy, or well placed in life. He is defined by what he refuses and by what he loves: the law of the Lord, which he turns over in his mind day and night. The word is Torah, meaning instruction, the whole of God's teaching, not merely a list of rules. Such a person is like a tree beside streams of water, rooted, green and fruitful.

The wicked, by contrast, are chaff, the husks blown away when grain is tossed into the wind on the threshing floor. Placed at the front of the book, this short psalm quietly invites every reader to come to the psalms that follow as the first kind of person.

=== 2
The second psalm stands beside the first as the other half of the Psalter's gateway. Psalm one began with the word blessed; Psalm two ends with it. But where the first looked at a single life, this one looks at the nations.

It is a royal psalm, set at a coronation in Jerusalem. In the ancient world a new king's accession was a dangerous moment, when subject peoples might try to break free. Here the kings of the earth plot against the Lord and his anointed one, in Hebrew his messiah. The psalm has no heading, though in the book of Acts the early believers speak of it as David's.

Listen for the changing voices: the rebellious rulers, heaven's laughter, God declaring that he has installed his king on Zion, the king announcing God's decree, you are my son, and finally a warning to the rulers to submit. The New Testament returns to this psalm again and again when it speaks of Jesus as God's Son.

=== 3
Here, for the first time, a psalm carries a heading, and it takes us into one of the darkest chapters of David's life: when he fled from his son Absalom.

The second book of Samuel tells the story. Absalom spent years winning the hearts of the people, then had himself proclaimed king at Hebron and marched on Jerusalem. David left the city, climbing the Mount of Olives barefoot and weeping, and crossed the Jordan. On the way a man of Saul's family pelted him with stones and cursed him, as though God had finally abandoned him.

That is the taunt this psalm repeats: God will not deliver him. Against it David sets God as a shield around him, the one who lifts his head. Then comes a remarkably simple line. He lies down, sleeps, and wakes again, sustained by the Lord. Listen too for the word Selah, which appears here for the first time.

=== 4
Psalm four is a prayer for the end of the day, closing with David lying down to sleep in peace, and it has long been paired with the morning psalm before it. Its heading brings the first of many musical directions: for the choirmaster, with stringed instruments.

No occasion is named. David appeals to God, who has relieved his distress before, and then turns, unusually, to speak to the people around him, men who drag his honour through the mud and chase after lies. He tells them to search their hearts on their beds and be still.

The scene widens. Many are asking who will show them any good, and David answers with a prayer that echoes the old priestly blessing, asking God to let the light of his face shine. The joy God has given him, he says, is greater than the joy of a harvest when grain and new wine abound.

=== 5
Psalm four ended at bedtime; Psalm five begins at daybreak. David lays out his plea before God and waits to see what God will do. The heading asks for flutes.

This is a lament, though a composed one, setting two kinds of people side by side before God. On one side stand the boastful, the liars and the violent, whose throats are open graves. On the other stands the psalmist, who can enter God's house only because of God's own loving devotion.

That phrase will be heard again and again in the psalms, so it deserves a word. The Hebrew is hesed, the loyal, committed love God has bound himself to show his people. The Berean Standard Bible calls it loving devotion; others say steadfast love or mercy.

=== 6
Psalm six is the prayer of a man at the end of his strength. He is gravely ill, it seems, and fears that his suffering is God's discipline. His bones ache, and night after night he floods his bed with tears. Church tradition counted it the first of seven penitential psalms.

The heading's Sheminith may mean the eighth, perhaps an instrument or a musical setting; no one is sure. Listen for the cry how long, O Lord, how long, which will return often in the psalms, and for the reason the psalmist gives God for saving him: in Sheol, the shadowy realm of the dead, no one gives him praise.

Then, without warning, the mood changes. He orders his enemies away, because the Lord has heard his weeping. He says it three times in quick succession, and the prayer that began in tears ends in confidence.

=== 7
The heading of Psalm seven calls it a Shiggaion, a word whose meaning is lost, though some think it describes an impassioned, irregular song. It names a cause, the words of Cush, a Benjamite. Who Cush was, Scripture never says. We know only that David had bitter enemies in Saul's tribe of Benjamin, and that this man had evidently been accusing him.

The psalm is the plea of someone falsely charged, hunted as if by a lion. David swears an oath of innocence, calling disaster down on himself if he has really done the wrong he is accused of.

Then the scene becomes a courtroom. God is summoned to take his seat above the assembled nations, the righteous judge who searches hearts and minds. By the close, the wicked man has dug a pit and fallen into it himself, and David gives thanks to the Lord Most High.

=== 8
After seven psalms of struggle comes the first pure hymn of praise. Psalm eight celebrates God's glory in creation, and the surprising place he has given human beings within it. The heading mentions the Gittith, perhaps an instrument or tune linked with the Philistine city of Gath.

The same exclamation opens and closes the psalm: how majestic is your name in all the earth. Between them the poet stands under the night sky. He looks at the moon and stars, the work of God's fingers, and the vastness of it makes him ask what man is, that God should care for him.

The answer echoes the first chapter of Genesis. God has crowned human beings with glory and honour and set them over his works, the flocks and herds, the wild animals, the birds and the fish. The letter to the Hebrews later reads these lines in the light of Jesus.

=== 9
Psalm nine is a song of thanks, and it belongs closely with Psalm ten. Hidden in the Hebrew is an acrostic, its stanzas beginning with successive letters of the alphabet, which starts here and runs on, somewhat broken, into the next psalm. The ancient Greek translation treated the two as one.

The heading names a tune, The Death of the Son, its meaning now unknown. God is praised as a righteous judge on his throne, who has overthrown hostile nations and remembers the cry of the afflicted, and Zion appears as the place where he dwells.

Yet the mood is not simply triumphant. Midway through, the singer asks for mercy, still pressed by enemies, and the psalm closes not with a victory song but with a prayer that the nations may learn they are only men. Near the end, Higgaion and Selah appear together, perhaps calling for a pause to reflect.

=== 10
Psalm ten has no heading of its own, one reason many readers take it as the second half of Psalm nine. But the confidence of that psalm gives way here to a question sufferers have asked in every age: why, O Lord, do you stand far off?

Much of the psalm is a portrait of the wicked man. He is proud, greedy and secure, sure that God never notices. Then the picture turns predatory. He lurks near the villages like a lion in a thicket, waiting to drag off the helpless in his net.

Against that portrait the psalmist calls on God to rise and remember the fatherless and the oppressed. By the end the opening question has its answer, not as an explanation but as a declaration: the Lord is King forever and ever, and he hears the desire of the humble.

=== 11
Psalm eleven is a short song of trust, and it begins with a scene. Someone is urging David to run: flee like a bird to your mountain. The wicked have their arrows on the string, they say, and if the foundations are crumbling, what can the righteous do?

David does not dispute the danger. Instead he lifts his eyes. The Lord is in his holy temple, the Lord is on his heavenly throne, and his eyes are examining everyone, the righteous and the wicked alike. The fire and sulphur that will rain on the wicked recall the judgement on Sodom in Genesis.

In a few lines the psalm captures the choice between panic and refuge, and it closes with one of the most hopeful promises in these early psalms: the upright will see his face.

=== 12
Psalm twelve laments a whole society. The psalmist looks around and the faithful seem to have vanished. What is left is a world of talk: flattering lips, double hearts, and people who boast that their tongues will carry them, since no one can master them.

Then, at the centre, God himself speaks. Because the poor are plundered and the needy groan, he says, he will now arise. Words of God set within a lament appear several times in the psalms, and they may reflect a moment in worship when a divine answer was declared.

The psalmist responds that the Lord's words, unlike the liars', are flawless, like silver refined seven times. Yet nothing is neatly resolved. The last line looks again at the world as it still is, where the wicked strut about and vileness is prized.

=== 13
Psalm thirteen is one of the shortest laments in the book, and one of the clearest. In six verses it travels the whole path so many psalms take, from complaint, to petition, to trust.

It opens with four questions, each beginning how long: how long will God forget, how long will he hide his face, how long must the psalmist wrestle with sorrow, how long will his enemy prevail. No occasion is named, and the psalm is left open for any troubled person to take up.

Then comes a plea: look on me, answer me, give light to my eyes before I sleep in death. And finally the turn, marked by a single word, but. Nothing outward has changed yet. What has changed is where the psalmist rests his weight, on God's loving devotion, and the last line is a song.

=== 14
The fool says in his heart, there is no God. The Hebrew word for fool here does not mean someone lacking intelligence. It describes a person who is morally senseless, who lives as though God does not matter and will never call anyone to account.

The Lord looks down from heaven, searching for anyone who understands and seeks him, and finds no one who does good. The apostle Paul quotes these lines in his letter to the Romans. Yet the psalm also speaks of evildoers devouring God's people like bread, and of God standing with the righteous.

This poem appears again, almost word for word, as Psalm fifty-three. Here it closes with a longing that the salvation of Israel would come out of Zion, and that the Lord would restore his captive people, so that Jacob might rejoice and Israel be glad.

=== 15
Who may abide in your tent? Who may dwell on your holy mountain? Psalm fifteen begins with a question asked at the threshold of God's house. The tent recalls the tabernacle of the wilderness years, and the tent David pitched on Zion for the ark of the covenant.

It is often called an entrance psalm, perhaps used as worshippers approached the sanctuary, with one voice asking and another answering. The answer says nothing about sacrifice or ritual. It describes character: someone who walks with integrity, speaks truth from the heart, does not slander his neighbour, keeps his word even when it costs him, lends without interest and refuses a bribe.

Such a person, the psalm says, will never be shaken. Psalm twenty-four will ask almost the same question in a grander setting.

=== 16
Psalm sixteen is called a Miktam, a term that may mean a golden poem or an inscription; no one is sure. It is a song of trust, and its central image comes from the land of Israel itself.

When the tribes entered Canaan under Joshua, the land was divided by lot and marked out with boundary lines. The tribe of Levi received no land; the Lord himself was their inheritance. David takes up that language. The Lord is his portion and his cup, his boundary lines have fallen in pleasant places, and his inheritance is delightful. Alongside that joy he refuses any part in the worship of other gods.

The last lines reach beyond this life. God will not abandon him to Sheol, or let his holy one see decay. On the day of Pentecost, the apostle Peter quoted these words in Jerusalem and applied them to the resurrection of Jesus.

=== 17
Headed simply a prayer of David, Psalm seventeen is the plea of an innocent man under attack. He asks God to hear a just case, offered from lips without deceit. God has tested his heart, even at night, and found no evil.

His claim is not to be sinless, but to be in the right in this conflict. His enemies have tracked him down and surrounded him, eyes fixed on throwing him to the ground, like a young lion crouching in ambush.

At the centre come two of the tenderest images in the book. He asks to be kept as the apple of God's eye, the pupil that anyone instinctively protects, and hidden in the shadow of his wings, like a bird sheltering its young. The prayer closes at waking, with the singer satisfied in the presence of God.

=== 18
Psalm eighteen is the longest psalm in Book One, and one of the few whose heading tells a story. David sang it on the day the Lord delivered him from all his enemies and from the hand of Saul. The same song appears, with small differences, near the end of the second book of Samuel, where it looks back across his whole life: the years as a fugitive, the wars that followed, his rise to the throne.

It is a royal thanksgiving in great movements. First comes the cry for help from a man caught in the cords of death. Then God answers, and his coming is overwhelming: earthquake, smoke and fire, darkness, thunder, hail and lightning, God riding a cherub on the wings of the wind. It recalls the Lord's descent on Mount Sinai.

After the rescue, David reflects on God's faithfulness to the faithful. Then the song becomes a warrior's hymn, as God trains his hands for battle, sets his feet on the heights and makes him head of nations. The last line names David and promises loving devotion to his descendants forever.

=== 19
Psalm nineteen moves through two great subjects and then turns inward. It begins with the sky. The heavens declare the glory of God, without words or voice, yet their message reaches the whole earth. The sun is a bridegroom leaving his chamber, a champion eager to run his course. In this first part the poet uses the general Hebrew word for God.

Then he begins speaking of the law, and the name changes. Now it is the LORD, God's personal covenant name, repeated again and again. His law, testimony, precepts and commandments are praised in balanced lines, more precious than gold and sweeter than honey. The God who speaks silently through creation has spoken plainly to Israel.

Finally the psalmist looks at himself, asking to be cleansed from hidden faults and kept from wilful sin. His closing prayer, that the words of his mouth and the meditation of his heart may please God, has been prayed by countless readers since.

=== 20
Psalms twenty and twenty-one belong together. Both are royal psalms, and they read like two moments in one event: a prayer before battle, and thanks after it.

Here the king is about to march out to war, and the people pray for him. May the Lord answer him in the day of trouble, send help from the sanctuary in Zion, remember his offerings and grant the desire of his heart. Kings offered sacrifices before a campaign, and the psalm seems to have been sung in the temple courts as he did so.

Halfway through, a single voice speaks, perhaps a priest or the king himself: now I know that the Lord saves his anointed. Then the famous contrast. Some trust in chariots and some in horses, the great weapons of the age, but Israel trusts in the name of the Lord. The last cry belongs to the whole congregation: O Lord, save the king.

=== 21
Psalm twenty asked God to grant the king his heart's desire. Psalm twenty-one announces that he has. The prayer before battle has become thanksgiving after it, and again it is the gathered people speaking to God about their king.

They celebrate what God has given him: rich blessings, a crown of pure gold, long life, splendour and joy in God's presence. At the centre stands the reason for it all. The king trusts in the Lord, and through the loving devotion of the Most High he will not be shaken.

Then the address turns, either to the king or to God himself, and the tone grows fierce, as enemies are seized, consumed as if in a furnace and put to flight. In the final line the congregation lifts its praise back to God, for his strength.

=== 22
My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? Psalm twenty-two opens with the most desolate words in the collection. The heading names a tune, The Doe of the Dawn, and attributes the psalm to David, but gives no occasion.

It is a lament, and an extreme one. Onlookers mock the sufferer, sneering that he trusted in the Lord, so let the Lord rescue him. Enemies surround him like the strong bulls of Bashan, the rich cattle country east of the Jordan, then like roaring lions, then like a pack of dogs. His strength is poured out like water, his bones are on display, and those around him divide his garments and cast lots for his clothing.

The Gospels record that Jesus cried out the opening words of this psalm from the cross, and the evangelists saw in it the mockery of the crowd and the soldiers gambling for his clothes. But the psalm does not end where it begins. Two-thirds of the way through it turns sharply, with God's answer, and the cry becomes praise, spreading from the singer's brothers to all Israel, to the ends of the earth, and to a people yet unborn.

=== 23
After the depths of Psalm twenty-two comes the most familiar psalm of all. It is a song of trust attributed to David, who spent his youth as a shepherd in the hills around Bethlehem, guarding his father's flocks from lions and bears.

In the ancient Near East, kings were often called the shepherds of their people. Here the king himself becomes the sheep. The Lord leads him to green pastures and quiet waters, restores him, and guides him along right paths. Even the valley of the shadow of death holds no terror, because the shepherd is with him, with rod and staff, the club that drives off predators and the crook that guides.

Midway through, the picture changes from open country to a banquet. God becomes the host who prepares a table, anoints his guest's head with oil and fills his cup to overflowing, while enemies look on. At the last, the guest is at home in the house of the Lord.

=== 24
Psalm twenty-four is a song for a procession. It opens by declaring that the whole earth belongs to the Lord, who founded it upon the seas. Then, like Psalm fifteen, it asks who may climb the hill of the Lord, and answers: the one with clean hands and a pure heart.

In the final section the worshippers seem to stand at the city gates. One voice calls on the ancient doors to lift up their heads, so that the King of Glory may come in. Another asks, who is this King of Glory? The exchange is repeated, more grandly the second time, until the answer rings out: the Lord of Hosts, commander of the armies of heaven.

No occasion is given. Many readers have heard an echo of the day David brought the ark up to Jerusalem, and the psalm may well have been sung at festival processions. But that is an inference, not something the text states.

=== 25
Psalm twenty-five is a prayer for guidance and forgiveness, and it is carefully built. It is an acrostic, each verse in the Hebrew beginning with the next letter of the alphabet, a pattern of order and completeness that cannot survive translation.

The psalmist is lonely and afflicted, pressed by enemies, but his deepest concern is God's ways. Again and again he asks to be shown, taught and led. He asks God not to remember the sins of his youth, but to remember him according to his loving devotion, for the Lord is good and upright, and therefore shows sinners the way.

Guidance, confession and pleas for rescue weave in and out of one another, as they so often do in life. When the alphabet is complete, one more line is added, widening the prayer from one person to the nation: redeem Israel, O God, from all its distress.

=== 26
Vindicate me, O Lord, for I have walked with integrity. Like Psalms seven and seventeen, Psalm twenty-six is not a claim to sinlessness. It is the appeal of someone who fears being swept away with the wicked, and asks God to examine his heart and see the difference.

He names the company he refuses to keep: the deceitful, the hypocrites, the mob of evildoers, men whose right hands are full of bribes. And he names the place he loves, the house where God dwells, where his glory resides.

At the centre is a picture of worship. He washes his hands in innocence, a sign of being clean of guilt, and goes around God's altar, singing aloud of his wonderful works. By the last line he stands on level ground, blessing the Lord in the congregation.

=== 27
The Lord is my light and my salvation, whom shall I fear? Psalm twenty-seven begins with ringing confidence. Even if an army camps around him and war breaks out, David says, his heart will not give way.

Beneath that confidence lies a single longing. One thing he asks above all: to dwell in the house of the Lord, to gaze on his beauty and seek him in his temple. There God will hide him in his shelter and set him high upon a rock.

Halfway through, the tone changes so sharply that some have wondered whether two psalms were joined. Now he is pleading: hear me, do not hide your face, do not turn me away. False witnesses are rising against him, breathing violence. Yet the trust of the opening holds. He is sure he will see the Lord's goodness in the land of the living, and he ends by urging himself, or his hearers, to wait for the Lord.

=== 28
Psalm twenty-eight travels the lament's familiar road, from distress to thanksgiving, in a short space. It opens with a fear of silence. If God does not answer, David says, he will be like those going down to the Pit, the grave.

He prays with his hands raised toward God's holy sanctuary, the inner room where the ark of the covenant rested. His fear is of being dragged away with the wicked, who speak peace to their neighbours with malice in their hearts.

Then, with no explanation, prayer becomes praise. Blessed be the Lord, for he has heard. As often in David's psalms, the final lines widen from the king's own need to the people he leads. The Lord is the strength of his people and a stronghold for his anointed, and David asks him to shepherd Israel and carry them forever.

=== 29
Psalm twenty-nine is a hymn in the shape of a thunderstorm. It opens in heaven, calling the heavenly beings to give the Lord the glory due his name, and then brings the storm down to earth.

Seven times we hear the voice of the Lord, like peals of thunder. The storm comes in over the many waters and sweeps across the land. It shatters the cedars of Lebanon, makes the mountains skip like calves, Sirion among them, an old name for Mount Hermon, and shakes the wilderness of Kadesh far to the south.

Israel's Canaanite neighbours credited the storm to their god Baal, and the psalm may deliberately claim that ground for the Lord. Meanwhile in his temple all cry, glory. The storm passes into calm, with the Lord enthroned over the flood as king forever, blessing his people with peace.

=== 30
Psalm thirty is a song of thanksgiving from someone brought back from the edge of death. Its heading is puzzling: a song for the dedication of the temple, of David. The temple was built by Solomon after David died, so readers have suggested that the Hebrew word for house means David's palace, or that David wrote it looking ahead to the temple he prepared for, or that it was sung at a later dedication. The heading does not explain.

The psalm itself is intensely personal. Grown secure in prosperity, the singer told himself he would never be shaken. Then God hid his face, and sickness brought him near the Pit. He remembers his prayer: can the dust praise you?

God heard and healed him, and the psalm is full of reversals: weeping that lasts for a night and joy in the morning, mourning turned into dancing, sackcloth stripped away and replaced with gladness.

=== 31
Psalm thirty-one is a long prayer from someone in grave danger who keeps returning to God as his rock and refuge. Its best-known line comes early: into your hands I commit my spirit. According to Luke's Gospel, Jesus spoke these words as he died.

The psalm swings more than once between distress and confidence. The singer is wasting away with grief, a disgrace to his neighbours, avoided by friends in the street, forgotten like a dead man, a broken pot. He hears whispering all around, and a phrase the prophet Jeremiah would later use many times: terror on every side. Against all this he sets a simple affirmation. My times are in your hands.

Near the end he gives thanks for God's loving devotion shown to him in a city under siege, then turns outward, urging all God's people to be strong and take heart.

=== 32
Psalm thirty-two is called a Maskil, a word that may mean a skilful song or a song of instruction. Both fit. It celebrates the joy of being forgiven, and church tradition counted it the second of the penitential psalms. Paul quotes its opening in Romans as David's description of that blessing.

It begins with two beatitudes for the one whose sins are covered. Then the psalmist tells his story. While he kept silent about his sin, he wasted away under God's heavy hand, his strength dried up as in summer heat. When at last he confessed, God forgave. The sin is never named.

In the second half a new voice seems to speak, probably God himself, promising to instruct and guide, and warning against being like a stubborn horse or mule that must be steered with bit and bridle. Then comes a call to the righteous to rejoice.

=== 33
Psalm thirty-two closed with a call to the righteous to shout for joy. Psalm thirty-three, which has no heading, takes up that call and turns it into a full hymn of praise.

It begins with music: harp and ten-stringed lyre, a new song played skilfully with shouts of joy. The reasons for praise are on the largest scale. The Lord made the heavens by his word and the stars by the breath of his mouth. He gathers the waters of the sea and stores up the deep. He spoke, and it came to be.

From creation the psalm moves to history. The plans of the nations come to nothing, but the Lord's purposes stand forever. No king is saved by the size of his army, and a warhorse is a vain hope. The congregation waits quietly for the Lord, their help and shield, asking that his loving devotion rest on them.

=== 34
The heading of Psalm thirty-four takes us back to David's years on the run from Saul: when he pretended to be insane before Abimelech. The first book of Samuel tells the story. David fled into Philistine territory, to Gath, the home town of Goliath. There he was recognised, and in fear for his life he acted the madman, scrawling on the gate and letting saliva run down his beard, until the king sent him away. In Samuel the king is called Achish. Abimelech seems to have been a royal title among the Philistines, much as Pharaoh was in Egypt.

The psalm is a thanksgiving, and like Psalm twenty-five an acrostic. David invites others to magnify the Lord with him, because this poor man called and the Lord heard. Then he becomes a teacher, calling his hearers children. Listen for the invitation taste and see that the Lord is good, and the promise that the Lord is near to the brokenhearted.

=== 35
Psalm thirty-five is the prayer of a man attacked by people he once treated as friends. He asks the Lord to fight for him like a warrior, with shield, spear and javelin. Then the scene becomes a law court, where hostile witnesses accuse him of things he knows nothing about.

What wounds him most is their ingratitude. When they were sick, he wore sackcloth and fasted for them, grieving as if for a brother or his own mother. Now that he has stumbled, they gather in glee, mocking him like jesters at a feast and crying, aha, aha.

The psalm moves through three rounds of complaint and prayer, each ending with a promise of praise. Its pleas for his enemies' shame are hard to hear, but they come from someone who has handed the matter to God rather than taking revenge himself.

=== 36
Psalm thirty-six sets two portraits side by side. Its heading calls David the servant of the Lord, as Psalm eighteen did, and it opens with an oracle in his heart about the wicked.

The wicked man has no fear of God before his eyes. He is too pleased with himself to see his own sin, and he lies on his bed plotting evil.

Then, without transition, the psalmist lifts his eyes, and the language soars. God's loving devotion reaches to the heavens and his faithfulness to the clouds, his righteousness is like the highest mountains and his judgements like the great deep. People shelter in the shadow of his wings, feast in his house and drink from his river of delights. With you is the fountain of life, he says, and in your light we see light. A short prayer for protection follows, and a glimpse of evildoers fallen, unable to rise.

=== 37
Psalm thirty-seven is a wisdom psalm, and the longest of David's acrostics. It is less a prayer than a body of counsel, from someone who says he was once young and now is old.

Its subject troubled Israel's wise men: what should the faithful do when the wicked prosper? The answer begins with its opening words, do not fret. The success of the wicked is brief. They wither like grass and vanish like smoke, while those who wait for the Lord will inherit the land. That phrase recurs throughout, recalling God's promise to Israel's ancestors, and Jesus echoes it in the Sermon on the Mount: the meek will inherit the earth.

Because the psalm follows the alphabet, it moves less by argument than by return, circling its theme from many angles. Listen for its gentle commands: trust in the Lord, delight in him, commit your way to him, be still, and wait.
