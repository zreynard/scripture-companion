=== intro
Until now the Bible has mostly told a story. The book of Psalms does something different. It gathers up the voices of the people inside that story, and lets us hear them speaking to God. Here are a hundred and fifty poems, the songs and prayers of ancient Israel, written over many centuries and collected into a single book. Its Hebrew title means praises. Our word psalm comes through Greek, from a word for a song sung to the plucking of strings.

These poems span a remarkable stretch of history. One is credited to Moses, which would take us back to the wilderness. Many belong to the age of David and the kingdom he founded, about a thousand years before Christ. Others lament the destruction of Jerusalem by the Babylonians, and at least one is sung by exiles sitting beside the rivers of Babylon. So the collection grew slowly, through the years of the kings, the exile and the return, and it seems to have reached its final shape in the centuries after the temple was rebuilt. It became the songbook of the second temple, and later of the synagogue and the church.

Most psalms open with a short heading, which the Bible we are reading counts as part of the first verse. These superscriptions are very old, and they are worth listening to. Some name a writer. Some give musical directions: for the choirmaster, with stringed instruments, to a tune with a name such as The Doe of the Dawn. Some use technical words whose meaning was lost long ago, such as Maskil, Miktam and Shiggaion. The word Selah appears often within the poems themselves, probably marking a pause or a musical interlude, though no one now knows exactly how it was used. And a handful of headings, all of them about David, link a psalm to a particular moment in his life: running from Saul, hiding in a cave, confronted by the prophet Nathan, fleeing his own son Absalom. Most psalms carry no such note, and we should not invent one where Scripture is silent.

David's name stands over more psalms than any other, about half of the whole collection. He was remembered as Israel's singer: the shepherd boy who played the lyre for King Saul, and the king who brought the ark to Jerusalem with music and dancing and organised the Levites as singers for the house of God. The Hebrew phrase of David can mean by David, but also for him or belonging to his collection, so readers have weighed it carefully. Other names appear as well. Asaph was one of the chief musicians David appointed, and a group of psalms bears his name, probably including the work of the singers who followed him. The sons of Korah were a family of Levites, descended from the Korah who rebelled against Moses in the wilderness, and later found serving as gatekeepers and singers at the temple. Two psalms are linked with Solomon, one with Moses, one with Heman and one with Ethan, both described as Ezrahites. Many psalms are anonymous.

The psalms come in recognisable kinds. The most common is the lament, a cry for help from someone who is sick, slandered, hunted or abandoned, or from the whole nation in defeat. Laments complain honestly, plead with God, and very often turn, sometimes abruptly, to trust and praise. There are hymns that praise God as creator and king, and songs of thanksgiving for a rescue already received. Royal psalms concern Israel's king, his coronation, his wedding, his battles, and God's promise to the house of David. Wisdom psalms reflect on the two ways of life and the puzzle of why the wicked prosper. Near the end of the book comes a little collection of fifteen short songs called the songs of ascents, probably sung by pilgrims going up to Jerusalem for the great festivals.

All of this is poetry, and Hebrew poetry has its own music. It does not rhyme. Instead it works by parallelism: one line makes a statement, and the next echoes it, deepens it, or sets it against its opposite. The Lord is my shepherd; I shall not want. Because the rhythm lies in the thought rather than the sound, it survives translation remarkably well. A few psalms go further and are built as acrostics, each line or stanza beginning with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet. One small thing helps the listener too. Where English Bibles print the LORD, the Hebrew has God's personal name, the name he revealed to Moses at the burning bush.

The collection is arranged in five books, perhaps echoing the five books of Moses. Each of the first four closes with a short blessing of God, and the last psalm is itself one great closing burst of praise. Psalms one and two stand at the entrance as a gateway: one about the person who delights in God's law, the other about the king God has set on Zion. Book One is almost entirely David's, and full of laments. Book Two opens with the sons of Korah and returns to David. Book Three, darker in tone, is dominated by Asaph and ends with a grieving question about what has become of God's promise to David's line. Book Four answers with a prayer of Moses and a series of psalms declaring that the Lord reigns. Book Five gathers songs of pilgrimage and thanksgiving, and the whole book ends in a cascade of hallelujahs.

Israel sang these psalms in the temple courts, with lyres and harps, cymbals and trumpets, at the festivals and the daily sacrifices, and prayed them in homes and in exile far from Jerusalem. Jesus knew them by heart, quoted them often, and prayed one of them from the cross. They have been the prayer book of God's people ever since. As each one begins, the narrator will say what kind of psalm it is, what its heading tells us, and how it moves. Then the psalm will speak for itself.

=== 1
The Psalter opens not with a prayer but with a portrait. Psalm one has no heading and no named author. It is a wisdom psalm, and it describes two kinds of people and the two roads they travel.

The first is the blessed man, a word that means something like truly happy, or well placed in life. He is defined first by what he refuses, and then by what he loves: the law of the Lord, which he turns over in his mind day and night. The word translated law is Torah, which means instruction, the whole teaching God has given, not merely a list of rules. Such a person is pictured as a tree beside streams of water, rooted, green and fruitful.

The second picture is of the wicked, and it is as light as the first is solid: chaff, the husks blown away when grain is tossed into the wind on the threshing floor. The psalm is short, and its last line names the two ways and where each one leads. Placed at the front of the book, it quietly invites every reader to come to the psalms that follow as this kind of person.

=== 2
The second psalm stands beside the first as the other half of the Psalter's gateway. Psalm one began with the word blessed; Psalm two ends with it. But where the first psalm looked at a single life, this one looks at the nations of the world.

It is a royal psalm, and its setting is a coronation in Jerusalem. In the ancient world, the accession of a new king was a dangerous moment, when subject peoples might try to throw off their obligations. Here the kings of the earth are plotting against the Lord and against his anointed one. The Hebrew word for anointed is messiah. The psalm has no heading, though in the book of Acts the early believers speak of it as David's.

Listen for the changing voices. First the rebellious rulers speak, then heaven laughs, then God declares that he has installed his king on Zion, his holy mountain. Then the king himself announces the decree God has spoken to him, you are my son, and finally the poet turns to warn the rulers to submit while they can. The New Testament returns to this psalm again and again when it speaks of Jesus as God's anointed Son.

=== 3
Here, for the first time, a psalm carries a heading, and it takes us into one of the darkest chapters of David's life. A Psalm of David, when he fled from his son Absalom.

The story is told in the second book of Samuel. David's son Absalom spent years winning the hearts of the people, then had himself proclaimed king at Hebron and marched on Jerusalem. David left the city with his household and loyal men, climbing the Mount of Olives barefoot and weeping, and crossed the Jordan into the wilderness. On the way a man from Saul's family pelted him with stones and cursed him, as though God had finally abandoned him.

That is the taunt this psalm repeats: God will not deliver him. Against it David sets a different picture of God, as a shield around him and the one who lifts his head. In the middle comes a remarkably simple line. He lies down, he sleeps, and he wakes again, alive. This is often called a morning psalm. Notice too the word Selah, appearing here three times, perhaps marking a pause in the singing.

=== 4
Psalm four is a prayer for the end of the day. It closes with David lying down to sleep in peace, and for that reason it has long been paired with the morning psalm before it. The heading adds the first of many musical directions: for the choirmaster, with stringed instruments.

No occasion is named. The psalm opens with an urgent appeal to God, recalling that he has given relief in distress before. Then David turns, unusually, to speak to people around him, men who are dragging his honour through the mud and chasing after empty things. He tells them to be still, to search their hearts on their beds, and to trust the Lord.

In the second half the scene widens. Many are asking who will show them any good. David's answer is a prayer, an echo of the old priestly blessing that asks God to make his face shine. He ends by saying the joy God has given him is greater than the joy of a bumper harvest, when the grain and the new wine overflow.

=== 5
Psalm four ended at bedtime. Psalm five begins at daybreak, with David laying out his request before God like an offering and then watching to see what God will do. The heading asks for the accompaniment of flutes.

This is a lament, though a composed and steady one. Much of it contrasts two kinds of people standing before God. On one side are the boastful, the liars and the violent, whose throats are described as open graves. On the other is the psalmist, who knows he can enter God's house only because of God's own faithful love.

That phrase deserves a word of explanation, because it will be heard again and again in the psalms. The Hebrew word hesed is rich and hard to translate. The Berean Standard Bible renders it loving devotion; others say steadfast love or mercy. It means the loyal, committed love that God has bound himself to show his people. The psalm closes with a widening blessing over all who take refuge in him.

=== 6
Psalm six is the prayer of a man at the end of his strength. Something has brought him very low, apparently a serious illness, and he fears that it may be God's discipline. His bones ache, his soul is shaken, and night after night he soaks his bed with tears. In church tradition it became the first of seven penitential psalms, prayers of sorrow and repentance.

The heading includes the word Sheminith, which may mean the eighth, perhaps referring to an instrument or a musical setting; its meaning is uncertain. Listen for the cry, how long, O Lord, how long, which will return often in the psalms, and for the reason the psalmist gives God for saving him: in Sheol, the shadowy realm of the dead, no one remembers him or gives him praise.

Then, with no warning, the mood changes. He turns on his enemies and tells them to go, because the Lord has heard. Three times in two lines he says it. The prayer that began in tears ends in confidence.

=== 7
The heading of Psalm seven is unusual. It calls the poem a Shiggaion, a word whose meaning has been lost, though some think it describes an intense or irregular song. And it names a cause: the words of Cush, a Benjamite. Who Cush was, Scripture never says. We know only that David had bitter enemies among Saul's tribe of Benjamin, and that someone of that name had evidently been spreading accusations against him.

The psalm itself is a plea from someone falsely accused. David pictures himself hunted by a lion. Then he does something striking: he swears an oath of innocence, calling down disaster on himself if he has really done the wrong he is charged with.

From there the scene moves to a courtroom. God is called to rise up and take his seat as judge over the assembled nations, the one who tests hearts and minds. The poem ends with the image of a man who digs a pit for others and falls into it himself, and with thanks to the Lord Most High.

=== 8
After seven psalms of distress and struggle comes the first pure hymn of praise. Psalm eight is a song about God's glory in creation, and about the surprising place he has given to human beings within it. The heading mentions the Gittith, possibly an instrument or a tune linked with the Philistine city of Gath; no one knows for certain.

The psalm begins and ends with the same exclamation, how majestic is your name in all the earth, and everything in between is held inside it. The poet stands under the night sky, looking at the moon and the stars, which he calls the work of God's fingers, and the vastness of it all makes him ask what humanity can be, that God should care for it.

The answer echoes the first chapter of Genesis. God has crowned human beings with glory and honour and set them to rule over his works, the flocks and herds, the wild animals, the birds and the fish. The letter to the Hebrews will later return to these lines and read them in the light of Jesus.

=== 9
Psalm nine is a song of thanks, and it belongs closely with the psalm that follows it. The two are bound together by a pattern hidden in the Hebrew: an acrostic, its stanzas beginning with successive letters of the alphabet, which starts here and runs on, somewhat broken, into Psalm ten. The ancient Greek translation treated them as a single poem.

The heading names a tune, The Death of the Son, though what that title meant is unknown. The psalm praises God as a righteous judge on his throne, who has defeated hostile nations and remembers the cry of the afflicted. Zion, the hill of Jerusalem, appears as the place where the Lord dwells.

The mood is not simply triumphant. Midway through, the singer turns to ask for mercy, still pressed by enemies, and the psalm ends not with a victory celebration but with a prayer that the nations would be reminded they are only men. Near the end two musical terms appear together, Higgaion and Selah, perhaps calling for a pause for reflection.

=== 10
Psalm ten has no heading of its own, which is one reason many readers take it as the second half of Psalm nine. But its tone is very different. The confidence of the previous psalm gives way to a question that has been asked by sufferers in every age: why, O Lord, do you stand far off?

Much of the psalm is a portrait of the wicked man. He is proud, greedy and secure. He mocks God and assumes that God never notices. Then the picture turns predatory: he lurks near the villages like a lion in a thicket, waiting to drag off the helpless in his net.

Against that portrait the psalmist calls on God to rise and act, and to remember the fatherless and the oppressed. By the end the question of the opening line has received its answer, not in an explanation but in a declaration. The Lord is King forever and ever, and he hears the desire of the humble.

=== 11
Psalm eleven is a short song of trust, and it begins with a scene. Someone, perhaps well-meaning friends, is urging David to run: flee like a bird to the mountains. The wicked have their arrows on the string, they say, and if the very foundations are crumbling, what can a righteous person do?

David does not argue with their description of the danger. Instead he lifts his eyes. The Lord is in his holy temple, the Lord is on his throne in heaven, and his eyes are examining everyone, the righteous and the wicked alike. The image of fire and sulphur raining down recalls the judgement on Sodom in Genesis.

No occasion is given in the heading, and the psalm is brief, but it captures in a few lines the choice between panic and refuge. It ends with one of the most hopeful promises in the early psalms, that the upright will see God's face.

=== 12
Psalm twelve is a lament over a whole society. The psalmist looks around and feels that the faithful have disappeared. What is left is a world of talk: flattering lips, double hearts, and people who boast that their tongues will carry them to victory, because no one can master them.

Then, in the centre of the psalm, God himself speaks. Because the poor are being plundered and the needy groan, he says, he will now arise. Prophetic words like this, placed in the middle of a lament, appear several times in the psalms, and they may reflect a moment in worship when a word from God was declared.

After that, the psalmist answers. The words of the Lord, unlike the words of the liars, are flawless, like silver refined in a furnace seven times over. The poem does not end with everything resolved, though. Its last line returns to the world as it still is, where the wicked strut about and vileness is admired.

=== 13
Psalm thirteen is one of the shortest laments in the book, and one of the clearest. In six verses it travels the whole path that so many psalms take, from complaint, to petition, to trust.

It opens with four questions, each beginning how long. How long will God forget, how long will he hide his face, how long must the psalmist wrestle with sorrow, how long will his enemy have the upper hand. The heading names no occasion. David's life gave him reasons enough, but the psalm is left open, so that any troubled person can take up its words.

The second movement is a plea: look at me, answer me, give light to my eyes before I sleep in death. And then comes the turn, marked by a single word, but. Nothing outward has changed yet. What changes is where the psalmist rests his weight, on God's loving devotion. The psalm ends with a song.

=== 14
Psalm fourteen opens with a famous line: the fool says in his heart, there is no God. The Hebrew word for fool here does not mean someone lacking intelligence. It describes a person who is morally senseless, who lives as though God does not matter and will never call anyone to account.

The psalm then pictures the Lord looking down from heaven, searching for anyone who understands and seeks him, and finding no one who does good. The apostle Paul would later quote these lines in his letter to the Romans. But the psalm also speaks of God's people being devoured like bread by evildoers, and of God standing with the righteous.

This psalm appears a second time in the collection, almost word for word, as Psalm fifty-three, with one difference that will be noted there. It closes with a longing that salvation would come out of Zion, and that the Lord would restore the fortunes of his captive people, so that Jacob and Israel could rejoice.

=== 15
Psalm fifteen begins with a question asked at the threshold of God's house. Who may abide in your tent? Who may dwell on your holy mountain? The tent recalls the tabernacle of the wilderness years, and the tent David pitched in Jerusalem when he brought the ark of the covenant up to Zion.

This is often called an entrance psalm, and it may have been used as worshippers approached the sanctuary, with the question put by one voice and the answer given by another. The answer is surprising in its focus. It does not mention sacrifices or ritual purity. It describes a person's character: someone who walks with integrity, speaks the truth, does not slander a neighbour, keeps a promise even when it costs him, and refuses to profit from the poor or take a bribe.

The psalm ends with the assurance that such a person will never be shaken. Later in Book One, Psalm twenty-four will ask almost the same question in a grander setting.

=== 16
Psalm sixteen is called a Miktam, a term that may mean a golden poem or an inscription, though no one is sure. It is a song of trust, and its central image is drawn from the land of Israel itself.

When the tribes entered Canaan under Joshua, the land was divided by lot, and each family received its portion, marked out with boundary lines. The tribe of Levi received no land; the Lord himself was their inheritance. David takes up that language. The Lord is his portion and his cup, his boundary lines have fallen in pleasant places, and his inheritance is delightful. Alongside that joy he refuses any part in the worship of other gods.

The last lines move beyond this life. God will not abandon him to Sheol, or let his holy one see decay, and will show him the path of life. On the day of Pentecost, the apostle Peter quoted these words in Jerusalem, and argued that they found their fullest meaning in the resurrection of Jesus.

=== 17
Psalm seventeen is headed simply a prayer of David, and it is the plea of an innocent man under attack. He asks God to hear a just case, offered from lips without deceit. God has examined his heart, even in the night, and found nothing to condemn.

As with the earlier psalms of this kind, his claim is not to be sinless, but to be in the right in this particular conflict. His enemies have tracked him down and surrounded him, eyes fixed on bringing him to the ground, like a young lion crouching in ambush.

In the middle of the psalm come two of the tenderest images in the book. He asks God to keep him as the apple of his eye, the pupil that anyone instinctively protects, and to hide him in the shadow of his wings, like a bird sheltering its young. The psalm ends at dawn, with the singer waking to be satisfied with the presence of God.

=== 18
Psalm eighteen is one of the longest in Book One, and one of the few whose heading tells a story. It is a song David sang on the day the Lord delivered him from all his enemies and from the hand of Saul. The same poem appears, with small differences, near the end of the second book of Samuel, where it looks back across David's whole life: the years as a fugitive from Saul, the wars that followed, and his rise to the throne.

It is a royal song of thanksgiving, and it falls into great movements. First comes the cry for help, from someone caught in the cords of death. Then God answers, and the description of his coming is overwhelming: earthquake, smoke and fire, thick darkness, thunder, hail and lightning, God riding on a cherub on the wings of the wind. It recalls the Lord's descent on Mount Sinai, and it is the language of a storm rather than of a battlefield report.

After the rescue, David reflects on God's faithfulness to those who are faithful to him. Then the psalm becomes a warrior's song, as God trains his hands for battle, sets his feet on the heights, and makes him the head of nations. The final line names David and promises loving devotion to his descendants forever.

=== 19
Psalm nineteen moves through two great subjects, and then turns inward. It is one of the best-loved hymns in the collection.

It begins with the sky. The heavens declare the glory of God, without words or voice, and yet their message reaches the whole earth. The sun is pictured as a bridegroom leaving his chamber and a champion delighting to run his race, its heat reaching everything. In this first section the poet calls God by the general Hebrew word for God.

Then, abruptly, he begins speaking of the law, and the name changes. Now it is the LORD, God's personal covenant name, repeated again and again. The law of the Lord, his testimony, his precepts and his commandments are praised in six balanced lines as perfect, trustworthy, radiant and true, more precious than gold and sweeter than honey. The God who speaks silently through creation has spoken plainly to Israel.

Finally the psalmist looks at himself, asking to be cleansed from hidden faults and kept from deliberate sin. His closing prayer, that the words of his mouth and the meditation of his heart may please God, has been prayed by countless readers since.

=== 20
Psalms twenty and twenty-one belong together. Both are royal psalms, and they read like two moments in the same event: a prayer before a battle, and a song of thanks afterwards.

Psalm twenty is the first of the pair. The king is about to go out to war, and the people pray for him. They ask that God would answer him in the day of trouble, send him help from the sanctuary in Zion, remember his offerings, and give him the desire of his heart. In ancient Israel a king would offer sacrifices before a campaign, and the psalm seems to have been sung in the temple courts as he did so.

Halfway through, a single voice speaks, perhaps a priest or the king himself: now I know that the Lord saves his anointed. Then comes the famous contrast. Some trust in chariots and some in horses, the great weapons of the age, but Israel trusts in the name of the Lord. The psalm ends with the whole congregation praying, Lord, save the king.

=== 21
Psalm twenty asked God to grant the king his heart's desire. Psalm twenty-one opens by announcing that he has done so. The prayer before the battle has become thanksgiving after it, and again we hear the voice of the gathered people, speaking first to God about their king.

They celebrate what God has given him: rich blessings, a crown of pure gold, long life, splendour and majesty, and joy in God's presence. At the centre of the psalm is the reason for it all. The king trusts in the Lord, and through the loving devotion of the Most High he will not be shaken.

In the second half the address seems to turn to the king himself, or perhaps to God, and the tone becomes fierce, as his enemies are seized, consumed as if in a furnace and put to flight. The psalm closes with the congregation lifting their praise back to God, for his strength and power.

=== 22
Psalm twenty-two begins with the most desolate words in the whole collection. My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? The heading names a tune, The Doe of the Dawn, and the psalm is attributed to David, though no occasion is given.

It is a lament, and an extreme one. The psalmist feels utterly abandoned. He is mocked by onlookers who sneer that he trusted in the Lord, so let the Lord rescue him. He is surrounded by enemies pictured as the strong bulls of Bashan, the rich grazing country east of the Jordan, then as lions, and then as a pack of dogs. His body is wasting, his strength gone, his bones on display, and those around him are dividing his clothes and casting lots for them.

The Gospels record that Jesus cried out the opening words of this psalm from the cross, and the evangelists saw in it the soldiers gambling for his garments and the mockery of the crowd. The listener should know that the psalm does not end where it begins. Two-thirds of the way through it turns sharply. God has answered, and the cry becomes praise, spreading from the singer's brothers to all Israel, then to the ends of the earth, and finally to generations not yet born.

=== 23
After the depths of Psalm twenty-two comes the most familiar psalm of all. Psalm twenty-three is a song of trust, and it is attributed to David, who had spent his youth as a shepherd in the hills around Bethlehem, guarding his father's flocks from lions and bears.

In the ancient Near East, kings were often called the shepherds of their people. Here the king himself becomes the sheep. The Lord is his shepherd, leading him to grassy pastures and quiet waters, restoring him, and guiding him along right paths. Even the darkest valley holds no terror, because the shepherd is with him, with rod and staff, the club that fends off predators and the crook that guides.

Midway through, the picture changes from the open country to a banquet. God becomes the host who prepares a table, anoints his guest's head with oil and fills his cup to overflowing, while his enemies can only look on. The psalm ends with the guest at home in the house of the Lord.

=== 24
Psalm twenty-four is a song for a procession. It opens with a sweeping declaration that the whole earth belongs to the Lord, because he founded it upon the seas. Then, like Psalm fifteen, it asks who may go up to the hill of the Lord and stand in his holy place, and the answer is the one with clean hands and a pure heart.

In the final section the worshippers seem to have reached the gates of the city. A voice calls on the ancient doors to lift up their heads, so that the King of Glory may come in, and another voice asks, who is this King of Glory? The exchange is repeated, each time more grandly, until the answer rings out: the Lord of Hosts, the commander of the armies of heaven.

The heading gives no occasion. Many readers have heard in it an echo of the day David brought the ark of the covenant up to Jerusalem, and the psalm may well have been sung at festivals as the ark or the worshippers entered the city. But that is an inference, not a statement of the text.

=== 25
Psalm twenty-five is a prayer for guidance and forgiveness, and it is built with care. It is an acrostic: in the Hebrew, each verse begins with the next letter of the alphabet, a pattern that gives the poem a sense of order and completeness, though it is lost in translation.

No occasion is named. The psalmist is under pressure from enemies, lonely and afflicted, but his deepest concern is with God's ways. Again and again he asks to be shown, taught and led along God's paths. He also asks God not to remember the sins of his youth, but to remember him according to his loving devotion, because God is good and upright, and therefore shows sinners the way.

Prayer for guidance, confession of sin and pleas for rescue weave in and out of one another, the way they so often do in real life. At the very end, after the alphabet is complete, one more line is added, widening the prayer from one person to the whole nation: redeem Israel, O God, from all its distress.

=== 26
Psalm twenty-six is the prayer of someone who asks God to examine him and clear his name. Vindicate me, O Lord, he begins, for I have walked with integrity. Like Psalms seven and seventeen, this is not a claim to be sinless. It is an appeal from someone wrongly lumped together with the wicked, asking God to see the difference.

He describes the company he refuses to keep, the deceitful, the hypocrites and the mob of evildoers, men with bribes in their right hands. And he describes the place he loves: the house where God dwells, the place where his glory resides.

At the heart of the psalm is a picture of worship. He washes his hands in innocence, a sign of being clean of guilt, and goes around God's altar, singing aloud of his wonderful works. The psalm ends on level ground, with the singer standing in the congregation and blessing the Lord.

=== 27
Psalm twenty-seven begins with ringing confidence. The Lord is my light and my salvation, whom shall I fear? Even if an army camps around him and war breaks out, David says, his heart will not give way.

At the centre of that confidence lies a single longing. One thing he asks above all else: to dwell in the house of the Lord, to gaze on his beauty and to seek him in his temple. There God will hide him in his shelter, under the cover of his tent, and set him high upon a rock.

Then, halfway through, the tone changes so sharply that some readers have wondered whether two psalms were joined together. The singer is now pleading: hear me, do not hide your face, do not turn me away. False witnesses are rising against him, breathing violence. Yet the trust of the opening does not disappear. The psalm ends with him certain he will see the goodness of the Lord in the land of the living, and with a word of encouragement: wait for the Lord.

=== 28
Psalm twenty-eight follows the classic path of the lament from distress to thanksgiving, and it does so in a small space. It opens with a fear of silence. If God does not answer, David says, he will be like those going down to the Pit, the grave.

He prays with his hands raised toward God's holy sanctuary, the innermost room where the ark of the covenant stood. His concern is not to be swept away along with the wicked, those who speak of peace to their neighbours while hiding malice in their hearts, and he asks God to give them what their deeds deserve.

Then, without any explanation, the prayer becomes praise. Blessed be the Lord, for he has heard. As often in these psalms of David, the last lines widen out from the king's own need to the people he leads. The Lord is the strength of his people and the stronghold of his anointed. The final request asks God to be Israel's shepherd and to carry them forever.

=== 29
Psalm twenty-nine is a hymn in the form of a thunderstorm. It opens in heaven, calling on the heavenly beings to give the Lord the glory due to his name, and then it brings the storm down to earth.

Seven times we hear the phrase the voice of the Lord, like peals of thunder. The storm seems to come in off the Mediterranean, the many waters, and then to sweep across the land. It breaks the great cedars of Lebanon, makes the mountains leap like calves, among them Sirion, an old name for Mount Hermon, and shakes the wilderness of Kadesh far to the south. Lightning flashes, and oaks are twisted and stripped.

Israel's Canaanite neighbours credited the storm to their god Baal, and some of the psalm's imagery may deliberately claim that ground for the Lord. Meanwhile, in his temple, all cry glory. The psalm ends in calm, with the Lord enthroned over the flood as king forever, and with a quiet blessing of strength and peace for his people.

=== 30
Psalm thirty is a song of thanksgiving from someone who has been brought back from the edge of death. Its heading is unusual: a song for the dedication of the temple, of David. Since the temple was built by Solomon after David's death, readers have suggested various explanations: that the Hebrew word for house refers to David's own palace, that David wrote it looking ahead to the temple he had prepared for, or that it was later sung at a dedication. The heading itself does not say.

The psalm is intensely personal. The singer had grown secure in prosperity and told himself he would never be shaken. Then God hid his face, and illness brought him close to the Pit. He remembers what he prayed: what profit is there in my death, can the dust praise you?

God heard, and healed him. The psalm is full of reversals: weeping that lasts for a night and joy in the morning, mourning turned into dancing, sackcloth stripped away and replaced with gladness. The singer calls on the faithful to join him, and promises to give thanks forever.

=== 31
Psalm thirty-one is a long prayer of trust, written by someone in grave danger who nonetheless keeps returning to God as his rock and refuge. Its most famous line comes early. Into your hands I commit my spirit. According to the Gospel of Luke, Jesus spoke these words as his last before he died.

The psalm moves back and forth between distress and confidence, more than once. The singer describes himself as wasting away in grief, a disgrace to his neighbours, avoided in the street by friends, forgotten like a dead man and as useless as a broken pot. He hears whispering all around him, and a phrase the prophet Jeremiah would later use many times: terror on every side. Against all this he sets a simple affirmation. My times are in your hands.

Near the end, he gives thanks for God's loving devotion shown to him in a city under siege, and admits that in his alarm he had thought himself cut off. The psalm ends by turning outward, urging all God's people to be strong and take heart.

=== 32
Psalm thirty-two is called a Maskil, a word that may point to a skilful song or a song of instruction. Both fit. It is a psalm of thanksgiving for forgiveness, and in church tradition it became the second of the penitential psalms. The apostle Paul quotes its opening in his letter to the Romans as David's description of the blessing of being forgiven.

It begins with two beatitudes for the one whose sins are covered. Then the psalmist tells his own story. While he kept silent about his sin, he wasted away under God's heavy hand, his strength drained as though in the summer heat. When at last he confessed, God forgave. No details of the sin are given, and the psalm makes no attempt to supply them.

In the second half a new voice seems to speak, probably God himself, promising to instruct and guide, and warning against being like a stubborn horse or mule that must be steered with bit and bridle. The psalm ends with a call to the righteous to rejoice.

=== 33
Psalm thirty-two ended with a call to the righteous to rejoice and shout for joy. Psalm thirty-three, which has no heading, takes up the same call and turns it into a full hymn of praise.

It begins with music: the harp, the ten-stringed lyre, a new song played skilfully with shouts of joy. Then it gives the reasons for praise, and they are on the largest scale. The Lord made the heavens by his word, and the stars by the breath of his mouth. He gathers the sea as if into a jar and stores up the deep. He spoke, and it came to be.

From creation the psalm moves to history. The plans of the nations come to nothing, but the Lord's purposes stand forever. He looks down on every human being. No king is saved by the size of his army, and a warhorse is a false hope. The psalm closes with the congregation waiting quietly for the Lord, their help and shield, and asking that his loving devotion rest upon them.

=== 34
The heading of Psalm thirty-four takes us back to the years when David was running from Saul. It recalls the day he pretended to be insane before the king, who drove him away. The first book of Samuel tells the story. Alone and in danger, David fled into Philistine territory, to the city of Gath, of all places, the home town of Goliath. There he was recognised, and in fear for his life he acted the madman, scrawling on the gate and letting saliva run down his beard, until the king dismissed him in disgust. In Samuel the king is named Achish. The heading calls him Abimelech, which seems to have been a royal title among the Philistines, much as Pharaoh was in Egypt.

The psalm itself is a thanksgiving, and like Psalm twenty-five it is an acrostic. It begins with David inviting others to praise God with him, because this poor man called out, and the Lord heard. Then it becomes a lesson, as David calls his hearers children and offers to teach them the fear of the Lord. Listen for its well-known invitation, taste and see that the Lord is good, and for its promise that the Lord is near to the brokenhearted.

=== 35
Psalm thirty-five is the prayer of a man under attack from people he once treated as friends. He asks the Lord to come to his aid as a warrior, taking up shield, spear and javelin. Then the imagery shifts to the law court, where hostile witnesses accuse him of things he knows nothing about.

What wounds him most is their ingratitude. When they were sick, he wore sackcloth and fasted for them, grieving as he would for a brother or his own mother. Now that he has stumbled, they gather in glee, mocking him like jesters at a feast and crying, aha, aha.

The psalm moves through three cycles of complaint and prayer, each ending with a promise of praise. Its requests that God would shame and scatter his enemies are hard to hear, but they come from someone who has handed the matter to God rather than taking revenge himself. The psalm has no stated occasion, though it fits the years when David was hunted and slandered. Its last lines look forward to his friends shouting for joy at his vindication.

=== 36
Psalm thirty-six sets two portraits side by side. The heading calls David the servant of the Lord, a title also given to him in Psalm eighteen, and the opening line speaks of an oracle in his heart, a message about the wicked.

The first portrait is of the wicked man. He has no fear of God before his eyes. He is so pleased with himself that he cannot see his own sin. He lies on his bed and plans evil, and he has stopped trying to do good.

Then, without transition, the psalmist lifts his eyes to God, and the language soars. God's loving devotion reaches to the heavens, his faithfulness to the clouds, his righteousness is like the highest mountains and his judgements like the deepest sea. People take refuge in the shadow of his wings, feast in his house and drink from his river of delights. For with you is the fountain of life, the psalmist says, and in your light we see light. The psalm ends with a brief prayer for protection and a glimpse of the evildoers fallen.

=== 37
Psalm thirty-seven is a wisdom psalm, and the longest of David's acrostics, with each pair of verses beginning with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet. It is less a prayer than a body of counsel, spoken by someone who says he was once young and is now old.

Its subject is a question that troubled Israel's wise men: what should the faithful do when the wicked prosper? The psalm's answer begins with its opening words. Do not fret. Again and again it says that the success of the wicked is short-lived. They wither like grass and vanish like smoke, while those who trust in the Lord will inherit the land. That phrase, inheriting the land, recurs throughout the psalm, recalling God's promise to Israel's ancestors. Jesus echoes it in the Sermon on the Mount: the meek will inherit the earth.

Because the psalm is built on the alphabet, it moves less by argument than by return, circling its theme from many angles. Listen for its gentle, repeated commands: trust in the Lord, delight in him, commit your way to him, be still, and wait.

=== 38
Psalm thirty-eight is the third of the church's seven penitential psalms. Its opening line is almost identical to the opening of Psalm six, and its heading adds that it is for remembrance, perhaps meaning a psalm to bring the sufferer's case to God's attention, or one used with a particular offering; the term is uncertain.

The psalmist is ill, and he connects his suffering directly to his own sin. His wounds fester, his back burns, his heart pounds and the light in his eyes has gone out. Worse, his friends and family keep their distance from him, while his enemies are plotting against him.

What he does in response is striking. He becomes like a deaf man who cannot hear and a mute who cannot answer, refusing to argue with his accusers, and waits instead for the Lord. He confesses his iniquity openly. The psalm has no turn to praise at the end; it closes with a plea that God would not forsake him and would come quickly, the Lord his Saviour.

=== 39
The heading of Psalm thirty-nine names Jeduthun, one of the three leaders of music David appointed in the worship of Israel, alongside Asaph and Heman. The psalm itself is one of the most personal and sombre in the book.

It begins with silence. The psalmist resolved to keep quiet in front of the wicked, to guard his tongue as if with a muzzle. But the silence only made his distress burn hotter, until at last he spoke. What he says is not addressed to his enemies but to God: show me my end, let me know how fleeting my life is.

What follows is a meditation on the brevity of human life. A lifetime is a few handbreadths. Each person is a mere breath, a passing shadow who heaps up wealth without knowing who will gather it. The psalmist calls himself a foreigner and a sojourner with God, as his fathers were. Unusually, the psalm ends without resolution, with a request that God would look away from him for a while, so that he might find some cheer before he is gone.

=== 40
Psalm forty begins where many laments end, with the answer already received. I waited patiently for the Lord, David says, and he heard my cry. God lifted him out of a pit, out of the miry clay, set his feet upon a rock and put a new song in his mouth.

The song becomes a testimony before the great assembly. In the middle of it comes a passage about sacrifice. God did not want sacrifices and offerings, David says, but opened his ears, and he answered, here I am, I delight to do your will. The letter to the Hebrews quotes these lines and places them on the lips of Christ.

Then, unexpectedly, the mood darkens. Troubles beyond counting surround him again, his sins have caught up with him, and his heart fails. The psalm, which began with thanksgiving, ends with an urgent plea: O my God, do not delay. This final section, from the request to hurry to help him onward, appears again almost word for word as Psalm seventy.

=== 41
Psalm forty-one brings Book One of the Psalter to its close. It begins as Psalm one did, with the word blessed, but here the blessing falls on the one who cares for the poor and weak, whom the Lord will sustain on his sickbed.

Then the psalmist speaks of his own illness. He confesses that he has sinned, and asks God to heal him. Meanwhile his enemies wait for him to die, and visitors who come to his bedside pretend sympathy, then go out and spread rumours that he will never rise again. Most painful of all, his close friend, one who shared his bread, has turned against him. In John's Gospel, Jesus quotes this line at the Last Supper, speaking of the disciple who would betray him.

The psalm ends with the singer's confidence that God upholds him. Its final line is not part of the psalm itself, but the formal ending of the first book: blessed be the Lord, the God of Israel, from everlasting to everlasting. Amen and Amen.

=== 42
With Psalm forty-two, Book Two of the Psalter begins. Its first psalms belong to the sons of Korah, the family of Levite singers. From here until well into Book Three, the poems prefer to call God simply God, rather than using his personal name, the LORD, though no one knows quite why the collection took that shape.

Psalms forty-two and forty-three are almost certainly one poem. They share a refrain, sung three times: why are you downcast, O my soul? Put your hope in God. The singer is far from Jerusalem, in the land of the Jordan and the peaks of Hermon in the far north, where the headwaters of the river crash down from the mountain. He remembers leading crowds in festive procession to the house of God, and now he thirsts for God as a deer pants for streams of water.

Around him people taunt him: where is your God? The roaring waterfalls become an image of grief overwhelming him, breaker after breaker. Why he is so far away, the psalm does not tell us. But each time the refrain returns, he talks back to his own despair.

=== 43
Psalm forty-three has no heading, and it continues the poem begun in Psalm forty-two. The same exile is speaking, still far from the house of God, and still asking why he must go about mourning because of the enemy.

Now, though, the lament turns more directly into a request. He asks God to plead his case against an ungodly nation and deceitful men. Then comes the heart of this final stanza: a prayer that God would send out his light and his truth, like guides, to lead him back to the holy mountain, the place where God dwells.

He pictures the return. He will go to the altar of God, to God his greatest joy, and praise him with the lyre. The despair of the first two stanzas has not yet lifted. But the refrain is heard for a third and final time, and this time it ends looking forward to the altar in Jerusalem.

=== 44
Psalm forty-four is a lament for the whole nation, and one of the most searching in the book. It is another Maskil of the sons of Korah.

It begins by remembering. The fathers have told the story of how God drove out the nations and planted Israel in the land, not by their own swords, but by his right hand. The people still trust him: they say they do not rely on bow or sword, and they boast in God all day long.

Then comes the shock. God has rejected them. The army has been defeated, the people scattered among the nations, sold off as cheaply as sheep, and made a laughingstock. The psalm does not tell us which defeat this was, and many have been suggested. What makes it so striking is its protest. The nation has not forgotten God or turned to foreign gods, and yet this has happened. For your sake, they say, we face death all day long. Paul quotes that line in Romans. The psalm ends with a bold cry for God to wake up, and an appeal to his loving devotion.

=== 45
Psalm forty-five is unlike any other in the collection. Its heading calls it a love song, to the tune of The Lilies, and it was written for a royal wedding. Which king is being married, the psalm does not say.

The poet begins by telling us his heart is stirred and his tongue is like the pen of a skilled writer. Then he turns to the king himself, and praises his beauty, his gracious speech, and his majesty as a warrior riding out for truth and justice. In one remarkable line the king's throne is addressed as the throne of God, enduring forever. The letter to the Hebrews quotes these verses about the Son of God.

The scene then moves to the bride. She is a princess from another land, urged to forget her own people and her father's house, and to honour the king. Gifts arrive from Tyre, the wealthy Phoenician city on the coast. She is led into the palace in gold-embroidered robes, with her companions behind her. The psalm ends with a promise to the king of sons who will become princes, and a name remembered through all generations.

=== 46
Psalm forty-six is a song of confidence in God when the whole world seems to be collapsing. The heading adds the term Alamoth, which may mean young women and perhaps refers to high voices; its exact meaning is not known. Centuries later, this psalm inspired Martin Luther's hymn A Mighty Fortress Is Our God.

It moves in three stanzas, and the second and third end with a refrain: the Lord of Hosts is with us, the God of Jacob is our fortress. In the first stanza, nature itself is in upheaval, as the mountains topple into the sea and the waters roar. In the second, the scene shifts to the city of God, Jerusalem, which in reality had no great river, but here is pictured as gladdened by streams and protected by God's presence, while nations rage and kingdoms totter.

In the third stanza the listener is invited to come and see the Lord bringing wars to an end, breaking bows and burning shields. Then God himself speaks: be still, and know that I am God.

=== 47
Psalm forty-seven is a hymn celebrating God as king of all the earth, and it is full of noise. Clap your hands, all you peoples, it begins, shout to God with cries of joy.

At its centre is a dramatic picture. God has gone up amid shouts of joy, the Lord with the sound of the horn, the ram's horn trumpet that sounded at great occasions in Israel's worship and at the crowning of kings. It may echo a procession in which the ark of the covenant, the symbol of God's throne, was carried up to Zion. Then the call to sing praises rings out again and again.

The psalm is aimed at the nations as much as at Israel. God has chosen Jacob's inheritance, but he reigns over all peoples. In its closing scene the leaders of the nations gather as the people of the God of Abraham, recalling the old promise that through Abraham all the families of the earth would be blessed.

=== 48
Psalm forty-eight is a song of Zion, celebrating Jerusalem as the city of the great King. Together with the two psalms before it, it forms a trio about God's reign and his presence among his people.

The psalm praises the beauty and height of Mount Zion, comparing it to Zaphon, the mountain in the far north where Israel's neighbours imagined their gods assembling. For Israel the true holy mountain is Zion, and its true defence is God in her citadels.

Then comes a story, told in quick strokes. Kings joined forces and advanced on the city. They saw it, were astonished, and fled in terror, gripped by pains like a woman in labour, and their great ocean-going ships, the ships of Tarshish, were wrecked by an east wind. No particular siege is named. In the final lines the worshippers are invited to walk around Zion, count her towers and study her ramparts, so that they can tell the next generation that this God is their God forever.

=== 49
Psalm forty-nine is a wisdom psalm, and it begins by summoning everyone, high and low, rich and poor. The singer of this psalm of the sons of Korah says he will solve a riddle to the music of the lyre.

The riddle is the problem of wealth. Why should the faithful be afraid when rich and ruthless people surround them? The answer is that money cannot buy what matters most. No one can pay God a ransom to escape death. Wise and foolish alike die and leave their riches to others. They may name their estates after themselves, but the grave becomes their permanent home. Twice the refrain returns: a man in his splendour, without understanding, is like the beasts that perish.

Death is pictured as a shepherd herding the proud into Sheol. But in the middle of the psalm comes one of the clearest flashes of hope beyond death in the Old Testament: God will redeem my life from Sheol, for he will surely take me to himself. The psalm ends with its warning not to be overawed when another man grows rich.
