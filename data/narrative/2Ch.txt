=== intro
Second Chronicles is the second half of a single work, and it opens exactly where the first half closed. David has died full of days, and Solomon sits on the throne of the Lord. From here the book covers about four hundred years, from Solomon's reign in the tenth century before Christ to the burning of Jerusalem by the Babylonians early in the sixth, and then, in its final lines, it leaps across the years of exile to the day the Persian king Cyrus allowed the exiles to go home.

The listener has heard most of these events before, in the books of Kings, and the same writer who shaped First Chronicles is at work here, retelling the history for the community that returned from Babylon. Knowing what he has chosen to do makes the book far easier to follow.

The first nine chapters belong to Solomon, and almost all of them concern the temple: its preparation, its building, its furnishings, the day the ark was carried in and the glory of the Lord filled the house, Solomon's great prayer of dedication, and God's answer. Then comes Solomon's wealth and fame, and the visit of the queen of Sheba. What is missing is as telling as what is there. Kings closed its account of Solomon with his many foreign wives, the altars he built to their gods and the adversaries God raised against him. Chronicles leaves all of that aside. Its Solomon is above all the king who built the house of God.

When the kingdom divides after Solomon's death, the book makes a choice that changes its whole shape. Kings moved back and forth between Israel in the north and Judah in the south. Chronicles follows Judah alone, king after king, from Rehoboam to Zedekiah. The northern kingdom appears only where it touches Judah, in war or in alliance, and the northern prophets Elijah and Elisha, so prominent in Kings, are almost absent. For this writer the north had turned away from the house of David and from the temple in Jerusalem, yet he never stops calling its people brothers, and more than once a king of Judah invites them to come back.

The writer measures each king by one great question: did he seek the Lord? The word seek runs through the book, along with its companions, humble themselves, forsake and be unfaithful. God's reply to Solomon after the temple's dedication works almost as a key to what follows. If my people humble themselves, pray, seek my face and turn from their wicked ways, I will hear from heaven, forgive their sin and heal their land. Again and again, a king's faithfulness or unfaithfulness bears fruit within his own reign. Several kings who begin well end badly, among them Asa, Joash, Amaziah and Uzziah, and one notorious king, Manasseh, is shown repenting in captivity, an episode Kings does not record. Prophets appear at the turning points, many of them known only from this book, warning kings or encouraging them.

Some reigns receive far more space here than in Kings. Jehoshaphat appoints judges and sends teachers through the towns, and an army goes out to battle led by singers. Hezekiah, given four full chapters, reopens and cleanses the temple and holds a great Passover, sending couriers through the north to invite the remnant of Israel to come. Josiah repairs the temple, rediscovers the Book of the Law and keeps a Passover unlike any since the days of Samuel. For a community that had itself rebuilt a temple, restored its worship and gathered from exile, these reforms were close to home.

The prophets whose books stand later in the Old Testament belong inside these years. Isaiah appears beside Hezekiah, and Jeremiah laments over Josiah and warns the last kings. Listeners who come to those books afterwards will find their settings here.

The book is honest that the end came. In its last chapter the final kings fall one after another, the temple is burned, the walls of Jerusalem are broken down and the survivors are carried to Babylon, while the land lies desolate for seventy years, as Jeremiah had said. But the writer does not end in ruin. His last words are the decree of Cyrus, inviting anyone among the Lord's people to go up to Jerusalem and build his house. In most Hebrew Bibles Chronicles stands last of all, so the Hebrew Scriptures close on that invitation. In the Christian Old Testament, the book of Ezra follows, beginning with the same decree and taking up the story of those who went.

=== 1
David is dead, and Solomon is firmly established over the kingdom. Chronicles moves straight to worship.

Solomon summons the leaders of all Israel and leads the whole assembly to Gibeon, a few miles north-west of Jerusalem. The listener will remember the arrangement David left behind. The ark of the covenant rests in a tent in Jerusalem, but the old Tent of Meeting from the wilderness stands at Gibeon, with the bronze altar made in Moses' day by the craftsman Bezalel. There Solomon offers a thousand burnt offerings.

That night God appears to Solomon with an open invitation: ask what you wish. The young king's request, and God's answer to it, shape the reign that follows. The chapter ends with a brief picture of Solomon's wealth: silver and gold as common in Jerusalem as stones, chariots and horses, and a trade in horses with Egypt and with Kue, in what is now southern Turkey.

=== 2
Solomon returns from Gibeon to the work his father prepared for him: the house of the Lord. He conscripts labourers, and he writes to Hiram, king of Tyre, the Phoenician city on the coast that had supplied David with cedar for his palace.

Solomon's letter explains what the temple is for: incense, the bread of the presence, and the regular burnt offerings of the Sabbaths, new moons and appointed feasts. It also carries a striking admission. The house will be great, because God is greater than all gods, and yet the highest heavens cannot contain him. Who am I, Solomon asks, to build a house for him?

Hiram replies warmly. He blesses the God of Israel, agrees to supply timber from the forests of Lebanon, and sends a master craftsman, Huram-abi, whose mother came from the tribe of Dan and whose father was a man of Tyre. The logs will be floated down the coast in rafts to Joppa, the nearest port to Jerusalem, and carried inland from there. In return, Solomon will send wheat, barley, wine and olive oil. The workforce is drawn from the foreigners living in Israel.

=== 3
The materials are ready, and building begins in the fourth year of Solomon's reign. Chronicles names the site with care: Mount Moriah in Jerusalem, on the threshing floor of Ornan the Jebusite, where the Lord appeared to David. The name recalls the land of Moriah, where Abraham was sent to offer Isaac, and this is the only place in the Old Testament where the temple mount is called by it.

This chapter and the next describe the temple, more briefly than First Kings does. There is a porch at the front and a long main hall panelled with cypress and overlaid with gold. At the back is the Most Holy Place, twenty cubits long and twenty wide, roughly thirty feet each way, covered in fine gold, where two golden cherubim stand with outstretched wings reaching from wall to wall. Chronicles adds a detail Kings does not give: a veil of blue, purple and crimson, woven with cherubim, like the curtain of the old tabernacle.

At the front stand two bronze pillars with capitals of chainwork and pomegranates, and Solomon gives them names, Jachin and Boaz.

=== 4
The building is complete, and now come its furnishings, the objects that would be used every day in the worship of the temple.

In the courtyard there is a great bronze altar for burnt offerings, and beside it the Sea, a huge cast bronze basin resting on twelve bronze oxen, three facing each point of the compass. The Sea held water for the priests' washing, and ten smaller basins on stands were used to rinse the parts of the offerings. Inside the temple Solomon places ten golden lampstands and ten tables, five on each side of the main hall.

Much of the bronze work is credited to Huram, the craftsman sent by the king of Tyre, who casts it in clay moulds in the Jordan valley. There is so much bronze that no one weighs it. The gold furnishings follow: the golden altar of incense, the tables for the bread of the presence, the lampstands and lamps, and the golden doors of the inner sanctuary and the main hall. Some of the materials came from the plunder of David's wars, set aside years before for this purpose.

=== 5
The temple is finished and furnished, and Solomon brings in the treasures his father dedicated. Now the ark of the covenant must be carried from the City of David, on the southern spur of Jerusalem, up to the new temple on the higher ground to the north.

Solomon assembles the elders of Israel at the autumn Feast of Tabernacles, in the seventh month. This time the ark is carried as it should be, by the Levites, and the old Tent of Meeting and its holy furnishings are brought up too. The priests set the ark in the Most Holy Place, beneath the wings of the cherubim. Inside it there is nothing but the two tablets of stone Moses placed there at Mount Horeb.

The account follows First Kings closely, but Chronicles adds the music. As the priests come out, the Levite singers of Asaph, Heman and Jeduthun stand beside the altar in fine linen, with a hundred and twenty priests sounding trumpets, and they lift their voices together in a refrain the listener has heard before: for he is good; his loving devotion endures for ever.

=== 6
The glory of the Lord has filled the temple in a cloud so thick that the priests cannot stand to minister. Solomon turns to the people, blesses them, and declares that God's promise to David, that his son would build the house, has been kept. Then, standing on a bronze platform in the middle of the courtyard, he kneels before the whole assembly, spreads out his hands toward heaven and prays.

This is the prayer the listener heard in First Kings, given here almost word for word. Solomon acknowledges that even the highest heavens cannot contain God, much less this house. He asks that God's eyes would be open toward this place, and he names the troubles that would drive the people to pray toward it: defeat, drought, famine, plague and war. Foreigners, too, may come and pray. The last situation he names is exile in a distant land. For a writer whose own community had lived through exactly that, these lines were not distant history.

The ending differs from Kings. Instead of recalling Moses and the exodus, Solomon closes with words from the hundred and thirty-second psalm, calling on God to arise and enter his resting place, and to remember his loving devotion to David.

=== 7
Solomon has finished praying, and Chronicles records something Kings does not: fire comes down from heaven and consumes the sacrifices, as it did when David built his altar on this same ground, and the glory of the Lord fills the temple again. The people fall on their faces on the pavement.

The dedication becomes a great national festival, seven days for the altar and seven more for the Feast of Tabernacles, with people gathered from the far north to the border of Egypt. Then Solomon sends them home rejoicing.

Some time later the Lord appears to Solomon at night, as he did at Gibeon, to tell him that his prayer has been heard. His answer includes a promise found only in Chronicles, which echoes through the rest of the book: if my people humble themselves, pray, seek my face and turn from their wicked ways, then I will hear from heaven, forgive their sin and heal their land. It ends with a solemn warning about what will become of the king, the people and this house if they turn to other gods.

=== 8
Twenty years have passed since the work began, and the temple and Solomon's palace both stand complete. This chapter surveys the rest of his achievements, briefly, as First Kings does.

Solomon builds and fortifies across his realm. He takes Hamath-zobah in the north and builds Tadmor in the wilderness, the oasis in the Syrian desert later known as Palmyra. He fortifies Upper and Lower Beth-horon, guarding the road up to Jerusalem from the coast, and builds store cities and chariot cities. The remaining Canaanite peoples are put to forced labour, while Israelites serve as soldiers and officers.

Two details show the writer's concerns. Solomon moves his Egyptian wife, Pharaoh's daughter, out of the City of David, because the places where the ark has been are holy. And he keeps the full round of offerings commanded by Moses, with the priests, Levites and gatekeepers serving in the divisions David laid down.

Finally Solomon turns south to Ezion-geber, at the head of the Gulf of Aqaba, where Hiram's seasoned sailors join his men on voyages to Ophir, a distant land famous for its gold.

=== 9
Solomon's fame has spread far beyond Israel, and this chapter shows it through the eyes of a foreign visitor. The queen of Sheba arrives in Jerusalem with a great caravan of camels carrying spices, gold and precious stones. Sheba is usually placed in south-western Arabia, in the region of modern Yemen, a land that grew rich on the incense trade, though some have linked it with the African coast across the Red Sea.

She has come to test Solomon with hard questions, and the story follows First Kings closely. What she sees and hears in Jerusalem leaves her breathless.

The rest of the chapter is a portrait of Solomon at the height of his glory, his gold and ivory, his ships and his fame among the kings of the earth. Then, after forty years, Solomon dies and is buried in the City of David. Chronicles says nothing of the darker last years that Kings describes. Its account of Solomon ends in splendour, and his son Rehoboam takes the throne.

=== 10
Solomon is dead, and his son Rehoboam goes to Shechem, the old centre of the northern tribes, to be made king by all Israel. There he is met by Jeroboam son of Nebat, back from Egypt, where he had fled from Solomon. Chronicles has not told how the prophet Ahijah promised Jeroboam ten of the tribes; it expects the listener to know that story from First Kings, and mentions the prophecy only in passing.

The people bring their grievance. Solomon's building projects were carried on the backs of forced labour, and they ask the new king to lighten the yoke. Rehoboam takes three days to decide, consulting first the older men who served his father, then the young men who grew up with him.

His answer splits the kingdom. The northern tribes reject the house of David with the old cry, to your tents, O Israel, and Rehoboam is left with only Judah and Benjamin. The writer adds that this turn of events came from God. From here on, Chronicles will follow the kings of Judah alone.

=== 11
The kingdom has split, and Rehoboam's first instinct is to fight. He gathers the warriors of Judah and Benjamin to win back the north. But a man of God named Shemaiah brings the word of the Lord: do not fight against your brothers, for this is my doing. Remarkably, they listen and go home.

Rehoboam turns instead to defence. He fortifies a ring of cities across Judah, Bethlehem, Tekoa, Lachish, Azekah, Hebron and others, stocking them with food, weapons and officers. Many of these towns guarded the roads from the coastal plain and the south.

The chapter then records something Kings does not. In the north, Jeroboam has set up his own priests and his calf idols, so the priests and Levites leave their towns and lands and come south to Judah and Jerusalem. They are followed by people from every tribe of Israel who have set their hearts to seek the Lord. For three years, the writer says, Judah walks in the way of David and Solomon. The chapter closes with Rehoboam's large family, his favourite wife Maacah daughter of Absalom, and his choice of her son Abijah as heir.

=== 12
Rehoboam's kingdom has been strengthened, but once he feels secure, he and all Israel with him forsake the law of the Lord. Chronicles often calls the people of Judah Israel, because for this writer they carry the name of the whole nation.

In Rehoboam's fifth year an Egyptian army invades. Its king, Shishak, is known from Egyptian records as the founder of a new dynasty, and an inscription on a temple wall at Karnak lists towns he claimed to have taken on this campaign. His forces capture the fortified cities Rehoboam built and reach Jerusalem itself.

Kings records only the invasion and the plunder. Chronicles shows why it happened and why it was not worse. The prophet Shemaiah, who stopped the civil war, tells the king and his leaders: you have forsaken me, so I have forsaken you to Shishak. Their response, and God's answer, are the first clear example of a pattern that will run through the whole book. The chapter closes with Rehoboam's reign of seventeen years, and a verdict: he did evil, because he did not set his heart to seek the Lord.

=== 13
Rehoboam is dead, and his son Abijah becomes king of Judah. Kings, which calls him Abijam, gives him a few lines and a poor verdict. Chronicles gives him a chapter and one great speech.

The war between north and south has continued. Abijah's army faces Jeroboam's, twice its size, near Mount Zemaraim in the hill country of Ephraim, close to the border between the two kingdoms. Before the battle Abijah climbs the mountain and calls out to Jeroboam and all Israel.

His speech sets out the case for Judah. The Lord gave the kingship to David and his descendants for ever by a covenant of salt, an unbreakable agreement. Jeroboam rebelled against his master. The north has its golden calves and has driven out the true priests, while in Jerusalem the sons of Aaron and the Levites still offer the sacrifices and tend the lamps. Do not fight against the Lord, Abijah cries, for you will not succeed. Meanwhile Jeroboam has sent troops around to ambush Judah from behind.

=== 14
Abijah has died, and his son Asa becomes king. The land has peace for ten years, and Chronicles explains why: Asa does what is good and right in the eyes of the Lord. He removes the foreign altars and the high places, breaks the sacred pillars and cuts down the Asherah poles, and he commands Judah to seek the Lord. The peace gives him time to build walled cities, and he tells the people that the land is still theirs because they have sought the Lord.

Then a great army comes up from the south, led by Zerah the Cushite. The name Cush usually refers to the lands south of Egypt, though this force may have come from Egypt or from peoples living in the southern desert; the historical details are uncertain. They reach Mareshah, in the lowlands south-west of Jerusalem.

Asa marches out and prays one of the short, urgent prayers that mark this book: there is no one besides you to help the powerless against the mighty. The battle and the pursuit that follow, as far as Gerar near the coast, are told in Chronicles alone. The next chapter will show what Asa does with his victory.

=== 15
Asa's army is returning from its victory over Zerah, laden with plunder, when the Spirit of God comes upon a prophet named Azariah son of Oded. He goes out to meet the king with a message that states the conviction of the whole book: the Lord is with you while you are with him. If you seek him, he will be found by you; if you forsake him, he will forsake you.

Azariah recalls darker times, when Israel was without the true God, without a teaching priest and without the law, an age that sounds like the days of the judges. Encouraged, Asa widens his reform, removing idols from Judah and Benjamin and from towns he has taken in the north, and repairing the altar in front of the temple.

In the fifteenth year of his reign he gathers the people in Jerusalem, among them many from Ephraim, Manasseh and Simeon who have come south because they saw that the Lord was with him. Together they make a solemn covenant to seek the Lord with all their heart and soul. Not even the queen mother is spared when Asa clears the land of idols.

=== 16
Asa has reigned for many years in peace. Now, late in his reign, trouble comes from the north. Baasha, king of Israel, fortifies Ramah, a few miles north of Jerusalem on the main road, to cut off traffic in and out of Judah. The year given, the thirty-sixth of Asa's reign, is hard to fit with the dates in Kings, and it has been explained in different ways.

Asa's response is the one Kings records. He sends silver and gold from the temple and palace treasuries to Ben-hadad, the Aramean king in Damascus, to break his treaty with Baasha. The plan works, and Baasha abandons Ramah.

Kings leaves it there. Chronicles adds the prophet's verdict. Hanani the seer reminds Asa of the battle with Zerah, when he relied on the Lord against a far larger army; now he has relied on a foreign king instead. How Asa receives that rebuke, and how his last years unfold, make this one of the saddest chapters in the book.

=== 17
Asa's son Jehoshaphat now takes the throne, and Chronicles gives him four chapters, far more than Kings does. He begins by strengthening Judah against the northern kingdom, placing troops in the fortified cities and in the towns of Ephraim his father had taken.

The writer is warm towards him. Jehoshaphat walks in the earlier ways of his ancestor David, seeks the God of his father rather than the Baals, and takes delight in the ways of the Lord. He removes high places and Asherah poles from Judah.

In the third year of his reign he does something new. He sends out a group of officials, Levites and priests to travel through the towns of Judah, carrying the Book of the Law of the Lord and teaching the people. It is the first time in this history that a king organises the teaching of God's law across the land, and it anticipates the role of the Levites as teachers in Ezra's day.

The result is peace and prosperity. The surrounding nations fear to attack him, the Philistines and Arabs bring tribute, and Jehoshaphat builds fortresses and store cities and gathers a great army.

=== 18
Jehoshaphat is rich and honoured, and now he makes a fateful decision: he allies himself by marriage with Ahab, the northern king who married Jezebel and promoted the worship of Baal. Jehoshaphat's son Jehoram is married to Ahab's daughter Athaliah, and the consequences will reach far into Judah's future.

Some years later Jehoshaphat visits Ahab in Samaria, the northern capital. Ahab feasts him and asks him to join a campaign to recapture Ramoth-gilead, a fortified town east of the Jordan held by the Arameans. Jehoshaphat agrees, but asks first to hear the word of the Lord.

This is one of the very few stories about a northern king that Chronicles tells in full, and it follows First Kings almost word for word. Four hundred prophets promise victory. Jehoshaphat asks whether there is not another, and Ahab admits there is one, Micaiah son of Imlah, whom he hates because he never prophesies anything good about him. Chronicles includes the story because Judah's king was there, and because it shows the danger of his alliance.

=== 19
Jehoshaphat has come home safely from Ramoth-gilead, where Ahab fell. He is met by the prophet Jehu son of Hanani, whose father once rebuked Asa. Jehu rebukes the king for helping the wicked and loving those who hate the Lord, but adds that some good is found in him, because he has set his heart on seeking God.

Jehoshaphat's response is to go out among the people himself, from Beersheba in the south to the hill country of Ephraim, turning them back to the Lord. Then he reforms the courts. His name means the Lord judges, and the chapter is about justice.

He appoints judges in the fortified cities and charges them to remember that they judge not for man but for the Lord, with whom there is no injustice, partiality or bribery. In Jerusalem he sets up a higher court of Levites, priests and family heads to settle the hardest cases, under the chief priest in matters of the Lord and a leader of Judah in matters of the king.

=== 20
Some time later a great alliance moves against Judah: the Moabites and Ammonites, from east of the Dead Sea, with the Meunites and men from Mount Seir, the hill country of Edom. They have come from beyond the Sea and are already at En-gedi, an oasis on its western shore, within striking distance of Jerusalem.

Jehoshaphat is alarmed, and he sets his face to seek the Lord. He proclaims a fast, and people come from every town of Judah to the temple. The king stands before them and prays, recalling Solomon's prayer that whenever disaster came, the people could cry out at this house and God would hear. We do not know what to do, he says, but our eyes are upon you.

As the men of Judah stand with their wives and children before the Lord, the Spirit comes upon a Levite named Jahaziel, a descendant of Asaph, with a message for king and people. What happens the next morning in the wilderness of Tekoa, with singers marching at the head of the army, is one of the most unusual battles in the Old Testament. The chapter closes with a summary of Jehoshaphat's reign and a failed shipbuilding venture with Ahab's son Ahaziah.

=== 21
Jehoshaphat has died, and the throne passes to his eldest son Jehoram. The alliance with Ahab's house now bears its bitter fruit. Jehoram's wife is Ahab's daughter Athaliah, and he walks in the ways of the kings of Israel.

A word about names. At this time the northern kingdom was also ruled by a Jehoram, or Joram, a son of Ahab, so the two kingdoms had kings of the same name who were brothers-in-law. Chronicles follows only the king of Judah.

His reign begins with blood: once secure, Jehoram puts all six of his brothers to the sword. Yet the writer adds that the Lord would not destroy the house of David, because of his covenant and his promise to keep a lamp burning for David's line.

Under Jehoram, Edom throws off Judah's rule and the town of Libnah revolts. Then a letter arrives from the prophet Elijah, the only time that great northern prophet appears in Chronicles. Its message is stern, and the rest of the chapter shows it fulfilled. Jehoram dies, as the writer puts it, to no one's regret.

=== 22
Raiders have killed all Jehoram's sons except the youngest, Ahaziah, and the people of Jerusalem make him king. He reigns only one year. His mother Athaliah, granddaughter of Omri and daughter of Ahab, is his chief counsellor, and her family advise him to his ruin.

He joins his uncle Joram, king of Israel, in war against the Arameans at Ramoth-gilead, where Ahab died. Joram is wounded and goes to Jezreel to recover, and Ahaziah goes to visit him. It is a fatal visit. The listener knows from Second Kings what is happening in the north: Jehu son of Nimshi has been anointed to destroy the house of Ahab, and Ahaziah is caught up in the purge.

What follows is the darkest moment for David's line in the whole book. Athaliah, seeing her son dead, sets out to kill every royal heir of Judah and seizes the throne herself. If she succeeds, the promise to David will fail. But one infant survives. His aunt Jehoshabeath, sister of the dead king and wife of the priest Jehoiada, hides the baby Joash and his nurse, and for six years he lives hidden in the house of God.

=== 23
For six years Athaliah has ruled Judah, the only queen ever to reign there in her own right, and the young Joash has been hidden in the temple. Now, in the seventh year, the priest Jehoiada acts.

He makes a covenant with the military commanders, and they go quietly through the towns of Judah gathering Levites and family heads to Jerusalem. In the temple Jehoiada presents the king's son to them: he must reign, as the Lord promised concerning the descendants of David.

The plan follows Second Kings, but Chronicles gives the priests and Levites the central role. Jehoiada times it for the Sabbath changeover, when two divisions would be in the temple at once, giving him a double guard without raising suspicion. Only those who are consecrated may enter the house of the Lord, and the Levites surround the boy armed with the spears and shields of King David kept in the temple.

The coronation takes place by the pillar at the entrance, with trumpets and singers. When Athaliah hears the noise and comes to see, her cry of treason comes too late.

=== 24
Joash becomes king at seven years old and reigns for forty years. Chronicles divides his reign sharply in two: he does what is right all the days of Jehoiada the priest.

The first half of the chapter tells how the young king sets his heart on repairing the house of the Lord, damaged under Athaliah, whose sons had taken its sacred objects for the Baals. When the Levites are slow to collect the money, a chest is placed at the temple gate for the tax Moses laid on Israel, and it is filled again and again.

Then Jehoiada dies, very old, and is buried among the kings. With him gone, the officials of Judah come to Joash, and he listens to them. The people abandon the temple, and the prophets God sends are ignored. Then the Spirit comes upon Zechariah, Jehoiada's own son. Many readers have seen in his death the event Jesus recalled when he spoke of the blood of the prophets, from Abel to Zechariah, who perished between the altar and the sanctuary. Abel's death is told near the beginning of the Hebrew Bible, and Zechariah's near its end.

=== 25
Joash has been murdered by his own servants, and his son Amaziah becomes king. The writer's verdict comes at once: he did what was right in the eyes of the Lord, but not wholeheartedly. The rest of the chapter shows what that means.

He executes his father's murderers but spares their children, as the law of Moses commands. Then he prepares to reconquer Edom, which had broken away from Judah in the days of Jehoram, and hires a hundred thousand soldiers from the northern kingdom. A man of God warns him that the Lord is not with Israel, and Amaziah sends them home, losing the silver he has paid. The angry mercenaries raid Judah's towns on their way back.

Amaziah defeats Edom in the Valley of Salt. Then he does something that makes no sense to the writer: he brings home the gods of the defeated Edomites and worships them, and silences the prophet who challenges him. Proud of his victory, he then challenges Jehoash, king of Israel, to battle. Jehoash answers with a scornful fable about a thistle and a cedar, and they meet at Beth-shemesh.

=== 26
Amaziah has been killed at Lachish, and the people of Judah make his sixteen-year-old son Uzziah king. Kings calls him Azariah and tells his story in a few verses. Chronicles gives him a full chapter.

He reigns for fifty-two years, a long age of prosperity in both kingdoms. The prophets Amos and Hosea were active in these years, and Isaiah would date his great vision of the Lord to the year Uzziah died. Uzziah seeks God in the days of a man named Zechariah, who instructs him in the fear of God, and as long as he seeks the Lord, God gives him success.

The chapter lists that success in detail. Uzziah breaks down the walls of Philistine cities, subdues Arabs and Meunites, receives tribute from Ammon and rebuilds the port of Eloth on the Red Sea. He builds towers, digs cisterns for his herds, and is described as a lover of the soil. His army is well equipped, and Jerusalem's towers carry engines for shooting arrows and hurling stones.

Then comes the turning. Once Uzziah is powerful, his pride leads to his downfall. He enters the temple to burn incense on the altar, a task given only to the priests, and the chief priest Azariah and eighty others go in after him.

=== 27
Uzziah has died a leper, set apart from the house of the Lord, and his son Jotham, who governed for his father during his illness, now reigns in his own right. This short chapter has nothing but good to say of him.

Jotham does what is right, as his father did, and the writer adds pointedly that he did not enter the temple of the Lord, the sin that brought Uzziah down. He rebuilds the Upper Gate of the temple and strengthens the wall on the hill of Ophel, between the City of David and the temple. He builds towns and fortresses, and defeats the Ammonites, who pay him tribute for three years.

Yet one line carries a warning: the people still behaved corruptly. The prophets Isaiah and Micah were already at work, speaking against injustice and false worship in Judah, and beyond the borders Assyria was rising again under Tiglath-pileser. The next reign would feel its full weight.

=== 28
Jotham ordered his ways before the Lord. His son Ahaz does the opposite, and the writer portrays him as one of the worst kings Judah ever had. He makes cast images of the Baals, burns incense in the Valley of Ben-hinnom outside Jerusalem, and sacrifices his own sons in the fire.

Judgement falls quickly. The king of Aram, ruling from Damascus, and Pekah, king of Israel, both strike Judah hard. This is the crisis behind the seventh chapter of Isaiah, when the prophet went out to meet Ahaz and urged him to trust the Lord.

Chronicles adds a remarkable scene in Samaria. The northern army returns with two hundred thousand captives from Judah, its own kinsmen, and a prophet named Oded goes out to meet them, with some of the leaders of Ephraim at his side. What they do with the captives is one of the most humane moments in the history of the divided kingdom.

Meanwhile Ahaz, pressed by Edomites and Philistines as well, turns to the king of Assyria for help and pays dearly for it. In his distress he becomes even more unfaithful, and the chapter ends with the doors of the temple shut.

=== 29
Ahaz is dead, and his son Hezekiah takes the throne. Chronicles gives him four chapters. Kings told his story mainly through the Assyrian invasion and his illness; Chronicles tells those events briefly, and gives most of its attention to what Hezekiah does for the temple and its worship.

He wastes no time. In the very first month of his reign he opens and repairs the doors of the house of the Lord that his father had shut. Then he gathers the priests and Levites in the square on the east side. Our fathers were unfaithful, he tells them. They shut the doors, put out the lamps and stopped the offerings, and that is why the wrath of the Lord has fallen on us. Now it is in my heart to make a covenant with the Lord.

The Levites set to work, and the cleansing of the temple takes sixteen days, the impurities carried out to the Kidron Valley east of the city. Then Hezekiah and the officials go up for sacrifices of atonement for the kingdom, the sanctuary and all Israel, and as the burnt offering is made, the Levites take up the instruments of David and the singing begins.

=== 30
The temple has been cleansed and its worship restored. Now Hezekiah plans something more ambitious: a Passover for all Israel. He sends couriers not only through Judah but through the territory of the old northern kingdom, from Beersheba to Dan, inviting the people to Jerusalem.

The north has suffered terribly at the hands of Assyria. Chronicles does not narrate the fall of Samaria, which the listener heard in Second Kings, but Hezekiah's letter speaks to those who remain, who have escaped the grasp of the kings of Assyria and whose relatives are in captivity. Return to the Lord, it says, and he will return to you.

The response is mixed. In many towns the couriers are mocked, but some from Asher, Manasseh and Zebulun humble themselves and come. The feast is held in the second month instead of the first, because the priests were not ready in time, an allowance the law of Moses made for those who could not keep it at the proper time. Many of the northern visitors have not been purified, and Hezekiah prays for them. Nothing like it, the writer says, had been seen in Jerusalem since the days of Solomon.

=== 31
The great Passover is over. Those who attended go out through Judah and Benjamin, and on into Ephraim and Manasseh in the north, breaking down sacred pillars, Asherah poles, high places and altars. Then everyone returns home.

Hezekiah sets about putting the temple's life on a lasting footing. He re-establishes the divisions of priests and Levites that David appointed, gives from his own possessions for the regular offerings, and asks the people to provide for the priests and Levites so that they can devote themselves to the law of the Lord.

The response is overwhelming. From the third month to the seventh, through the grain harvest to the end of summer, the people bring firstfruits and tithes, piled in great heaps. Storerooms are prepared in the temple, and Levites are appointed to distribute the gifts fairly to the priestly families in their towns.

These details mattered to the writer's own community, which faced the same question of how to support the temple's ministers, as the book of Nehemiah shows. The chapter closes with a summary of Hezekiah: he sought his God with all his heart, and he prospered.

=== 32
The temple has been cleansed, the Passover kept and the priests provided for. Then, the writer says, after all these acts of faithfulness, Sennacherib king of Assyria invades Judah. Faithfulness, in this book, does not mean the absence of danger.

This invasion, about seven hundred years before Christ, is one of the best-documented events in the Old Testament. Sennacherib's own annals boast that he shut Hezekiah up in Jerusalem like a bird in a cage, and carved reliefs from his palace at Nineveh show the siege of Lachish, the great fortress of Judah's lowlands. Kings tells the story at length; Chronicles condenses it into a single chapter.

Hezekiah stops up the springs outside the city so that the Assyrians will find no water, repairs the walls and gathers weapons. Then he speaks to his commanders and people: with the Assyrian king is only an arm of flesh, but with us is the Lord our God. Sennacherib's envoys taunt the defenders on the wall in Hebrew, and Hezekiah and the prophet Isaiah cry out to heaven.

The chapter goes on to Hezekiah's illness, his pride, and the envoys from Babylon. It also mentions his tunnel, which carried the waters of the Gihon spring through the rock to the west side of the City of David. That tunnel can still be walked through today.

=== 33
Hezekiah has been buried with honour, and his son Manasseh becomes king at twelve. He will reign for fifty-five years, longer than any other king of Judah, and he undoes everything his father did. He rebuilds the high places, worships the Baals and the stars, sacrifices his sons in the Valley of Ben-hinnom, consults mediums, and places an idol in the very house where God had put his name. The listener heard in Second Kings that Manasseh's sins sealed Judah's fate.

Chronicles tells that part of his story in much the same way. Then it adds something Kings does not mention at all. The Lord sends the commanders of the king of Assyria against him, and they take Manasseh captive with a hook and bronze shackles and carry him to Babylon, which in those years was under Assyrian rule.

What happens to him there is one of the most surprising turns in the book, and it shows, perhaps more clearly than any other story in Chronicles, how God answers those who humble themselves. A later Jewish writer composed a prayer in Manasseh's name, now found among the books of the Apocrypha. The chapter ends with the brief reign of his son Amon.

=== 34
Amon has been murdered in his palace, and the people of the land have made his eight-year-old son Josiah king. He will be the last great king of Judah, and the writer's verdict is the highest possible: he walked in the ways of his father David and did not turn aside to the right or to the left.

Chronicles tells his story in a slightly different order from Kings. In the eighth year of his reign, while still a youth, Josiah begins to seek the God of David. In the twelfth he starts to purge Judah and Jerusalem of idols, and the purge reaches north into the old lands of Manasseh, Ephraim and Simeon, as far as Naphtali. Assyria's grip on the region was failing, and Josiah could act in lands that had been under Assyrian rule for a century. The prophet Jeremiah was called in Josiah's thirteenth year, and Zephaniah also prophesied in his reign.

Then, in the eighteenth year, Josiah orders the temple repaired. During the work the high priest Hilkiah finds the Book of the Law of the Lord given through Moses, and when it is read to the king, he tears his clothes.

=== 35
The Book of the Law has been found and the covenant renewed. Now, in the same eighteenth year of his reign, Josiah keeps the Passover in Jerusalem, on its proper day in the first month.

Chronicles describes it in loving detail. Josiah tells the Levites to put the holy ark in the temple and to serve in the divisions laid down by David and Solomon. The king and his officials provide the animals from their own flocks and herds. The singers of Asaph stand at their posts, and the gatekeepers do not even need to leave their gates. No such Passover, the writer says, had been kept since the days of Samuel the prophet.

Then the chapter moves on some thirteen years. Assyria is collapsing before the rising power of Babylon, and Pharaoh Neco of Egypt is marching north to Carchemish on the Euphrates to support the last Assyrian forces. Josiah goes out to block his way on the plain of Megiddo. Neco sends a warning: I have not come against you, and God is with me. Josiah, like Ahab before him, disguises himself and goes into battle. The chapter ends in mourning, with Jeremiah lamenting the king.

=== 36
Josiah is dead, and the kingdom of Judah has little more than twenty years left. The final chapter of Chronicles covers them in a few short paragraphs.

Four kings follow in quick succession, three of Josiah's sons and one grandson, each reigning under the shadow of a foreign power. First Egypt makes and unmakes kings in Jerusalem. Then Babylon, under Nebuchadnezzar, takes Egypt's place as master of the region. The last king, Zedekiah, is the king the prophet Jeremiah confronted again and again. He refuses to humble himself before the prophet, and he rebels against Babylon.

Chronicles does not describe the siege or the fall in detail. Instead it explains them. The Lord sent his messengers again and again, because he had compassion on his people and on his dwelling place, but they mocked them until there was no remedy. The land, the writer says, would lie desolate for seventy years, keeping the Sabbath rests it had been denied, as Jeremiah had said.

Yet that is not the last word. The final lines leap forward to the first year of Cyrus king of Persia. The book of Ezra begins with the same words and tells what followed.
