=== intro
Second Chronicles is the second half of a single work, and it opens exactly where the first half closed. David has died full of days, and Solomon sits on the throne of the Lord. From here the book covers about four hundred years, from Solomon's reign in the tenth century before Christ to the burning of Jerusalem by the Babylonians early in the sixth, and then, in its final lines, it leaps across the years of exile to the day the Persian king Cyrus allowed the exiles to go home.

The listener has heard most of these events before, in the books of Kings, and the same writer who shaped First Chronicles is at work here, retelling the history for the community that returned from Babylon. Knowing what he has chosen to do makes the book far easier to follow.

The first nine chapters belong to Solomon, and almost all of them concern the temple: its preparation, its building, its furnishings, the day the ark was carried in and the glory of the Lord filled the house, Solomon's great prayer of dedication, and God's answer. Then comes Solomon's wealth and fame, and the visit of the queen of Sheba. What is missing is as telling as what is there. Kings closed its account of Solomon with his many foreign wives, the altars he built to their gods and the adversaries God raised against him. Chronicles leaves all of that aside. Its Solomon is above all the king who built the house of God.

When the kingdom divides after Solomon's death, the book makes a choice that changes its whole shape. Kings moved back and forth between Israel in the north and Judah in the south. Chronicles follows Judah alone, king after king, from Rehoboam to Zedekiah. The northern kingdom appears only where it touches Judah, in war or in alliance, and the northern prophets Elijah and Elisha, so prominent in Kings, are almost absent. For this writer the north had turned away from the house of David and from the temple in Jerusalem, yet he never stops calling its people brothers, and more than once a king of Judah invites them to come back.

The writer measures each king by one great question: did he seek the Lord? The word seek runs through the book, along with its companions, humble themselves, forsake and be unfaithful. God's reply to Solomon after the temple's dedication works almost as a key to what follows. If my people humble themselves, pray, seek my face and turn from their wicked ways, I will hear from heaven, forgive their sin and heal their land. Again and again, a king's faithfulness or unfaithfulness bears fruit within his own reign. Several kings who begin well end badly, among them Asa, Joash, Amaziah and Uzziah, and one notorious king, Manasseh, is shown repenting in captivity, an episode Kings does not record. Prophets appear at the turning points, many of them known only from this book, warning kings or encouraging them.

Some reigns therefore receive far more space here than in Kings. Jehoshaphat appoints judges and sends teachers through the towns, and an army goes out to battle led by singers. Hezekiah, given four full chapters, reopens and cleanses the temple and holds a great Passover, sending couriers through the north to invite the remnant of Israel to come. Josiah repairs the temple, rediscovers the Book of the Law and keeps a Passover unlike any since the days of Samuel. For a community that had itself rebuilt a temple, restored its worship and gathered from exile, these reforms were close to home.

The prophets whose books stand later in the Old Testament belong inside these years. Isaiah appears beside Hezekiah, and Jeremiah laments over Josiah and warns the last kings. Listeners who come to those books afterwards will find their settings here.

The book is honest that the end came. In its last chapter the final kings fall one after another, the temple is burned, the walls of Jerusalem are broken down and the survivors are carried to Babylon, while the land lies desolate for seventy years, as Jeremiah had said. But the writer does not end in ruin. His last words are the decree of Cyrus, inviting anyone among the Lord's people to go up to Jerusalem and build his house. In the traditional order of the Hebrew Bible, Chronicles stands last, so the Hebrew Scriptures close on that invitation. In the Christian Old Testament, the book of Ezra follows, beginning with the same decree and taking up the story of those who went.

=== 1
David is dead, and Solomon is firmly established over the kingdom. Chronicles passes over the struggle for the throne and the settling of old scores that open First Kings, and moves straight to worship.

Solomon summons the leaders of all Israel and leads the whole assembly to Gibeon, a few miles north-west of Jerusalem. The listener will remember the arrangement David left behind. The ark of the covenant rests in a tent in Jerusalem, but the old Tent of Meeting from the wilderness stands at Gibeon, with the bronze altar made in Moses' day by the craftsman Bezalel. There Solomon offers a thousand burnt offerings.

That night God appears to Solomon with an open invitation: ask what you wish. The young king's request, and God's answer to it, shape the reign that follows. The chapter ends with a brief picture of Solomon's wealth: silver and gold as common in Jerusalem as stones, chariots and horses, and a trade in horses with Egypt and with Kue, in what is now southern Turkey.

=== 2
Solomon returns from Gibeon to the work his father prepared for him: the house of the Lord. He conscripts labourers, and he writes to Hiram, king of Tyre, the Phoenician city on the coast that had supplied David with cedar for his palace.

Solomon's letter explains what the temple is for: incense, the bread of the presence, and the regular burnt offerings of the Sabbaths, new moons and appointed feasts. It also carries a striking admission. The house will be great, because God is greater than all gods, and yet the highest heavens cannot contain him. Who am I, Solomon asks, to build a house for him?

Hiram replies warmly. He blesses the God of Israel, agrees to supply timber from the forests of Lebanon, and sends a master craftsman, Huram-abi, whose mother came from the tribe of Dan and whose father was a man of Tyre. The logs will be floated down the coast in rafts to Joppa, the nearest port to Jerusalem, and carried inland from there. In return, Solomon will send wheat, barley, wine and olive oil. The workforce is drawn from the foreigners living in Israel.

=== 3
The materials are ready, and building begins in the fourth year of Solomon's reign. Chronicles names the site with care: Mount Moriah in Jerusalem, where the Lord appeared to David, on the threshing floor of Ornan the Jebusite. The name Moriah recalls the land where Abraham was sent to offer Isaac, and this is the only place in the Old Testament where the temple mount is called by it.

This chapter and the next describe the temple itself. The account is shorter than the one in First Kings and focuses on the main structure. There is a porch at the front, a long main hall panelled with cypress and overlaid with gold, decorated with palm trees and chains, and at the back the Most Holy Place, twenty cubits long and twenty wide, roughly thirty feet each way, covered in fine gold. Inside it stand two great golden cherubim whose outstretched wings reach from wall to wall. Chronicles adds a detail Kings does not give: a veil of blue, purple and crimson, woven with cherubim, like the curtain of the old tabernacle.

At the front stand two bronze pillars with capitals of chainwork and pomegranates, and Solomon gives them names, Jachin and Boaz.

=== 4
The building is complete, and now come its furnishings, the objects that would be used every day in the worship of the temple.

In the courtyard there is a great bronze altar for burnt offerings, and beside it the Sea, a huge cast bronze basin resting on twelve bronze oxen, three facing each point of the compass. The Sea held water for the priests' washing, and ten smaller basins on stands were used to rinse the parts of the offerings. Inside the temple Solomon places ten golden lampstands and ten tables, five on each side of the main hall.

Much of the bronze work is credited to Huram, the craftsman sent by the king of Tyre, who casts it in clay moulds in the Jordan valley. There is so much bronze that no one weighs it. The gold furnishings follow: the golden altar of incense, the tables for the bread of the presence, the lampstands and lamps, and the golden doors of the inner sanctuary and the main hall. Some of the materials came from the plunder of David's wars, set aside years before for this purpose.

=== 5
The temple is finished and furnished, and Solomon brings in the treasures his father dedicated. Now the most important moment arrives: the ark of the covenant must be carried from the City of David, on the southern spur of Jerusalem, up to the new temple on the higher ground to the north.

Solomon assembles the elders of Israel at the feast of the seventh month, the autumn Feast of Tabernacles. This time the ark is carried as it should be, by the Levites, and the old Tent of Meeting and its holy furnishings are brought up too. The sacrifices are beyond counting. The priests set the ark in the Most Holy Place, beneath the wings of the cherubim. Inside it there is nothing but the two tablets of stone Moses placed there at Mount Horeb.

The account follows First Kings closely, but Chronicles adds the music. As the priests come out, the Levite singers of Asaph, Heman and Jeduthun stand beside the altar in fine linen, with a hundred and twenty priests sounding trumpets, and they lift their voices together in a refrain the listener has heard before: for he is good; his loving devotion endures for ever.

=== 6
The glory of the Lord has filled the temple in a cloud so thick that the priests cannot stand to minister. Solomon now turns to the people, blesses them, and speaks.

He recalls God's promise to David that his son would build the house, and declares that the promise has been kept. Then, standing on a bronze platform in the middle of the courtyard, he kneels before the whole assembly, spreads out his hands toward heaven and prays.

This is the prayer the listener heard in First Kings, and Chronicles gives it almost word for word. Solomon acknowledges that even the highest heavens cannot contain God, much less this house. He asks that God's eyes would be open toward this place, and he names the troubles that would one day drive the people to pray toward it: defeat, drought, famine, plague, and war. Foreigners, too, may come and pray. The last of these situations is exile in a distant land, when the people come to their senses and turn back to God. For a writer whose own community had lived through exactly that, these lines were not distant history.

The ending differs from Kings. Instead of recalling Moses and the exodus, Solomon closes with words found in the hundred and thirty-second psalm, calling on God to arise and enter his resting place, and to remember his loving devotion to David.

=== 7
Solomon has finished praying, and Chronicles records something Kings does not: fire comes down from heaven and consumes the sacrifices, as it did when David built his altar on this same ground, and the glory of the Lord fills the temple again. The people fall on their faces on the pavement and repeat the refrain of the singers.

The dedication becomes a great national festival. The altar is dedicated for seven days, followed by the Feast of Tabernacles for seven more, with people gathered from the far north to the border of Egypt. Then Solomon sends them home rejoicing.

Some time later, when the temple and the royal palace are complete, the Lord appears to Solomon at night, as he did at Gibeon. He tells him that the prayer has been heard. What follows includes a promise found only in Chronicles, one that echoes through the rest of the book: if my people humble themselves, pray, seek my face and turn from their wicked ways, then I will hear from heaven and forgive them and heal their land. The chapter ends with a solemn warning about what will happen to the king, the people and this house if they turn to other gods.

=== 8
Twenty years have passed since the work began, and both the temple and Solomon's palace are complete. This chapter surveys the rest of his achievements, briefly, as First Kings does.

Solomon builds and fortifies across his realm. He rebuilds towns, takes Hamath-zobah in the north, and builds Tadmor in the wilderness, the oasis in the Syrian desert later known as Palmyra. He fortifies Upper and Lower Beth-horon, guarding the road up to Jerusalem from the coast, and builds store cities and chariot cities. The remaining Canaanite peoples are put to forced labour, while Israelites serve as soldiers and officers.

Two details show the writer's concerns. Solomon moves his Egyptian wife, Pharaoh's daughter, out of the City of David, because the places where the ark has been are holy. And he keeps the full round of offerings commanded by Moses, at the Sabbaths, the new moons and the three great feasts, with the priests, Levites and gatekeepers serving in the divisions David laid down.

Finally, Solomon turns south to Ezion-geber, at the head of the Gulf of Aqaba on the Red Sea, where Hiram's experienced sailors join his men on voyages to Ophir, a distant land famous for its gold.

=== 9
Solomon's fame has spread far beyond Israel, and this chapter shows it through the eyes of a foreign visitor. The queen of Sheba arrives in Jerusalem with a great caravan of camels carrying spices, gold and precious stones. Sheba is usually placed in south-western Arabia, in the region of modern Yemen, a land that grew rich on the incense trade, though some have linked it with the African coast across the Red Sea.

She has come to test Solomon with hard questions, and the story follows First Kings closely: what she sees and hears in Jerusalem leaves her breathless, and she blesses the God of Israel for setting Solomon on his throne.

The rest of the chapter is a portrait of Solomon at the height of his glory: the gold that came in each year, the shields of hammered gold, the great ivory throne with its lions, the ships returning every three years with gold, silver, ivory, apes and peacocks, and kings from every land seeking his wisdom. Then, after forty years, Solomon dies and is buried in the City of David. Chronicles says nothing of the darker last years that Kings describes. Its account of Solomon ends in splendour, and his son Rehoboam takes the throne.

=== 10
Solomon is dead, and his son Rehoboam goes to Shechem, the old centre of the northern tribes, to be made king by all Israel. There he is met by Jeroboam son of Nebat, back from Egypt, where he had fled from Solomon. Chronicles has not told how the prophet Ahijah promised Jeroboam ten of the tribes, a story from First Kings; it expects the listener to know it, and mentions the prophecy in passing.

The people bring their grievance. Solomon's building projects were carried on the backs of forced labour and heavy demands, and they ask the new king to lighten the yoke. Rehoboam takes three days to decide. He listens first to the older men who served his father, and then to the young men who grew up with him.

His answer splits the kingdom. The northern tribes reject the house of David with the old cry, to your tents, O Israel, and the official in charge of the forced labour is stoned to death. Rehoboam flees to Jerusalem in his chariot, left with only Judah and Benjamin. The writer adds that this turn of events came from God, fulfilling his word. From here on, Chronicles will follow the kings of Judah alone.

=== 11
The kingdom has split, and Rehoboam's first instinct is to fight. He gathers the warriors of Judah and Benjamin to win back the north. But a man of God named Shemaiah brings the word of the Lord: do not fight against your brothers, for this is my doing. Remarkably, they listen and go home.

Rehoboam turns instead to defence. He fortifies a ring of cities across Judah, Bethlehem, Tekoa, Lachish, Azekah, Hebron and others, stocking them with food, weapons and officers. Many of these towns guarded the roads from the coastal plain and the south.

The chapter then records something Kings does not. In the north, Jeroboam has set up his own priests and his calf idols, so the priests and Levites leave their towns and lands and come south to Judah and Jerusalem. They are followed by people from every tribe of Israel who have set their hearts to seek the Lord. For three years, the writer says, Judah walks in the way of David and Solomon. The chapter closes with Rehoboam's large family, his favourite wife Maacah, a granddaughter or descendant of Absalom, and his choice of her son Abijah as heir.

=== 12
Rehoboam's kingdom has been strengthened, but once he is established and secure, he and all Israel with him forsake the law of the Lord. Chronicles uses the name Israel here for Judah, because for this writer the faithful remnant in the south is Israel.

In Rehoboam's fifth year, an Egyptian army invades. Its king, Shishak, is known from Egyptian records as the founder of a new dynasty, and an inscription on a temple wall at Karnak lists towns he claimed to have taken in this campaign. His forces include Libyans and Cushites, and they capture the fortified cities Rehoboam built and reach Jerusalem itself.

Kings records only the invasion and the plunder. Chronicles shows why it happened and why it was not worse. The prophet Shemaiah, the same man who stopped the civil war, tells the king and leaders: you have forsaken me, so I have forsaken you to Shishak. Their response, and God's answer through Shemaiah, are the first clear example of a pattern that will run through the whole book. The chapter closes with a summary of Rehoboam's reign of seventeen years, and a verdict: he did evil, because he did not set his heart to seek the Lord.

=== 13
Rehoboam is dead, and his son Abijah becomes king of Judah. Kings, which calls him Abijam, gives him a few lines and a poor verdict. Chronicles gives him a whole chapter and one great speech, because his reign shows what the writer believes about the kingdom of David.

The war between north and south that began under Rehoboam has continued. Abijah's army faces Jeroboam's, which is twice its size, near Mount Zemaraim in the hill country of Ephraim, close to the border between the two kingdoms. Before the battle, Abijah climbs the mountain and calls out to Jeroboam and all Israel.

His speech sets out the case for Judah. The Lord gave the kingship to David and his descendants for ever by a covenant of salt, an unbreakable agreement. Jeroboam rebelled against his master. The north has golden calves, and it has driven out the true priests and replaced them with anyone who brings an offering. But in Jerusalem the sons of Aaron and the Levites still offer the sacrifices and tend the lamps. Do not fight against the Lord, Abijah cries, for you will not succeed. Meanwhile Jeroboam has sent troops around to ambush Judah from behind.

=== 14
Abijah has died, and his son Asa becomes king. The land has peace for ten years, and Chronicles explains why: Asa does what is good and right in the eyes of the Lord. He removes the foreign altars and the high places, breaks the sacred pillars and cuts down the Asherah poles, and he commands Judah to seek the Lord. The peace gives him time to build walled cities, and he tells the people that the land is still theirs because they have sought the Lord.

Then a great army comes up from the south, led by Zerah the Cushite. The name Cush usually refers to the lands south of Egypt, though this force may have come from Egypt or from peoples living in the southern desert; the historical details are uncertain. They reach Mareshah, in the lowlands south-west of Jerusalem.

Asa marches out and prays one of the short, urgent prayers that mark this book: there is no one besides you to help the powerless against the mighty. The battle and the pursuit that follow, as far as Gerar near the coast, are told in Chronicles alone. The next chapter will show what Asa does with his victory.
