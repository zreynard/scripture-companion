=== 76
The Asaph psalms continue, and this one is a song of victory. Its opening lines plant us firmly in Jerusalem, here called Salem, the ancient name it carried when Abram met Melchizedek, king of Salem, in Genesis. God has made his dwelling in Zion, and there he has broken the weapons of war.

The psalm dwells on the aftermath: warriors lying in their last sleep, horse and rider stunned into stillness at a single rebuke from the God of Jacob.

From that silent battlefield the poem widens to the whole earth. God rises to judge, not to crush the humble but to save them, and the kings and princes of the nations are summoned to bring him their tribute.

=== 77
Few psalms begin in deeper distress. The singer has cried out through the night with outstretched hands, and still his soul refuses to be comforted. Sleep will not come, and words will not come either. The heading links him with Asaph and with Jeduthun, another of David's chief musicians.

The first half of the psalm is a series of aching questions. Has the Lord rejected us for ever? Has his loving devotion come to an end? Has God forgotten to be gracious? No cause is given for the anguish.

Then there is a turn, and it comes through memory. The singer resolves to remember the works of the Lord, and his mind goes back to the oldest story Israel knew: the sea at the Exodus, the waters trembling, thunder and lightning overhead, a path through the deep where no footprints could be seen. The psalm ends quietly, with the people led like a flock by the hand of Moses and Aaron.

=== 78
Give ear, O my people, to my instruction. With those words Asaph gathers his hearers like a teacher, and what follows, a Maskil and the second longest psalm in the Psalter, is Israel's history set to music. His purpose is plain: what the fathers handed down must pass to children not yet born, so that they will trust God and not repeat their fathers' stubbornness.

The story he tells runs from the plagues in Egypt, in the region of Zoan, through the wilderness years with their manna, quail and water from the rock, and on into the land of Canaan. Its refrain is painful. God gives and gives again, and Israel forgets.

Two places frame the ending. Shiloh, where the tabernacle once stood, was abandoned, a memory of the dark days in the first book of Samuel when the ark was captured and the priests fell by the sword. Then God chose the tribe of Judah, Mount Zion, and a shepherd boy named David, taken from the sheepfolds to shepherd his people.

=== 79
Now the tone turns to grief. The nations have entered God's inheritance, defiled his temple and left Jerusalem a heap of rubble. The dead lie unburied around the city, food for birds and beasts, and Israel's neighbours look on with scorn.

Like the psalm of Asaph that mourned the burned sanctuary, this one never names the invader. It speaks instead for the captives who groan and those condemned to die, and it hears the taunt the nations fling at Israel: where is their God?

The prayer moves through the familiar shapes of lament. It asks how long the Lord's anger will burn, pleads for forgiveness of past sins, and calls for justice on the nations who have mocked God's name. Its closing lines reach beyond the ruins, to a people who will once again, as the sheep of his pasture, give thanks for ever.

=== 80
A refrain holds this psalm together, sounded three times and growing stronger each time: restore us, O God, make your face shine upon us, that we may be saved. The words echo the priestly blessing Aaron's sons spoke over Israel in Numbers.

God is addressed as the Shepherd of Israel, enthroned between the cherubim above the ark, and the tribes named before him are Ephraim, Benjamin and Manasseh. They are the tribes descended from Rachel, through her sons Joseph and Benjamin.

At its centre is an extended picture of Israel as a vine. God brought it out of Egypt, cleared the ground for it and planted it, and it spread from the mountains to the sea and the river. Now its walls are broken, passers-by strip its fruit and the boar from the forest tramples it. The question the psalm presses is simply why.

=== 81
This psalm belongs to a festival. The ram's horn is to sound at the New Moon and at the full moon on the day of the feast, and the call to music comes with tambourine, harp and lyre. Over it stands the name of Asaph, and the word Gittith, last heard over Psalm eight.

Then, unexpectedly, the singing stops and God himself speaks. In the voice of a prophet the psalm recalls the slavery in Egypt, the burden lifted from Israel's shoulders, the thunder at Sinai, and the testing at the waters of Meribah, where the people quarrelled with Moses for water.

The heart of the message is the first commandment, and the sorrow of a God whose people would not listen. Yet the closing lines hold out an offer: finest wheat, and honey from the rock.

=== 82
This short psalm opens on a heavenly courtroom. God takes his place in the divine assembly and passes judgment among those called gods. His charge against them is that they have judged unjustly, favoured the wicked and abandoned the weak, the fatherless and the poor.

They are called sons of the Most High, yet the sentence falls on them all the same: they will die like mortals and fall like any prince.

Jesus quoted this psalm in John's Gospel, in an argument with his opponents in Jerusalem. The psalm closes with a single line of prayer, asking God himself to rise and judge the earth, for every nation belongs to him.

=== 83
The last of the Asaph psalms is an urgent prayer against a gathering alliance. God is begged not to stay silent, for his enemies are plotting to wipe Israel out so completely that her name will no longer be remembered.

The conspirators are named one by one, a ring of peoples around Israel's borders: Edom and the Ishmaelites, Moab and the Hagrites, Gebal, Ammon and Amalek, Philistia and the people of Tyre, with Assyria lending its strength to Moab and Ammon, the descendants of Lot.

For its precedent the prayer reaches back to the book of Judges, to Deborah's victory over Sisera and Jabin at the River Kishon, and to Gideon's rout of the Midianite princes Oreb and Zeeb, and the kings Zebah and Zalmunna. Its final desire is that the nations will learn that the Lord alone is Most High over all the earth.

=== 84
The sons of Korah return, and this is one of their loveliest songs. Its subject is longing for the house of God. The singer envies even the sparrow and the swallow, who have found a place to nest near the altars of the Lord of Hosts.

The psalm has the feel of pilgrimage. It blesses those whose hearts are set on the road to Zion, and imagines them passing through the Valley of Baca, the valley of weeping, and finding it turned into springs by the autumn rains. They go from strength to strength until they stand before God.

A brief prayer follows for the king, called God's anointed and the people's shield. Then comes the line for which the psalm is best known: better is one day in your courts than a thousand elsewhere. The singer would rather keep the door of God's house than live comfortably in the tents of the wicked.

=== 85
This psalm looks backwards and forwards at once. It begins by remembering a time of restoration: the Lord showed favour to his land, brought Jacob back from captivity, forgave and covered his people's sin.

Yet the present is troubled. God's displeasure lingers, and the people ask whether God will be angry with them for ever, whether he will revive them again.

Then the singer falls silent to listen, and what he hears is a promise of peace. The closing lines are some of the most beautiful in the Psalter. Loving devotion and faithfulness meet, righteousness and peace kiss each other, faithfulness springs up from the ground and righteousness looks down from heaven, and the land yields its harvest.

=== 86
In the middle of Book Three, among the psalms of Asaph and the sons of Korah, stands a single prayer of David. It is the prayer of a man who calls himself poor and needy, surrounded by a band of ruthless and arrogant men who seek his life.

Much of its language is gathered from elsewhere in Scripture, especially earlier psalms, and at its heart is the great description of God first given to Moses on Mount Sinai in Exodus: compassionate and gracious, slow to anger, abounding in loving devotion and faithfulness.

The psalm moves between personal plea and wide horizons. David asks for an undivided heart to fear God's name, and in the same breath he looks to the day when all the nations God has made will come and bow before him.

=== 87
A short and unusual song in praise of Zion. The Lord has founded his city on the holy mountains, and he loves its gates more than all the dwellings of Jacob.

Then the Lord speaks, and his words are startling. He lists the nations that know him, and they are Israel's old rivals and oppressors. Rahab here is not the woman of Jericho but a poetic name for Egypt. Beside it stand Babylon, Philistia, Tyre and Cush, the land far up the Nile. Of each one it will be said, this one was born in Zion, as if the Lord were enrolling them in the register of his city's citizens.

Last come singers and pipers, and a line of pure delight in the city of God: all my springs of joy are in you.

=== 88
There is no darker psalm in the Bible. Its superscription is crowded: a song, a psalm of the sons of Korah, for the choirmaster, according to Mahalath Leannoth, and it names Heman the Ezrahite, whose name stands beside Ethan's in the first book of Kings among men famed for wisdom.

From beginning to end the psalm is a cry from the edge of death. The singer has been afflicted since his youth. He feels already counted among the dead, laid in the lowest pit, overwhelmed by God's wrath like waves. His friends have been taken from him, and he asks whether the dead can ever praise God or speak of his faithfulness in the grave.

Most laments turn towards hope before the end. This one does not. Its last line is darkness is my closest companion. And yet the singer never stops praying, day and night, morning after morning, to the Lord he still calls the God of his salvation.

=== 89
The last psalm of Book Three is a long meditation on God's covenant with David, attributed to Ethan the Ezrahite, named alongside Heman among the wise in the first book of Kings.

It begins in praise. The Lord's loving devotion and faithfulness are celebrated in the heavens and on earth, among the holy ones and over the raging sea. Then the psalm recalls in detail the promise God gave through the prophet Nathan in the second book of Samuel: David's line would be established for ever, his throne would endure like the sun and moon, and even if his sons sinned, God would discipline them but never withdraw his love.

Then the music breaks. God has spurned his anointed, the walls are down and the crown lies in the dust. The psalm wrestles with what looks like a broken promise, asking where the faithful love of old has gone. Book Three closes with a doxology: blessed be the Lord for ever, amen and amen.

=== 90
Book Four opens with the oldest voice in the Psalter. This is the only psalm attributed to Moses, called here, as at the close of Deuteronomy, the man of God. After the lament for David's fallen throne at the end of Book Three, the Psalter reaches back beyond the monarchy altogether, to the man who led Israel through the wilderness.

The psalm sets the eternity of God beside the brevity of human life. Before the mountains were born, God was God. To him a thousand years are like a day gone by or a watch in the night. Men and women, by contrast, spring up like grass in the morning and wither by evening. Seventy years are given, or eighty for the strong, and they pass quickly.

From that sober reckoning comes a prayer: that God would teach his people to number their days, satisfy them with his love each morning, and establish the work of their hands.

=== 91
No author is named for this psalm, and none is needed. It is a song of trust that moves between voices. It begins with the person who dwells in the shelter of the Most High, and at once comes the reply, I will say to the Lord, you are my refuge and my fortress. Then, in the closing lines, the voice is God's own, promising to deliver and honour the one who loves him.

The images come thick and fast: the fowler's snare, the shelter of wings and feathers, the arrow by day and the plague that stalks in darkness, thousands falling on every side while harm does not come near. The psalm piles up protection against every kind of danger.

The devil himself quoted this psalm. When he tempted Jesus, he set him on the pinnacle of the temple in Jerusalem, urged him to throw himself down, and cited its promise that the angels would lift him up so that his foot would not strike a stone.

=== 92
This is the only psalm in the collection marked for a particular day: a song for the Sabbath. Its opening sets the rhythm of a day given to God, with loving devotion proclaimed in the morning and faithfulness at night, to the music of the ten-stringed harp and the lyre.

The singer has been made glad by what God has done, and his gladness turns into reflection. The wicked may spring up like grass and seem to flourish for a time, but they are short-lived, and only a fool fails to see it. The Lord, by contrast, is exalted for ever.

The psalm closes with one of the Psalter's loveliest pictures of the righteous: flourishing like the palm tree, growing like a cedar of Lebanon, planted in the courts of God's house and still bearing fruit in old age.

=== 93
The Lord reigns. With those words begins a cluster of psalms in Book Four that celebrate God as King, and this short one sets the tone for them all.

The Lord is robed in majesty and armed with strength, his throne established from eternity, and because of that the world itself stands firm. Then the poem lifts its eyes to the sea. The floods have raised their voice, the breakers pound, the waters roar. In the ancient world the sea stood for chaos and threat, and the psalm lets us hear its noise before setting God above it all: mightier than the thunder of the great waters is the Lord on high.

It ends quietly, in the temple, with God's testimonies standing sure and holiness adorning his house for ever.

=== 94
Amid the kingship psalms comes a sharp cry for justice. God is called on as the God of vengeance and the Judge of the earth, because the wicked are crushing his people. They kill the widow, the foreigner and the fatherless, the very people the law of Moses was most careful to protect, and they tell themselves that the God of Jacob does not see.

The singer answers them with blunt common sense. He who planted the ear, does he not hear? He who formed the eye, does he not see?

The second half grows personal. The singer's foot was slipping, anxiety was overwhelming him, and only the Lord's loving devotion held him up. The psalm also asks whether a corrupt throne, one that frames injustice by decree, could ever be allied with God, and so names the oppressors as those in power. It closes in confidence that the Lord will be the singer's rock and bring the wicked to account.

=== 95
This psalm begins as a summons to worship and ends as a warning. In the first half the congregation calls itself to come before the Lord with joyful songs, for he is the great King above all gods. The depths of the earth and the mountain peaks are in his hand, the sea and the dry land are his work, and the people are the sheep of his pasture. So they are invited to bow down and kneel.

Then the mood changes. Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts. And God himself speaks, recalling Massah and Meribah, where Israel tested him in the wilderness, demanding water from the rock, and the forty years of that generation who never entered his rest.

The letter to the Hebrews takes up this psalm at length, pressing its word today on its own readers, and attributes it to David.

=== 96
Sing to the Lord a new song, and not Israel alone, but all the earth. This psalm carries the kingship theme out to the nations. The gods of the peoples are idols, but the Lord made the heavens, and the families of the earth are called to ascribe to him the glory due his name and to bring their offerings into his courts.

Much of this psalm also appears in the first book of Chronicles, in the song of thanksgiving given to Asaph and his fellow Levites on the day David brought the ark of the covenant up to Jerusalem.

Its ending is a burst of joy that reaches beyond humanity. The heavens are glad, the sea resounds, the fields exult and the trees of the forest sing, all for one reason: the Lord is coming to judge the world in righteousness and the peoples in his faithfulness.

=== 97
Once again the cry rings out: the Lord reigns. This time the King's arrival is pictured in the language of a great storm, or of Sinai itself. Clouds and thick darkness surround him, fire goes before him, lightning lights up the world, and the mountains melt like wax before the Lord of all the earth.

Against that blaze of glory the idols are put to shame, and those who boast in them are humbled. Zion hears and rejoices, and the towns of Judah are glad at his judgments.

The psalm then turns to those who love the Lord, and its tone softens. He guards the lives of his faithful ones, and light is sown for the righteous, gladness for the upright in heart.

=== 98
A close companion to the psalm two before it, this one also opens with a call to sing a new song, but here the reason is a victory already won. The Lord's right hand and holy arm have saved, and he has made his salvation known in the sight of the nations, remembering his love and faithfulness to the house of Israel.

Then the whole creation is summoned to the celebration. Lyre and harp, trumpets and the ram's horn sound before the Lord, the King. The sea roars, the rivers clap their hands and the mountains sing together for joy, because he comes to judge the earth.

English congregations know this psalm well, often without realising it. Isaac Watts drew on it for his hymn Joy to the World.

=== 99
The kingship psalms come to a climax with this one. The Lord reigns, enthroned above the cherubim, great in Zion and exalted over all the peoples, and the nations are told to tremble.

Like a refrain, one phrase returns three times: he is holy. The King's holiness is shown in his love of justice and the equity he has established in Jacob.

The psalm then does something unusual, naming figures from Israel's past. Moses and Aaron were among his priests, and Samuel among those who called on his name. They cried to the Lord and he answered them, speaking from the pillar of cloud. He forgave them, the psalm says, and yet he also dealt with their wrongdoing. The final call is to worship at his holy mountain, for the Lord our God is holy.

=== 100
A psalm of thanksgiving, short enough to learn by heart, and countless people have. All the earth is called to make a joyful noise to the Lord, to serve him with gladness and to come before him with singing.

The reasons are simple and deep. The Lord is God. He made us, and we are his, his people and the sheep of his pasture. So worshippers are invited to enter his gates with thanksgiving and his courts with praise, as pilgrims and festival crowds once did at the temple.

The closing lines gather up the faith of the whole Psalter: the Lord is good, his loving devotion endures for ever, and his faithfulness continues through all generations. In English churches this psalm has long been sung as All People That on Earth Do Dwell, to the tune known as the Old Hundredth.

=== 101
This psalm of David has the shape of a king's pledge. He begins by singing of loving devotion and justice, and then sets out how he means to live and rule.

At home, he will walk with integrity of heart and set nothing worthless before his eyes. At court, he will not tolerate the slanderer, the proud or the deceiver. Liars will not stand in his presence, but the faithful of the land will dwell with him and serve him.

And the pledge ends where a king's day begins. Every morning, he says, he will remove the wicked of the land, and cut off every evildoer from the city of the Lord.

=== 102
The superscription here names no author and no event, only a situation: the prayer of one who is afflicted, when he grows faint and pours out his complaint before the Lord. Church tradition counted it the fifth of the seven penitential psalms.

The first part is a portrait of desolation. The singer's days vanish like smoke, his bones burn, he forgets to eat, and he compares himself to an owl among ruins and a lonely bird on a rooftop. He eats ashes and mixes his drink with tears.

Then his gaze lifts from himself to the Lord enthroned for ever and to Zion, whose stones and dust her people still cherish. He expects the Lord to rebuild Zion and to hear the groans of prisoners. Its final lines contrast the heavens, which wear out like a garment, with God, who remains the same. The letter to the Hebrews applies those closing words to the Son.

=== 103
Bless the Lord, O my soul. With those words David speaks to his own heart, calling it not to forget all the Lord's benefits: forgiveness, healing, redemption from the pit, a life crowned with love and compassion.

The psalm widens from one man's experience to Israel's. God made known his ways to Moses, and the description he gave of himself at Sinai is heard again: compassionate and gracious, slow to anger, abounding in loving devotion. As high as the heavens are above the earth, so great is his love; as far as east is from west, so far has he removed our sins.

The fragility of human life is set beside God's mercy. We are dust, our days like grass, but his love is from everlasting to everlasting. The psalm ends by calling the angels, the heavenly hosts and all creation to bless the Lord, before returning to where it began.

=== 104
The previous psalm ended with the words that open this one, bless the Lord, O my soul, and the two are a pair. Where the last praised God's mercy to his people, this one praises his work in creation.

Its movement loosely follows the creation account of Genesis. God stretches out the heavens like a tent, sets the earth on its foundations and drives back the waters to their bounds. Then the poem lingers over the living world: springs in the valleys, wild donkeys drinking, birds singing in the branches, wine and oil and bread for people, storks in the cypresses, wild goats on the high mountains, lions roaring at night and men going out to work at sunrise. Out on the sea go the ships, and Leviathan, made to play there.

All creatures look to God for their food and their very breath. The psalm closes with the Psalter's first Hallelujah, which simply means praise the Lord.

=== 105
Book Four ends with two long psalms that tell Israel's history, and this is the first. Its opening verses also appear in the first book of Chronicles, as part of the song of thanks sung when David brought the ark to Jerusalem.

The psalm is a celebration of God's faithfulness to his covenant, the promise of the land of Canaan sworn to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. It follows the family when they were few and wandering, protected among kings. It tells of famine, and of Joseph sold as a slave, his feet bruised by shackles until the word of the Lord proved him right. Then come the years in Egypt, Moses and Aaron, the plagues, the departure with silver and gold, the cloud, the quail and the water from the rock.

What is striking is what this telling leaves out. There is almost no mention of Israel's failures here. That story is told in the psalm that follows.

=== 106
The second of the history psalms tells the same story from the other side. The previous psalm recounted God's faithfulness; this one confesses Israel's unfaithfulness. We have sinned like our fathers, the singer says, and then he shows how.

The record is long and honest: rebellion at the Red Sea, craving in the wilderness, envy of Moses and Aaron and the earth swallowing Dathan and Abiram, the golden calf at Horeb, refusal to enter the pleasant land, the worship of Baal of Peor, the bitterness at Meribah. Then, in Canaan, the people mingled with the nations and even sacrificed their sons and daughters to idols. Two men stand out as intercessors: Moses standing in the breach, and Phinehas, whose intervention stopped the plague.

Yet again and again God heard their cry and remembered his covenant. The final plea is the cry of a scattered people: save us, and gather us from the nations. Book Four closes with a blessing, and the people answering, Amen. Hallelujah.

=== 107
Here Book Five, the last division of the Psalter, begins. Book Four ended with the prayer, gather us from the nations, and this psalm opens as though that prayer has been answered. Let the redeemed of the Lord say so, those he has gathered from east and west, from north and south.

What follows is a set of four scenes, each a different kind of trouble. Some wander lost and starving in desert wastes. Some sit in darkness and chains because they rebelled against God's word. Some, through their own folly, lie sick and near the gates of death. Some go down to the sea in ships and are tossed by a storm until their skill is gone. Each scene turns on the same line: then they cried to the Lord in their trouble, and he delivered them. Each ends with the same call to give thanks.

The psalm closes with God reversing fortunes, turning rivers into desert and desert into springs.

=== 108
David's name heads a psalm built from two earlier ones. Its first half is taken, almost word for word, from the triumphant ending of Psalm fifty-seven; its second half comes from Psalm sixty. Here the pieces are joined into a new whole.

It begins at dawn, with a steadfast heart and harp and lyre called to wake the morning, and with praise of God's loving devotion, higher than the heavens. Then comes a prayer for rescue, and God answers from his sanctuary. He speaks as the owner of the land, parcelling out Shechem and the Valley of Succoth, claiming Gilead, Manasseh, Ephraim and Judah, and treating Israel's old enemies Moab, Edom and Philistia as servants at his feet.

The final lines return to the field of battle. Who will bring me to the fortified city, who will lead me to Edom? With God, comes the answer, we will perform with valour.

=== 109
Few psalms are harder to read aloud than this one. David has met love with hatred and kindness with false accusation, and he brings his accusers before God in some of the fiercest language in Scripture.

In the long central section the curses fall on a single figure: may his days be few, may another take his office, may his children be fatherless and his name blotted out. Every word of it is spoken to God, and David names the one who will repay: may this be the Lord's reward to my accusers.

In the book of Acts, Peter applied one of these lines to Judas, when the apostles chose someone to take his place. By its close David is weak, gaunt and mocked, yet trusting that the Lord stands at the right hand of the needy to save him.

=== 110
No passage of the Old Testament is quoted or echoed in the New more often than this short psalm of David. It is a royal psalm, and its opening line is an oracle: the Lord said to my Lord, sit at my right hand until I make your enemies a footstool for your feet.

The one addressed is a king, given a mighty sceptre from Zion and a willing people on the day of battle. Then comes an oath, stranger still, sworn by the Lord who will not change his mind. This king is also a priest for ever, in the order of Melchizedek, the priest-king of Salem who blessed Abram in Genesis.

Jesus himself raised this psalm in the temple courts, asking how David could call his own descendant Lord. Peter quoted it on the day of Pentecost, and the letter to the Hebrews builds a long argument on its priesthood. Its last lines see the king victorious over the nations.

=== 111
Psalms one hundred and eleven and one hundred and twelve are twins. Both open with Hallelujah, and both are acrostics: in Hebrew, each line begins with the next letter of the alphabet, twenty-two in all, so the poem marches from beginning to end in an ordered sequence.

This first twin is about God. The singer gives thanks with all his heart in the assembly of the upright, and he praises the Lord's works: great, majestic and righteous, worth pondering by all who delight in them. Behind the phrases lie the stories of the Exodus and the wilderness, the food provided, the covenant remembered, the land of the nations given as an inheritance.

The last line has become one of the best-known sayings of the wisdom tradition, echoed in Proverbs and Job: the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom.

=== 112
The second twin takes up where the first left off. Having ended with the fear of the Lord, the poem now describes the person who lives in it: blessed is the man who fears the Lord, who greatly delights in his commandments. It too is an acrostic, moving through the Hebrew alphabet line by line.

The two psalms answer each other. Phrases used of God in the first are now used of the faithful man. God's righteousness endures for ever; so does his. God is gracious and compassionate; so is he. The man is generous, lends freely, gives to the poor, and does not fear bad news, because his heart is steady and trusting.

Only in its last line does the psalm glance at the wicked, who see all this and grind their teeth in frustration.

=== 113
With this psalm begins a group of six, Psalms one hundred and thirteen to one hundred and eighteen, known in Jewish tradition as the Hallel, from the Hebrew word for praise. They came to be sung at the great festivals, above all at Passover, where the first part was sung before the meal and the rest after it. On the night of the Last Supper, the Gospels say, Jesus and his disciples sang a hymn before they went out to the Mount of Olives.

This opening song calls the servants of the Lord to praise his name from the rising of the sun to its setting. He is enthroned far above the nations and the heavens, so high that he must stoop to look at them.

Yet the God who is so exalted lifts the poor from the dust and the needy from the rubbish heap, and seats them with princes. He gives the barren woman a home and children, an echo of Hannah's song in the first book of Samuel.

=== 114
The second of the Hallel psalms is a short, vivid poem about the Exodus. When Israel came out of Egypt, it begins, Judah became God's sanctuary and Israel his kingdom.

The poem then compresses forty years into a few lines. The sea saw and fled, as it did when Israel crossed the Red Sea. The Jordan turned back, as it did when they entered the land under Joshua. And the mountains skipped like rams, the hills like lambs.

Then the singer turns, almost playfully, to question them. What made you flee, O sea, and skip, O mountains? The answer comes in the final lines: the earth trembles at the presence of the God of Jacob, who turned the rock into a pool and flint into a spring of water.

=== 115
Not to us, O Lord, not to us, but to your name give glory. The Hallel continues with a psalm that answers the taunt of the nations: where is their God? Israel's God is in heaven, and he does whatever he pleases.

The psalm then turns to the idols of the nations, and its mockery is pointed. They are silver and gold, made by human hands. They have mouths but cannot speak, eyes but cannot see, ears, noses, hands and feet, and none of them work. Those who make them, the psalm says, will become like them.

The second half has a liturgical shape. Three groups are named, Israel, the house of Aaron and those who fear the Lord, and each is called to trust him and promised his blessing. In its final lines it is the living, not the silent dead, who bless the Lord now and for ever.

=== 116
A personal song of thanksgiving within the Hallel. The singer loves the Lord because he has heard his cry. The ropes of death had wound around him and the anguish of the grave had taken hold, and in that extremity he called on the name of the Lord and was saved.

Now he tells his own soul to return to its rest, and he asks how he can repay the Lord for all his goodness. His answer is to lift the cup of salvation and fulfil his vows publicly, in the courts of the Lord's house in Jerusalem.

The psalm contains a line that has comforted mourners for centuries: precious in the sight of the Lord is the death of his saints. The apostle Paul quoted another of its lines in his second letter to the Corinthians: I believed, therefore I spoke.

=== 117
The shortest psalm, and the shortest chapter in the whole Bible, is only two verses long, yet its reach is the widest of all. It calls every nation and every people to praise the Lord.

The reason given is his great loving devotion towards Israel and his faithfulness, which endures for ever. What God has done for one people is to be cause for praise among all of them. Paul quoted this psalm in his letter to the Romans, as part of his case that the nations were always meant to glorify God for his mercy.

=== 118
The Hallel ends with a song of thanksgiving and victory, framed at both ends by the same words: give thanks to the Lord, for he is good; his loving devotion endures for ever. As earlier in the Hallel, Israel, the house of Aaron and all who fear the Lord are called to answer.

Then one voice speaks of terrible danger. All the nations surrounded him, swarming like bees, and he was pushed hard and falling, but the Lord helped him. Then the scene moves to the gates of righteousness. Open them, the singer asks, that I may enter and give thanks, and from the house of the Lord comes a blessing in reply.

Several of its lines became famous in the Gospels. The stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone; Jesus applied that to himself. O Lord, save us, is the cry the crowds took up as Hosanna, and with it, blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord, when Jesus rode into Jerusalem.

=== 119
This is the longest psalm, and the longest chapter in the Bible: one hundred and seventy-six verses, all devoted to a single subject, the word of God.

Its shape is the most elaborate acrostic in Scripture. The psalm is divided into twenty-two stanzas, one for each letter of the Hebrew alphabet, and every one of the eight verses in a stanza begins with that stanza's letter. Many Bibles print the letter names, Aleph, Beth, Gimel and the rest, above each section. It is as though the singer means to say everything about God's word from A to Z.

Nearly every verse uses one of a small circle of words, each looking at God's revelation from a slightly different angle: law, testimonies, precepts, statutes, commandments, judgments, word and promise.

Yet this is not a dry poem. It is addressed to God throughout, by a singer who calls himself a stranger on the earth, lowly and despised. Rulers slander and persecute him. He has been afflicted, and says it was good for him, because it taught him God's statutes.

Out of that pressure come some of the Psalter's best-known lines: your word is a lamp to my feet and a light to my path. The psalm does not build to a triumphant climax. It ends with a humble admission: I have strayed like a lost sheep; seek your servant.

=== 120
Here begin the Songs of Ascents, fifteen short psalms, from one hundred and twenty to one hundred and thirty-four, each carrying the same heading. Jerusalem stands high in the Judean hills, and anyone travelling there was said to go up. These are songs for pilgrims making that ascent to the festivals.

The series begins far from Zion. The singer lives among people of lying lips and deceitful tongues, and he cries out against them. Woe to me, he says, that I dwell in Meshech, that I live among the tents of Kedar. Meshech lay far to the north, towards the Black Sea, and Kedar was a desert tribe of Arabia, so the names are a way of saying he lives among strangers and enemies.

The psalm ends on a note of weary longing. He is for peace, but when he speaks, they are for war.

=== 121
The pilgrim lifts his eyes to the hills and asks where his help will come from. The answer follows at once: from the Lord, the Maker of heaven and earth.

The rest of the psalm reads like a blessing spoken over a traveller. One idea keeps returning: the Lord as the one who keeps, watches and guards. He will not let your foot slip on the path. He never dozes or sleeps. He is shade at your right hand, so that neither the sun by day nor the moon by night will strike you.

The closing promise is as wide as life itself. The Lord will watch over your going out and your coming in, now and for evermore.

=== 122
This Song of Ascents carries David's name, and it brings the pilgrims to their destination. I was glad when they said to me, let us go to the house of the Lord. Now their feet are standing within the gates of Jerusalem.

The city is admired as a place built compact and joined together, where the tribes of Israel go up to give thanks, as the law commanded them to do at the great feasts. It is also the seat of justice, where the thrones of the house of David stand.

Then comes a call to pray for the peace of Jerusalem. In Hebrew the words for peace, shalom, and for Jerusalem echo one another, and the closing lines play on the sound: peace within your walls, prosperity inside your fortresses, for the sake of the house of the Lord.

=== 123
One of the shortest Songs of Ascents, and one of the most intimate. The pilgrim has lifted his eyes to the hills; now he lifts them higher, to the One enthroned in heaven.

The central image comes from an ordinary household. As servants watch the hand of their master, and a maid watches the hand of her mistress, waiting for a sign, so the people keep their eyes on the Lord until he shows them mercy.

The reason they need mercy comes only at the end. They have had more than enough of contempt and scorn from the arrogant and the proud. Who those scornful people are is not said, and the psalm does not answer its plea within its own few lines.

=== 124
This Song of Ascents, attributed to David, is a song of escape, and it invites the whole people to join in: let Israel now say it. If the Lord had not been on our side when men rose up against us, they would have swallowed us alive.

The dangers are pictured as a sudden flood, the torrent rising and the raging waters sweeping over the nation's head. Then the image changes. Israel has escaped like a bird from the fowler's snare. The net is broken, and the bird is free.

No particular danger is named, and the psalm needs none. It ends with a line that became a familiar opening of worship: our help is in the name of the Lord, the Maker of heaven and earth.

=== 125
Psalm one hundred and twenty-five takes its picture from the landscape of Jerusalem. Those who trust in the Lord are like Mount Zion, which cannot be shaken. And as the mountains surround Jerusalem, so the Lord surrounds his people.

Anyone who has stood in the old city knows the picture. Jerusalem sits on its ridge with higher hills around it, the Mount of Olives to the east among them.

The psalm also carries a concern. The sceptre of wickedness, the rule of the unjust, must not rest for long on the land given to the righteous, lest they too be tempted to reach out their hands to do wrong. A short prayer follows, for good to the good and judgment on the crooked, and it ends with a benediction: peace be upon Israel.

=== 126
This psalm remembers a moment of joy so great it felt unreal. When the Lord restored the captives of Zion, they were like people dreaming. Their mouths were full of laughter, and even the nations said, the Lord has done great things for them.

Then the mood changes from memory to prayer. Restore our captives, O Lord, like streams in the Negev. The Negev is the dry country of southern Judah, where stream beds lie empty for most of the year until the winter rains fill them suddenly.

The closing picture comes from the farming year. Those who sow in tears will reap with songs of joy. The one who goes out weeping, carrying seed, will come home singing, carrying sheaves.

=== 127
This is the only Song of Ascents attributed to Solomon, and it begins with building. Unless the Lord builds the house, those who build it labour in vain. Unless the Lord guards the city, the watchman stays awake for nothing. And Solomon, named in the heading, was the builder of the temple.

Anxious toil, rising early and working late, is also in vain, for the Lord gives sleep to those he loves. Solomon himself, as a newborn, was given a second name through the prophet Nathan: Jedidiah, which means loved by the Lord.

The second half turns to children, called a heritage and a reward from the Lord. Sons born in a man's youth are like arrows in a warrior's hand, and the man with a full quiver will not be put to shame when he faces his opponents at the city gate, where disputes were settled.

=== 128
A companion to the psalm before it, this Song of Ascents paints a picture of a blessed household. Blessed is everyone who fears the Lord and walks in his ways.

The scene is a family meal. The man eats the fruit of his own labour. His wife is like a fruitful vine within the house, and his children sit around the table like young olive shoots, which grow up around the trunk of an old olive tree.

Then the blessing widens from the home to Zion and the whole nation. May the Lord bless you from Zion, may you see the prosperity of Jerusalem all your days, and may you live to see your children's children. And it closes with the same benediction heard a few songs earlier: peace be upon Israel.

=== 129
Like the earlier song that began if the Lord had not been on our side, this one calls Israel to speak with one voice: many a time they have afflicted me from my youth, let Israel now say. The nation looks back over a long history of oppression and declares that its enemies have never prevailed.

The image is harsh and memorable. Ploughmen have ploughed across Israel's back and made their furrows long, like the lashes of a whip. But the righteous Lord has cut the cords of the wicked, the ropes that harnessed the plough.

The rest of the psalm prays that those who hate Zion will wither like grass growing on a flat rooftop, sprouting in the thin soil and dying before anyone can harvest it, never hearing the greeting passers-by gave the reapers at harvest: the blessing of the Lord be upon you.

=== 130
Out of the depths I cry to you, O Lord. This is one of the best loved of all the psalms, and one of the seven the church came to call penitential. Its depths are not described, but as it unfolds it becomes clear that the weight upon the singer is his own sin.

If the Lord kept a record of iniquities, who could stand? But with him there is forgiveness. So the singer waits. His soul waits for the Lord more than watchmen wait for the morning, and the line is repeated, as if the long night is not yet over.

In its last lines the prayer of one person becomes a word to the whole people. O Israel, hope in the Lord, for with him is loving devotion and full redemption, and he himself will redeem Israel from all its iniquities.

=== 131
Only three verses long, this Song of Ascents attributed to David is a portrait of quiet trust. David says his heart is not proud and his eyes are not lifted too high. He does not concern himself with things too great or too wonderful for him.

Instead he has calmed and quieted his soul, and he describes it with a gentle picture: like a weaned child with its mother. The weaned child no longer cries to be fed, but rests contentedly in its mother's arms.

As the psalm before it did, this one ends by turning from the individual to the nation: O Israel, hope in the Lord, from this time forth and for evermore.

=== 132
The longest of the Songs of Ascents looks back to the day the ark of the covenant came to Jerusalem. It recalls David's vow that he would not rest until he had found a dwelling place for the Mighty One of Jacob.

The memory is of a search. We heard of it in Ephrathah, the district around Bethlehem, and found it in the fields of Jaar, that is Kiriath-jearim, where the ark had stayed for many years after the Philistines returned it. The second book of Samuel tells how David brought it up with rejoicing, and when Solomon dedicated the temple, the second book of Chronicles has him close his prayer with words drawn from this psalm.

Then David's oath to God is answered by God's oath to David, the promise of a son on his throne, and of Zion chosen as the Lord's resting place for ever. The final lines speak of a horn and a lamp for David's line, and a gleaming crown for God's anointed.

=== 133
A brief psalm of David in praise of unity. How good and pleasant it is when brothers live together in harmony.

Two images explain it, and both run downward. The first is the precious anointing oil poured on Aaron's head when he was ordained as high priest, running down over his beard and the collar of his robes. The second is the dew of Hermon, the high mountain in the far north, falling on the mountains of Zion.

Both blessings come down from above, and both come to rest on the people of God. It is there, the psalm says, on the mountains of Zion, that the Lord has commanded his blessing, life for evermore.

=== 134
The last of the Songs of Ascents is a blessing for the night. It calls on the servants of the Lord who serve by night in his house, keeping watch while the city sleeps, to lift their hands towards the sanctuary and bless the Lord.

In the final verse the blessing is returned. May the Lord, the Maker of heaven and earth, bless you from Zion. With that the pilgrim songs come to rest in the place they set out to reach.

=== 135
The Songs of Ascents ended with a call to the servants of the Lord who stand in his house, and this Hallelujah psalm takes up the same call: praise him, you servants of the Lord, who stand in the courts of the house of our God.

Much of it is woven from phrases found elsewhere in the Psalter and the law, like a hymn built out of well-loved lines. The Lord has chosen Jacob as his treasured possession. He rules sky and sea, sending clouds, lightning and wind from his storehouses. He struck the firstborn of Egypt and defeated Sihon king of the Amorites and Og king of Bashan, the two kings east of the Jordan whose defeat Numbers records, and gave their land to Israel.

The mockery of idols from an earlier psalm returns almost word for word. Then the houses of Israel, Aaron and Levi, and all who fear the Lord, are called to bless him from Zion.

=== 136
Every line of this psalm is answered by the same refrain, twenty-six times over: his loving devotion endures for ever. Jewish tradition came to call it the Great Hallel. The same refrain rings out elsewhere in the Old Testament, at the dedication of Solomon's temple, before Jehoshaphat's army in Chronicles, and when the foundation of the second temple was laid in Ezra's day.

The lines move through Israel's story in order. First comes creation: the heavens, the earth spread out on the waters, the sun and moon and stars. Then the Exodus: the firstborn of Egypt, the Red Sea divided, Pharaoh swept away. Then the wilderness, the victories over Sihon and Og, and the gift of the land.

At the very end the story becomes personal and present. He remembered us in our low estate, and he gives food to every creature. Give thanks to the God of heaven.

=== 137
The setting of this psalm is stated in its first line, and it is unmistakable. By the rivers of Babylon the exiles sat down and wept when they remembered Zion. Jerusalem had fallen to Nebuchadnezzar's army, the temple was burned, and thousands of Judeans had been carried off to Mesopotamia.

Their captors wanted entertainment. Sing us one of the songs of Zion, they said. But the exiles hung their harps on the willows, for they could not sing the Lord's song in a foreign land. Then one voice swears an oath: if I forget you, O Jerusalem, may my right hand cease to function.

The last lines are among the hardest in the Bible. They call on the Lord to remember the Edomites, Judah's kinsmen, who urged the destroyers on, and pronounce a terrible blessing on whoever repays Babylon for what it has done. The psalm leaves raw grief and longing for justice unsoftened.

=== 138
After the lament from exile, the Psalter gathers a final collection of psalms attributed to David, eight of them, ending with Psalm one hundred and forty-five. This first is a song of wholehearted thanksgiving.

David gives thanks before the gods, and he bows toward God's holy temple. He had called, and on that very day God answered and strengthened his soul.

From one man's gratitude the psalm reaches outward. All the kings of the earth will praise the Lord when they hear his words, for though he is exalted, he looks upon the lowly. The final lines are confident and humble at once: the Lord will fulfil his purpose for me; do not abandon the work of your hands.

=== 139
O Lord, you have searched me and known me. This psalm of David is one of the most personal meditations in the Bible on God's knowledge and presence.

It moves in widening circles. God knows when David sits down and rises up, and every word before it reaches his tongue. There is nowhere to go from God's presence: not the heavens, not the depths of Sheol, not the wings of the dawn or the farthest sea, not even darkness, which is as light to him. Then the psalm turns inward and backward, to the womb, where God knit David together and wrote all his days in his book before one of them came to be.

The ending surprises many readers. David turns fiercely against the bloodthirsty men who defy God, and then he turns the searching light on himself: search me, O God, and know my heart, and lead me in the way everlasting.

=== 140
A prayer of David for rescue from violent men. They plot evil in their hearts, stir up conflict all day long, and sharpen their tongues like serpents, with the venom of vipers on their lips. The apostle Paul quoted that line in his letter to the Romans, in his account of human sinfulness.

The enemies are hunters as well as snakes. The proud have hidden a trap, spread a net along the path and set snares in David's way. As so often in these laments, the danger comes as much through words, slander and lies, as through the sword. Against them David holds on to one confession: you are my God. The Lord has shielded his head in the day of battle.

The prayer that their schemes rebound on them is followed by a quiet conviction: the Lord upholds the cause of the poor and the rights of the needy, and the upright will dwell in his presence.

=== 141
I call upon you, O Lord; come quickly to me. David asks that his prayer be set before the Lord like incense, and his lifted hands like the evening offering, made at the tabernacle and later the temple as daylight faded.

The danger in this psalm is partly within. David asks God to set a guard over his mouth and keep watch at the door of his lips, and not to let his heart be drawn to evil or to the rich food at the table of wrongdoers. He would rather be struck by a righteous man's rebuke, which he calls a kindness and oil for his head.

In the middle come grim lines of rulers thrown from the cliffs and bones scattered at the mouth of Sheol. At the end David's eyes are on the Lord, and he prays to pass safely by while the wicked fall into their own nets.

=== 142
Like Psalm fifty-seven, this prayer is set in the cave, in the years when David was a fugitive from King Saul, hiding in the wild country of Judah with Saul's men on his trail. The heading calls it a Maskil, and a prayer.

It is the cry of a man at the end of his strength. He pours out his complaint before the Lord and lays all his trouble before him. His spirit is faint. Snares are laid on his path. He looks to his right, where a protector would stand, and there is no one. No one cares for his soul.

Yet he turns to the Lord as his refuge and his portion in the land of the living. He asks to be brought out of prison, so that he may praise God's name, and he looks ahead to a day when the righteous will gather around him.

=== 143
The last of the seven penitential psalms of church tradition is another prayer of David. It opens with a plea not for vindication but for mercy: do not bring your servant into judgment, for no one living is righteous before you. Paul echoes those words in Romans and Galatians.

The enemy has crushed David's life to the ground and made him dwell in darkness like the long dead. His spirit faints. In that place he does what other psalmists did before him. He remembers the days of old and the works of God's hands, and he stretches out his hands, his soul thirsting like a parched land.

The second half is a chain of short, urgent requests. Answer me quickly. Let me hear your loving devotion in the morning. Teach me the way I should walk. Teach me to do your will, and let your good Spirit lead me on level ground.

=== 144
Much of this psalm will sound familiar. It draws heavily on David's great song of victory in Psalm eighteen, which is also found in the second book of Samuel: the Lord as rock and fortress, the God who trains his hands for war, who parts the heavens and comes down with lightning and arrows. In the middle stands a line very like Psalm eight: O Lord, what is man, that you regard him?

The king prays to be rescued from foreigners whose words are lies and whose right hands are false, and he promises a new song on the ten-stringed harp to the God who gives victory to kings and rescues his servant David.

Then the psalm changes key and paints a picture of a nation at peace: sons like well-tended plants, daughters like carved corner pillars, barns full, flocks multiplying, no breach in the walls and no cry of distress in the streets.

=== 145
The last psalm attributed to David is the only one in the Psalter headed a psalm of praise. The Hebrew word for praise used there gave the whole book its Hebrew title, Tehillim, the Praises.

It is an acrostic, each verse beginning with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet, so that praise runs from the first letter to the last. One generation, David says, will commend God's works to the next.

David begins by exalting God as King, and the psalm is full of the kingdom: its glory, its splendour, its endurance through all generations. Once again the great description from Exodus returns: gracious and compassionate, slow to anger, abounding in loving devotion. The Lord's care reaches every creature; the eyes of all look to him, and he opens his hand. The last line calls every creature to bless his holy name for ever, and the five psalms that follow take up that call.

=== 146
The Psalter closes with five psalms of pure praise, and each one begins and ends with the same word: Hallelujah, praise the Lord. Together they form the closing doxology of Book Five and of the whole collection.

This first one begins with the singer addressing his own soul, and resolving to praise the Lord as long as he lives. Then comes a warning. Do not put your trust in princes, in mortal men who cannot save. When their breath departs, they return to the earth, and their plans perish that very day.

The contrast is the God of Jacob, the Maker of heaven, earth and sea, who keeps faith for ever. The psalm lists what he does: he gives justice to the oppressed and food to the hungry, sets prisoners free, opens the eyes of the blind, lifts up those bowed down, watches over the foreigner, the fatherless and the widow. The Lord reigns for ever, the psalm concludes, your God, O Zion.

=== 147
The second of the closing Hallelujah psalms moves between the vast and the tender. The Lord builds up Jerusalem and gathers the exiles of Israel. He heals the broken-hearted and binds up their wounds, and he also numbers the stars and calls each one by name.

Twice more the singer calls for praise. The second call looks to the sky and the hills: clouds and rain, grass on the hillsides, food for the animals and for the young ravens when they cry. The third turns to Jerusalem, her gates made strong and her children blessed, peace at her borders and the finest wheat, and then to winter, with snow like wool, frost like ashes and hail like pebbles, all melted at his word. Last of all comes the greatest gift. God has made his word known to Jacob, as he has done for no other nation.

=== 148
Here praise becomes a summons to the entire universe, and it comes in two great movements, first from the heavens and then from the earth.

From above, the call goes out to the angels and heavenly hosts, to sun and moon and shining stars, to the highest heavens and the waters above the skies. They are to praise the name of the Lord, because he commanded and they were created.

From below, the list runs from the great sea creatures and the ocean depths to lightning and hail, snow and wind, mountains and hills, fruit trees and cedars, wild animals and cattle, creeping things and birds. Then humanity: kings and peoples, princes and rulers, young men and young women, old and young together. The roll call loosely echoes the order of creation in Genesis.

The final word belongs to Israel, the people near to God, for whom he has raised up a horn, a symbol of strength.

=== 149
The penultimate psalm returns from the whole creation to the gathered people of God. Sing to the Lord a new song, in the assembly of the faithful. Let Israel rejoice in their Maker and Zion's children in their King, with dancing, tambourine and harp.

The Lord takes pleasure in his people and adorns the afflicted with salvation, and the faithful are told to exult even on their beds.

Then the psalm takes a martial turn. The high praises of God are in their mouths and a double-edged sword in their hands, to carry out judgment on the nations and bind their kings in chains. Psalm two had pictured the nations raging against the Lord and his anointed. Here, near the end of the book, the faithful share in the judgment written against them, and the psalm calls it an honour for all his saints.

=== 150
The final psalm of the Psalter is a doxology from beginning to end. It closes Book Five, and with it the entire collection of one hundred and fifty psalms.

Every line is a call to praise. Praise God in his sanctuary and in the mighty heavens. Praise him for his mighty deeds and his surpassing greatness. Then the instruments of Israel's worship are brought in, one after another: the ram's horn, harp and lyre, tambourine and dancing, strings and flute, and two kinds of cymbals, clashing and resounding.

The book began with a quiet blessing on the man who meditates on God's law day and night. It ends with a single command addressed to every living thing: let everything that has breath praise the Lord. Hallelujah.
