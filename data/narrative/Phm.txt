=== intro
Philemon is the shortest of Paul's letters, a single page written about a single person. It is also the most private. Most of Paul's letters deal with the life of whole churches. This one asks a friend a personal favour, and the favour concerns a slave.

Philemon was a believer, and evidently a man of some means, since a church met in his house. He lived in Colossae, a modest town in the Lycus valley of Phrygia, in the interior of the province of Asia, more than a hundred miles inland from Ephesus. Paul had never visited the church in Colossae, which seems to have been founded by his colleague Epaphras, but he knew Philemon personally, and hints that Philemon owed his faith to him. That meeting may have happened during Paul's long stay in Ephesus, when, Acts says, the message spread through the whole province. The letter also greets Apphia, perhaps Philemon's wife, and Archippus, who may have been their son and who held some ministry in the church.

The slave is Onesimus, a common slave name meaning useful. He belonged to Philemon, and for reasons the letter does not fully explain, he had left his master's household. The traditional view is that he ran away, possibly taking money or goods with him. Some have suggested that he went to Paul deliberately, as a friend of his master who might plead his case. Either way, he found his way to Paul, who was then in prison, and there he became a believer. Paul came to love him as a son, and found him a real help.

Where Paul was held is uncertain. The traditional answer is Rome, during the house arrest at the end of Acts. Others suggest Ephesus, which was much closer to Colossae, or less often Caesarea. Wherever it was, this letter belongs with the letter to the Colossians. Both name Timothy as co-sender, both mention many of the same companions, and Colossians says that Onesimus was travelling to Colossae with Tychicus, who carried that letter. So Onesimus probably returned home carrying this one.

Paul faced a delicate task. Under Roman law Philemon had full power over a slave who had left him, and a returning runaway could expect punishment. Paul could have pressed his authority as an apostle. Instead he writes warmly and with great tact, even some gentle humour, appealing to Philemon as a partner and brother. The letter's few paragraphs give a close view of how the gospel was reshaping relationships within a single household in the Roman world.

=== 1
The letter opens with Paul calling himself a prisoner of Christ Jesus, not an apostle, and greeting Philemon together with Apphia, Archippus and the church that meets in his house. Whatever Philemon decides, he will decide in front of his church.

Paul begins with thanks for Philemon's love, which has refreshed the hearts of the saints. Only then, carefully, does he reach his request, and he holds back Onesimus's name until he has called him his own child. Then comes the wordplay on his name: once useless to Philemon, he is now useful to them both.

Paul says plainly that he could give orders, and chooses to appeal instead. He offers to cover any debt himself, writing the promise in his own hand. The letter closes with a request for a guest room, because Paul hopes to be released, and with greetings from his companions, among them Mark and Luke, and Demas, who would one day desert him.
