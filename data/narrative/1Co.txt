=== intro
The letters of Paul are not arranged in the order he wrote them. They are set out roughly by length, longest first, which is why Romans comes before this letter although it was written later. First Corinthians takes us a few years further back, to the city of Ephesus, on the western coast of what is now Turkey, where Paul spent nearly three years during his third missionary journey. From there he wrote to a young church across the Aegean Sea that was giving him a great deal of concern.

Corinth stood on the narrow neck of land that joins mainland Greece to the Peloponnese, with a harbour on either side: Lechaeum facing west toward Italy, and Cenchrea facing east toward Asia. Ships' cargoes, and sometimes small ships themselves, were hauled across the isthmus rather than risk the long and dangerous voyage around the southern cape. The old Greek city had been destroyed by Roman armies two centuries earlier. Julius Caesar refounded it as a Roman colony about a hundred years before Paul arrived, and it grew quickly into the capital of the province of Achaia, busy, wealthy and ambitious. Many of its people were newcomers, among them freed slaves and their descendants, and fortunes could be made there. It was a city where status, eloquence and public honour mattered a great deal, and every two years the famous Isthmian Games were held close by.

Paul first came to Corinth on his second journey, travelling alone after a cool reception in Athens. Acts tells the story. He found work as a tentmaker with a Jewish couple, Aquila and Priscilla, recently expelled from Rome, and preached in the synagogue every Sabbath. When Silas and Timothy joined him from Macedonia, he gave himself fully to preaching. Opposition in the synagogue drove him next door, to the house of a God-fearing Gentile named Titus Justus, but the synagogue leader Crispus believed, and so did many Corinthians. Paul stayed a year and a half. When his opponents brought him before the Roman proconsul Gallio, the case was thrown out. An inscription found at Delphi places Gallio in office around the year fifty-one, which makes this one of the most securely dated moments in Paul's life.

After Paul left, the eloquent teacher Apollos, from Alexandria in Egypt, came to Corinth and was greatly valued there. Paul meanwhile settled in Ephesus. News from Corinth reached him from several directions. He had already written them a letter, now lost, which they had partly misunderstood. Members of the household of a woman named Chloe brought word of quarrels in the church. And the Corinthians themselves wrote to him with a list of questions, perhaps carried by three of their members, Stephanas, Fortunatus and Achaicus, who were with Paul when he replied. First Corinthians is his answer to all of this, written in the middle fifties of the first century, some months before the feast of Pentecost. Sosthenes joins Paul in the greeting; he may be the synagogue leader of that name in Corinth whom Acts mentions, though we cannot be sure.

The church Paul addresses was small, perhaps no more than a few dozen people at first, meeting in private homes. Most were Gentiles, though some were Jews. Not many were wealthy or well born, Paul reminds them, but some plainly were, and the differences between rich and poor showed up even at the Lord's Supper. They were enthusiastic and richly gifted, proud of their knowledge and their spiritual experiences, and they had carried many of the habits of their city into the church: rivalry, admiration for clever speakers, lawsuits, and an easy acceptance of pagan feasts and sexual freedom.

The letter is practical from start to finish, and it follows the problems as they came to Paul. He begins with the factions that have formed around the names of their teachers, and with the message of the cross, which turns the world's ideas of wisdom and power upside down. He then deals with reports of serious wrongdoing in the church. From chapter seven he takes up their own questions one by one: marriage, food offered to idols, conduct in worship, and spiritual gifts. In the middle of this comes his famous chapter on love. Near the end he turns to the resurrection of the dead, which some in Corinth were denying, and he closes with the collection for Jerusalem and his travel plans.

The story did not end with this letter. Paul's relationship with Corinth went through a painful time in the months that followed, and Second Corinthians will tell what happened next.

=== 1
Paul writes from Ephesus, with Sosthenes alongside him, to the church of God in Corinth. Even in his greeting he reminds them that they belong to a wider fellowship, all those everywhere who call on the name of the Lord Jesus. Then he gives thanks for them. They have been richly gifted in speech and knowledge, and they lack no spiritual gift. Those are exactly the areas in which the letter will find most to correct.

Paul comes straight to the first problem. Members of Chloe's household have told him that the church is quarrelling and dividing into parties, each attached to a name: Paul, Apollos, Cephas, which is the Aramaic name of Peter, and even Christ. In a city that prized polished public speakers and gathered around favourite teachers as followers once gathered around philosophers, this may not have seemed strange to them.

Paul's response goes to the root. He sets the wisdom the Corinthians admire beside the message of a crucified Messiah, which looked like weakness and folly to almost everyone in their world. He reminds them who they were when God called them, and he quotes the prophets Isaiah and Jeremiah along the way.

=== 2
Paul has set the message of the cross against the wisdom of the world, and reminded the Corinthians that not many of them were wise or powerful by worldly standards when God called them. Now he turns to his own example, and to the time, some years earlier, when he first arrived in their city.

Acts tells us that Paul came to Corinth from Athens, where he had debated with philosophers and seen only a modest response. Here he remembers that arrival from the inside. He did not come with impressive eloquence, he says, but in weakness and fear and much trembling, determined to speak of nothing except Jesus Christ and him crucified. In a culture that judged teachers by their rhetorical polish, this was a deliberate choice.

Yet Paul does not dismiss wisdom altogether. In the second half of the chapter he speaks of a hidden wisdom of God, which the rulers of this age did not understand, or they would never have crucified the Lord of glory. It is revealed only by the Spirit of God, and Paul draws a contrast between those who have the Spirit and those who do not.

=== 3
The last chapter ended with the spiritual person, who receives what the Spirit of God reveals. Paul now turns that thought back on the Corinthians, and it is not a compliment. They pride themselves on their spiritual maturity, but Paul says he could not speak to them as spiritual people, only as infants. The proof is their jealousy and quarrelling.

He returns to the factions of chapter one, focusing now on himself and Apollos. Using a series of pictures, a field and a building, he explains what teachers really are. Paul planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth. Paul laid the foundation in Corinth, and others are building on it, and every builder's work will one day be tested.

Then the picture deepens. The church itself, Paul says, is God's temple, and the Spirit of God lives within it. Corinth was a city full of temples, and the warning that follows is meant to be taken seriously. The chapter ends by turning the slogans of the factions upside down: the Corinthians do not belong to their teachers, because all things belong to them, and they belong to Christ.

=== 4
Paul is still dealing with the parties in Corinth, and with the habit of ranking teachers against one another. He now describes how the Corinthians should regard him and Apollos: as servants of Christ and stewards entrusted with God's mysteries. What matters in a steward is faithfulness, and Paul leaves the final verdict on his own work to the Lord.

Then his tone sharpens. With heavy irony he contrasts the Corinthians, who act as if they already reign like kings, with the apostles, who live like condemned prisoners led at the end of a victory procession. Paul knew such hardship first hand: hunger, homelessness, beatings, and the manual labour that a cultured Corinthian might look down on, for he supported himself as a tentmaker.

The chapter ends on a more personal note. Paul speaks as their father in the faith, since he first brought them the gospel. He is sending Timothy, his trusted co-worker, whom he calls his beloved child, to remind them of his ways. And he warns those who have grown arrogant that he intends to come himself.

=== 5
With the question of factions addressed, Paul turns to something reported to him that he cannot pass over. A man in the church is living with his father's wife, presumably his stepmother. This was forbidden by the law of Moses, and Paul says it would shock even the pagans of Corinth, whose own law prohibited such unions.

What troubles Paul as much as the act is the church's response. Rather than grieving, they are proud, perhaps believing that their new freedom placed them beyond ordinary moral limits. Paul instructs them, when they gather, to remove the man from their fellowship, in the hope that he will ultimately be saved. He draws on the imagery of Passover, when every trace of yeast was swept out of Jewish homes, and calls Christ the Passover lamb.

The chapter also clarifies something Paul wrote in an earlier letter, now lost. He had told them not to associate with immoral people, and they had apparently understood him to mean the whole city. He explains that he meant those within the church who call themselves believers, not outsiders.

=== 6
A church that would not judge a scandal in its own ranks has, it turns out, been eager to go to law. That is the next failure Paul addresses. The Corinthian believers have been taking one another to court, before pagan judges.

In Roman Corinth, lawsuits were often a contest of status, and the wealthy and well connected usually had the advantage. Paul is appalled that believers who will one day share in judging the world cannot settle everyday disputes among themselves. Better to be wronged, he says, than to bring such cases before outsiders.

From there he reminds them what kind of people they used to be, and what God has done for them since. In the last part of the chapter he takes up slogans that some in Corinth seem to have been repeating, such as everything is permissible for me, and food for the stomach and the stomach for food. Paul answers them by turning to the body, which the Lord will raise, and to the use of prostitutes, which some apparently regarded as no more significant than a meal.

=== 7
Up to this point Paul has been responding to reports that reached him about Corinth. Now he begins to answer the letter the Corinthians had written to him. He signals it clearly: now for the matters you wrote about. The same phrase, now about, will mark each new subject for several chapters.

The first question concerns marriage and sexual relations. Some in Corinth seem to have concluded that a truly spiritual person should abstain altogether, even within marriage. The opening line of the chapter may well be their own slogan, which Paul quotes before qualifying it. He goes on to address married couples, the unmarried and widows, believers married to unbelievers, and those who are engaged.

Along the way he widens the discussion to other life situations, including circumcision and slavery, and urges each person to serve God in the place where they were called. Paul is careful to distinguish what he passes on as a command of the Lord from his own judgment. He also speaks of a present crisis and of the shortness of the time, which colour his advice throughout.

=== 8
Paul now takes up the second subject from the Corinthians' letter: food sacrificed to idols. In a city like Corinth this was an everyday matter. Animals were sacrificed in the many temples, and the meat was eaten at feasts in temple dining rooms, served at private dinners, or sold in the market. Social and business life often involved meals of this kind.

Some Corinthian believers argued from their knowledge. An idol is nothing, they said, and there is only one God, so the food is ordinary food. Paul agrees with their theology, and he restates the belief in one God that every Jew confessed, now including Jesus Christ as the one Lord. But he challenges how they are using their knowledge. Knowledge puffs up, he says, but love builds up.

His concern is for believers whose consciences are weak, who cannot eat such food without feeling they are taking part in idol worship. If they see others eating in an idol's temple, they may be drawn back into the old life. This discussion will continue for three chapters.

=== 9
At first glance this chapter seems to leave the subject of food offered to idols. In fact it is an illustration from Paul's own life. He has just said that he would give up meat altogether rather than cause a fellow believer to stumble. Now he shows that giving up his rights for the sake of others is the pattern of his whole ministry.

Paul is an apostle, and he has the right to be supported by the churches he serves, as Cephas and the other apostles and the brothers of the Lord are. He backs this up with examples from everyday life and from the law of Moses. But in Corinth he chose not to use that right, working with his own hands to preach the gospel free of charge. Some in Corinth seem to have questioned his authority on exactly this point.

The chapter ends with images drawn from athletics, familiar to anyone in Corinth, the host city of the Isthmian Games. Paul pictures the runner pressing toward the prize and the boxer in training, disciplining himself for a crown that will not fade.

=== 10
Chapter nine closed with Paul disciplining himself like an athlete, so that he would not be disqualified. Now he presses that warning home, and the story he chooses is Israel in the wilderness.

The Israelites who left Egypt with Moses all passed through the sea and all ate and drank what God provided. Yet many of them fell into idolatry and immorality and died in the desert. Paul presents these events as a warning to the Corinthians, who might think their own experience of God made them safe.

Then he returns directly to the question of idol food, now with sharper focus. Taking part in a feast at a pagan temple, he argues, is a real participation in something, just as sharing in the Lord's Supper is a participation in Christ. The two tables cannot both be shared. The chapter closes with practical guidance for ordinary situations, such as buying meat in the market or eating at an unbeliever's home.

=== 11
The long discussion of food offered to idols is over, and Paul now turns to the gatherings of the church for worship. This section runs for four chapters.

The first matter concerns men and women praying and prophesying in the assembly, and in particular whether women should cover their heads. In the ancient world, head coverings and hairstyles carried social meanings about honour, modesty and marital status, and those meanings are not always clear to readers today. Paul's argument draws on creation, on custom and on the practice of the other churches.

The second matter troubles him far more. When the Corinthians come together for the Lord's Supper, which was then part of a shared meal, some are going ahead with their own food while others go hungry. Wealthier members, free to arrive early and accustomed to the dining customs of the Roman world, may have been eating apart from poorer members and slaves, who came later from work. In response Paul repeats the words of Jesus on the night he was betrayed, which he had already passed on to them. It is probably the oldest written account of the Lord's Supper that has come down to us.

=== 12
The Corinthians' letter had raised a further question, about spiritual gifts, and Paul now turns to it. This subject will occupy him for three chapters, and it was clearly a source of pride and tension in the church.

The Corinthians valued spectacular gifts, especially speaking in tongues, and some seem to have regarded those who had them as more spiritual than others. Paul begins by reminding them of their pagan past, when they were led astray by idols, and he gives a simple test: no one can say Jesus is Lord except by the Holy Spirit.

Then he describes the variety of gifts, all given by the same Spirit for the common good. To make the point vivid he uses an image well known in ancient political speeches: the body, made up of many parts, each one necessary. The Corinthians had been treating their differences as grounds for ranking one another. Paul insists that every part belongs and that the parts that seem weaker are indispensable. The chapter ends with a promise to show them a more excellent way.

=== 13
At the end of the last chapter Paul promised to show the Corinthians the most excellent way. This short chapter is that way, and it has become one of the best known passages ever written.

It is often read at weddings, but in its setting it is addressed to a church divided by rivalry, pride and competition over spiritual gifts. Paul has just been describing those gifts, and he begins by setting the most impressive of them beside love: tongues, prophecy, knowledge, faith that moves mountains, even giving away everything one has. Without love, he says, none of it counts for anything.

Then he describes what love is like, in a series of short phrases. Many of them quietly answer the faults the letter has already exposed in Corinth: envy, boasting, pride, rudeness and self-seeking. Finally Paul looks ahead to the end of all things, when the gifts the Corinthians prize will pass away, and love will remain.

=== 14
Having described love, Paul returns to the practical question of spiritual gifts in the gatherings of the church. He now compares two gifts in particular: speaking in tongues and prophecy.

Speaking in tongues, Paul explains, builds up the speaker, but unless it is interpreted, no one else can understand it. Prophecy speaks intelligibly to the whole church. So he urges the Corinthians to seek the gifts that build up others. He draws illustrations from musical instruments, from a trumpet call in battle, and from the experience of hearing a foreign language, and he imagines an outsider walking into one of their meetings and concluding that they have lost their minds.

The chapter then gives guidelines for orderly worship, limiting how many may speak and insisting that they speak in turn. It includes instructions about women speaking in the assembly, which interpreters have long discussed, especially alongside the earlier passage in chapter eleven where women pray and prophesy. Paul's overarching concern is stated plainly at the close: everything must be done in a fitting and orderly way.

=== 15
Paul now comes to the last great subject of the letter, and he saves it for near the end. Some in the Corinthian church were saying that there is no resurrection of the dead. Many Greeks thought of the body as something to be left behind, and the idea of a dead body being raised could sound absurd.

Paul begins with the gospel he first preached in Corinth, which he himself had received. He sets it out in a short summary that many scholars believe was already a fixed form of words within a few years of the crucifixion: Christ died for our sins, was buried, and was raised on the third day. Then he lists those to whom the risen Christ appeared, including Cephas, the Twelve, more than five hundred people at once, most of them still living, James the brother of the Lord, and finally Paul himself.

From that foundation he argues that if Christ has been raised, the dead will be raised too. He answers the question of what kind of body the dead will have, drawing on the image of a seed sown in the ground, and the chapter rises to a triumphant close over death itself.

=== 16
The great argument about the resurrection is finished, and Paul ends with practical matters and personal news.

The first is the collection for the believers in Jerusalem, a project that mattered greatly to Paul, and that we last heard about at the close of Romans, written a little later. He has given the same instructions to the churches of Galatia: on the first day of each week, the Corinthians are to set money aside, so that it will be ready when he comes.

Then he shares his plans. He will stay in Ephesus until Pentecost, because a great door for effective work has opened there, though there are many opponents. After that he will travel through Macedonia and hopes to spend the winter in Corinth. He commends Timothy, who may arrive soon, and explains that Apollos is unwilling to come just now. He praises Stephanas, the first convert in their province, who has come with Fortunatus and Achaicus.

Greetings follow from the churches of Asia, and from Aquila and Prisca and the church in their house. Paul adds the final lines in his own hand.
