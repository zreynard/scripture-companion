=== intro
Amos is among the earliest of the prophets whose words were gathered into a book of their own. He came from Tekoa, a village on the edge of the Judean wilderness about ten miles south of Jerusalem, and he was not a professional prophet. He kept flocks and tended sycamore-fig trees, which grew in the warmer lowlands beyond his village. Yet the Lord sent him north, across the border, to preach to the kingdom of Israel.

The book dates his words to the days of Uzziah king of Judah and Jeroboam son of Jehoash king of Israel, two years before the earthquake. That earthquake was remembered for centuries; the prophet Zechariah could still mention it more than two hundred years later, and archaeologists have found signs of severe damage at several sites from around the middle of the eighth century before Christ. Amos preached in the years around seven hundred and sixty.

It was a time of confidence. Under Jeroboam the north had recovered the territory it had lost to the Arameans of Damascus, from Lebo-hamath in the far north to the Dead Sea, as the second book of Kings records. Assyria, the great power to the east, was for a time occupied with troubles closer to home, and Israel's neighbours were weak. In Samaria the wealthy built winter houses and summer houses and furnished them with ivory. Carved ivory panels from this period have been found in the ruins of the city. The shrines at Bethel, Gilgal and Beersheba were busy with sacrifices, tithes and festivals, and people looked forward to the Day of the Lord as a day of victory.

Amos saw what that prosperity had been built on. The poor were sold for silver and the needy for a pair of sandals. Judges took bribes in the gate, the place where justice was done. Merchants used false weights and could hardly wait for the Sabbath to be over so that they could get back to trading. And all this went on alongside lavish religious devotion. Amos's message is that the Lord who brought Israel out of Egypt will not accept worship from people who trample the weak, and that the Day of the Lord they longed for would be darkness, not light.

Amos never names Assyria as the enemy to come. He speaks only of a nation the Lord will raise up and of exile beyond Damascus. Within forty years of his preaching, that is what happened. Assyria revived under Tiglath-pileser, the northern kingdom was dismembered, and Samaria fell in about seven hundred and twenty-two before Christ. Amos was a near contemporary of Hosea, who was a man of the north and spoke a little later; the listener has already heard Hosea's very different voice describing the same society. In Judah, Isaiah and Micah were soon to begin.

The book is carefully shaped. It opens with a series of judgements on Israel's neighbours, each beginning with the same formula, which closes like a trap on Israel itself. Then come three chapters of oracles addressed to Samaria and its leaders, sharp, ironic and often bitterly funny. The last part is built around five visions, interrupted by the one story in the book, Amos's confrontation with the priest of Bethel. The final lines, after so much warning, look beyond the coming disaster to the rebuilding of the fallen house of David.

Amos's language is drawn from the life he knew: lions roaring over their prey, a shepherd rescuing two legs and a piece of an ear from a lion's mouth, a cart creaking under a load of sheaves, a basket of summer fruit. He was remembered long after the kingdom he warned had gone, and he is quoted twice in the book of Acts.

=== 1
After a single line introducing the shepherd from Tekoa, the first word comes like thunder: the Lord roars from Zion. The voice is from Jerusalem, and its effect is felt as far as the summit of Mount Carmel in the north.

What follows is a sequence of judgements on the nations around Israel. Each opens with the same formula: for three transgressions, even four, I will not revoke my judgement. It is a way of saying that the measure is more than full. Damascus is first, the capital of the Arameans, whose kings Hazael and Ben-hadad had ravaged Israel's lands in Gilead, east of the Jordan. Then come Gaza of the Philistines and Tyre, the great Phoenician port, both accused of handing whole communities over to slavery. Edom follows, and then Ammon, condemned for ripping open the pregnant women of Gilead, and the sequence runs on into the next chapter.

The crimes named are not offences against Israel's law, which these nations did not have, but acts of cruelty that any people should have recognised as evil. So far, every blow falls on Israel's enemies.

=== 2
The sequence of oracles continues. Moab is condemned for burning the bones of Edom's king, a desecration that had nothing to do with Israel. The Lord judges cruelty wherever it is found.

Then the circle tightens. Judah is next, Amos's own people, condemned not for brutality but for rejecting the Law of the Lord. Seven nations have now been named, and seven was the number of completeness. But there is an eighth, and it is Israel.

The formula is the same, but the charges are longer than all the others, and they are about everyday life: the poor sold for silver, the needy for a pair of sandals, garments taken in pledge from the poor and spread out beside the altars. Against this Amos sets what the Lord has done for them, from the exodus and the wilderness to the defeat of the Amorites. The chapter ends with an army in flight, its bravest warriors running away naked.

=== 3
Amos now addresses the whole family the Lord brought out of Egypt, and their privilege does not protect them. You only have I known of all the families of the earth, the Lord says, and therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities.

Then he defends his right to speak, with a string of questions from the countryside. A lion does not roar without prey, and a trap does not spring with nothing in it. The lion has roared, and so the prophet must speak.

The next oracle invites foreign witnesses, the people of Ashdod in Philistia and of Egypt, to gather on the hills around Samaria and see the oppression inside its walls. An enemy will surround the land. As a shepherd might rescue from a lion only two legs or a piece of an ear, so only fragments of Samaria's luxury will be saved. The chapter ends at Bethel, where the horns of the altar will be cut off, and among the ivory houses of the rich.

=== 4
This chapter opens with an insult that Amos's hearers could not have missed. He calls the wealthy women of Samaria cows of Bashan, after the rich pasture lands east of the Sea of Galilee, famous for their sleek, well-fed cattle. They oppress the poor and call to their husbands for more to drink. The day is coming when they will be dragged away with hooks through broken walls.

Then Amos turns to the shrines, sounding like a priest calling worshippers to the altar, but with heavy irony: go to Bethel and transgress, go to Gilgal and sin even more. The people love their sacrifices and tithes and like to be seen offering them.

The rest of the chapter recalls disasters the Lord has already sent: famine, drought, blight and locusts, plague and war, a catastrophe like Sodom's. After each comes the same refrain: yet you did not return to me. Finally comes a stark summons, prepare to meet your God, followed by a short hymn to the Creator, one of several in the book.

=== 5
Amos now takes up a lament, the kind of song sung at a funeral. Fallen is Virgin Israel, never to rise again. And he sings this dirge over a nation that is still prospering.

Yet within the lament is an invitation. Seek me and live, the Lord says, and do not seek Bethel, Gilgal or Beersheba, the shrines where the people sought him so busily. Beersheba lay in the far south of Judah, so northern pilgrims crossed the border to worship there. Amos turns again to the gate, where bribes are taken and the poor are robbed of justice. Seek good and not evil, he says, and perhaps the Lord will be gracious to the remnant of Joseph.

Then he turns the people's hopes upside down. They long for the Day of the Lord, expecting victory, but it will be darkness and not light. The Lord hates their feasts and will not hear their songs. Let justice roll on like a river, he says, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream. The chapter's final word is exile, beyond Damascus.

=== 6
The woes continue, now aimed at the leaders of both capitals, those at ease in Zion and those who feel secure on the hill of Samaria. They are the notable men of the foremost nation.

Amos sends them on an imaginary tour, to Calneh, to great Hamath in the north and down to Gath of the Philistines, and asks whether Israel is any better than these kingdoms. Then he paints a banquet among the elite of Samaria. They lie on beds inlaid with ivory, dine on lambs and fattened calves, sing to the harp, drink wine by the bowlful and anoint themselves with the finest oils. But they do not grieve over the ruin of Joseph. So they will be the first to go into exile.

There is a grim scene of a house emptied by death, and a mocking reference to two recent victories east of the Jordan. Lo-debar sounds in Hebrew like nothing, and Karnaim means horns, a symbol of strength. Finally, a nation will oppress Israel from Lebo-hamath to the Brook of the Arabah, the very borders Jeroboam had restored.

=== 7
Now come the visions, told in Amos's own words. The first two show judgements about to fall: a swarm of locusts just after the king's harvest has been gathered, and a consuming fire. Each time Amos pleads, how will Jacob survive, since he is so small, and each time the Lord relents.

The third is different. The Lord stands by a wall with a plumb line, the builder's tool for testing whether a wall is true. Israel has been measured, and this time there is no reprieve. The sanctuaries will be laid waste, and the Lord will rise against the house of Jeroboam with the sword.

Then comes the only story in the book. Amaziah, priest of Bethel, reports to King Jeroboam that Amos is conspiring against him, and orders the prophet back to Judah to earn his living there. Bethel, he says, is the king's sanctuary. Amos replies that he was no prophet, nor a prophet's son, but a herdsman whom the Lord took from following the flock. Then he speaks a final word to Amaziah himself.

=== 8
The visions resume with the fourth: a basket of summer fruit. The meaning turns on a pun. The Hebrew word for summer fruit, qayits, sounds like the word for end, qets. The end has come for my people Israel, the Lord says, and the songs of the temple will become wailing.

Amos then lets us overhear the merchants. They chafe at the New Moon and the Sabbath, the days when trade had to stop, and plan to shrink the measure, raise the price, rig the scales and sell even the chaff with the wheat. They will buy the poor for silver and the needy for a pair of sandals.

The Lord swears he will never forget, and the language becomes cosmic: the land heaving like the Nile in flood, the sun going down at noon, feasts turned into mourning as for an only son. And a different kind of famine is coming, not of bread or water, but of hearing the words of the Lord.

=== 9
The fifth and last vision is the most terrible. Amos sees the Lord standing beside the altar, commanding that the sanctuary be struck until it falls on the worshippers. There will be no escape, to Sheol or to heaven, to the top of Carmel or the bottom of the sea.

Israel assumed that its rescue from Egypt made it safe. The Lord's reply is startling. Are you not like the distant Cushites to me, he asks. He brought up the Philistines from Caphtor and the Arameans from Kir as well. Yet the house of Jacob will not be utterly destroyed. It will be shaken like grain in a sieve, and the sinners will fall.

Then, in the last verses, the book changes tone. The Lord will restore the fallen tent of David, the royal house pictured as a ruined shelter, and bring his people home. These lines are the book's final word, and James would quote them in Jerusalem, when the early church weighed the place of the Gentiles.
