=== intro
Amos is probably the earliest of the prophets whose words were gathered into a book of their own. He came from Tekoa, a village on the edge of the Judean wilderness about ten miles south of Jerusalem, and he was not a professional prophet. He kept flocks and tended sycamore-fig trees, work that took him down into the lowlands. Yet the Lord sent him north, across the border, to preach to the kingdom of Israel.

The book dates his words to the days of Uzziah king of Judah and Jeroboam son of Jehoash king of Israel, two years before the earthquake. That earthquake was remembered for centuries; the prophet Zechariah could still mention it more than two hundred years later, and archaeologists have found signs of severe damage at several sites from around the middle of the eighth century before Christ. Amos's ministry probably falls in the years around seven hundred and sixty, and it may have been brief.

It was a time of confidence. Under Jeroboam the north had recovered the territory it had lost to the Arameans of Damascus, from Lebo-hamath in the far north to the Dead Sea, as the second book of Kings records. Assyria, the great power to the east, was for a time occupied with troubles closer to home, and Israel's neighbours were weak. In Samaria the wealthy built winter houses and summer houses and furnished them with ivory. Carved ivory panels from this period have been found in the ruins of the city. The shrines at Bethel, Gilgal and Beersheba were busy with sacrifices, tithes and festivals, and people looked forward to the Day of the Lord as a day of victory.

Amos saw what that prosperity had been built on. The poor were sold for silver and the needy for a pair of sandals. Judges took bribes in the gate, the place where justice was done. Merchants used false weights and could hardly wait for the Sabbath to be over so that they could get back to trading. And all this went on alongside lavish religious devotion. Amos's message is that the Lord who brought Israel out of Egypt will not accept worship from people who trample the weak, and that the Day of the Lord they longed for would be darkness, not light.

Amos never names Assyria as the enemy to come. He speaks only of a nation the Lord will raise up and of exile beyond Damascus. Within forty years of his preaching, that is what happened. Assyria revived under Tiglath-pileser, the northern kingdom was dismembered, and Samaria fell in about seven hundred and twenty-two before Christ. Amos was a near contemporary of Hosea, who was a man of the north and spoke a little later; the listener has just heard Hosea's very different voice describing the same society. In Judah, Isaiah and Micah were soon to begin.

The book is carefully shaped. It opens with a series of judgements on Israel's neighbours, each beginning with the same formula, which closes like a trap on Israel itself. Then come three chapters of oracles addressed to Samaria and its leaders, sharp, ironic and often bitterly funny. The last part is built around five visions, interrupted by the one story in the book, Amos's confrontation with the priest of Bethel. The final lines, after so much warning, look beyond the coming disaster to the rebuilding of the fallen house of David.

Amos's language is drawn from the life he knew: lions roaring over their prey, a shepherd rescuing two legs and a piece of an ear from a lion's mouth, a cart creaking under a load of sheaves, a basket of summer fruit. His call to let justice roll on like a river has been quoted in every generation since. In the New Testament, Stephen quotes Amos in his defence before the council, and James quotes him at the council in Jerusalem.

=== 1
The book begins with a brief introduction to Amos, a sheepherder from Tekoa, and a date fixed by a famous earthquake. Then comes the first word, like thunder: the Lord roars from Zion. The voice comes from Jerusalem, and its effect is felt as far as the summit of Mount Carmel in the north.

What follows is a sequence of judgements on the nations around Israel. Each opens with the same formula: for three transgressions, even four, I will not revoke my judgement. It is a way of saying that the measure is more than full. Damascus is first, the capital of the Arameans, whose kings Hazael and Ben-hadad had once ravaged Israel's lands in Gilead, east of the Jordan. Then come Gaza and the Philistines, and Tyre, the great trading city of Phoenicia, both accused of handing whole communities over to slavery. Edom, Ammon and Moab follow in the next chapter.

The crimes named are not offences against Israel's law, which these nations did not have, but acts of cruelty that any people should have recognised as evil. Amos's hearers in Israel would have welcomed every word.

=== 2
The sequence of oracles continues. Moab is condemned for burning the bones of Edom's king, an act of desecration against an enemy that had nothing to do with Israel. The point is clear: the Lord judges cruelty wherever it is found.

Then the circle tightens. Judah is next, Amos's own people, condemned not for brutality but for rejecting the Law of the Lord. A northern audience might have nodded at that too. Seven nations have been named, and seven was the number of completeness. But there is an eighth, and it is Israel.

Here the formula is the same, and the charges are longer than all the others. They are not about warfare but about everyday life: the poor sold for silver, the needy for a pair of sandals, garments taken in pledge from the poor and spread out beside the altars. Against this Amos sets the story of what the Lord has done for them, from the defeat of the Amorites to the exodus and the forty years in the wilderness. The chapter ends with an army in flight, its bravest warriors running away naked.

=== 3
Amos now addresses the whole family that the Lord brought out of Egypt. Their privilege does not protect them. You only have I known of all the families of the earth, the Lord says, and therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities.

Then Amos defends his right to speak, in a series of questions drawn from the countryside. Does a lion roar when it has no prey? Does a trap spring when nothing has touched it? Every effect has a cause. The lion has roared, and so the prophet must speak.

The next oracle invites foreign witnesses, the people of Ashdod in Philistia and of Egypt, to gather on the hills around Samaria and see the oppression inside its walls. An enemy will surround the land. Then comes one of Amos's most vivid images, from a shepherd's experience: as a shepherd might rescue from a lion only two legs or a piece of an ear, so only fragments of Samaria's luxury will be saved. The chapter ends at Bethel, where the horns of the altar will be cut off, and among the winter houses, summer houses and houses of ivory of the rich.

=== 4
This chapter opens with an insult that Amos's hearers could not have missed. He calls the wealthy women of Samaria cows of Bashan, after the rich pasture lands east of the Sea of Galilee, famous for their sleek, well-fed cattle. They oppress the poor and call to their husbands for more to drink. The day is coming when they will be dragged away with hooks through broken walls.

Then Amos turns to the shrines, and speaks like a priest calling worshippers to the altar, but with heavy irony: go to Bethel and transgress, go to Gilgal and sin even more. The people love their sacrifices and tithes and like to be seen offering them.

The rest of the chapter recalls a series of disasters that the Lord has already sent: famine, drought, blight and locusts, plague and war, a catastrophe like Sodom's. After each comes the same refrain: yet you did not return to me. The chapter ends with a stark summons, prepare to meet your God, and then a short hymn to the Creator, one of several in the book.

=== 5
Amos now takes up a lament, the kind of song sung at a funeral. Fallen is Virgin Israel, never to rise again. To sing a dirge over a nation still prospering must have sounded like madness, or like a curse.

Yet within the lament is an invitation. Seek me and live, the Lord says, and do not seek Bethel, Gilgal or Beersheba, the shrines where the people sought him so busily. Beersheba lay in the far south of Judah, and northern pilgrims evidently crossed the border to worship there. Amos then turns again to injustice in the gate, where bribes are taken and the poor are taxed of their grain. Seek good and not evil, he says, and perhaps the Lord will be gracious to the remnant of Joseph.

In the second half of the chapter Amos turns the people's hopes upside down. They long for the Day of the Lord, expecting victory over their enemies, but it will be darkness and not light. The Lord hates their feasts and will not hear their songs. Let justice roll on like a river, he says, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream. The chapter ends with exile, beyond Damascus.

=== 6
The woes continue, now aimed at the leaders of both capitals, those at ease in Zion and those who feel secure on the hill of Samaria. They are the notable men of the foremost nation, the people to whom others come for judgement and favour.

Amos invites them to look at other cities, Calneh and Hamath in the north and Gath of the Philistines, places whose fate had shown that no kingdom is safe. Then he paints a picture of a banquet among the elite of Samaria. They lie on beds inlaid with ivory, eat lambs and fattened calves, sing to the harp, drink wine by the bowlful and anoint themselves with the finest oils. But they do not grieve over the ruin of Joseph. So they will be the first to go into exile.

The chapter also contains a grim little scene of a house emptied by death, and a mocking reference to two recent victories in Gilead. Lo-debar sounds in Hebrew like nothing, and Karnaim means horns, a symbol of strength. Finally comes a threat that reverses Jeroboam's achievements. A nation will oppress Israel from Lebo-hamath to the Brook of the Arabah, the very borders the king had restored.

=== 7
The book now turns to a series of visions, told in Amos's own words. The first two show judgements about to fall: a swarm of locusts after the king's share of the harvest has been taken, and a consuming fire. Each time Amos pleads, how will Jacob survive, since he is so small, and each time the Lord relents.

The third vision is different. The Lord stands beside a wall with a plumb line in his hand, the builder's tool for testing whether a wall is straight. Israel has been measured and found crooked, and this time there is no reprieve. The sanctuaries will be laid waste, and the Lord will rise against the house of Jeroboam with the sword.

At this point comes the only story in the book. Amaziah, the priest of Bethel, sends word to King Jeroboam that Amos is conspiring against him, and he orders the prophet to go back to Judah and earn his living there. Bethel, he says, is the king's sanctuary. Amos replies that he was no prophet, nor a prophet's son, but a herdsman whom the Lord took from following the flock. Then he speaks a final word to Amaziah himself.

=== 8
The visions resume with the fourth: a basket of summer fruit. The meaning turns on a pun. The Hebrew word for summer fruit, qayits, sounds like the word for end, qets. The harvest is ripe, and so is Israel. The end has come for my people Israel, the Lord says, and the songs of the temple will become wailing.

Amos then turns once more to the merchants. In a few lines he lets us hear them talk. They chafe at the New Moon festival and the Sabbath, the days when trade was supposed to stop, and they plan to shrink the measure, raise the price, rig the scales, and sell even the sweepings of the wheat. Most damning of all, they will buy the poor for silver and the needy for a pair of sandals.

The Lord swears he will never forget. The language of what follows becomes cosmic: the land heaving like the Nile in flood, the sun going down at noon, feasts turned into mourning as for an only son. Then comes a different kind of famine, not of bread or water, but of hearing the words of the Lord.

=== 9
The fifth and last vision is the most terrible. Amos sees the Lord standing beside the altar, most likely the altar at Bethel, and commanding that the sanctuary be struck until its pillars fall on the worshippers. There will be no escape, whether to Sheol below, to heaven above, to the top of Mount Carmel or the bottom of the sea.

Israel had assumed that its rescue from Egypt made it safe. The Lord's answer is startling: he has guided other peoples too, the Philistines from Caphtor and the Arameans from Kir, and Israel is no more to him than the distant Cushites if it does not do justice. Yet the house of Jacob will not be utterly destroyed. It will be shaken like grain in a sieve, and the sinners will fall.

Then, in the last verses, the book changes tone. The Lord will raise up the fallen tent of David, the ruined dynasty of the south, and restore his people. Some scholars believe these lines were added after Amos's time; in the book as it stands they are the final word. James would quote them at the council in Jerusalem, when the early church considered the place of the Gentiles.
