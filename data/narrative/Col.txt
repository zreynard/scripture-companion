=== intro
Colossians is a letter to a church Paul had never seen. He writes from prison to a small community of believers in a provincial town, because news has reached him that they are being drawn toward teaching that could lead them away from Christ. His answer is one of the most exalted portraits of Christ in the New Testament.

Colossae lay in the valley of the river Lycus, in the region of Phrygia, in the Roman province of Asia, more than a hundred miles inland from Ephesus. In earlier centuries it had been a prosperous place, known for its wool and its dyed cloth, but by Paul's day it had been overshadowed by its larger neighbours in the same valley, Laodicea, a wealthy centre of banking and trade, and Hierapolis, famous for its hot springs. Colossae was, by the standards of the time, an unimportant town. It is probably the least important town to which any of Paul's surviving letters is addressed.

The church there was not founded by Paul. During his long stay in Ephesus on his third journey, Acts tells us, the message spread so widely that all who lived in the province of Asia heard it. Among those who heard it, it seems, was a man from Colossae named Epaphras. He became a believer, carried the gospel back to his own town, and seems to have worked among the believers of Laodicea and Hierapolis as well. Paul calls him a beloved fellow servant and a faithful minister of Christ. The believers in Colossae were mostly Gentiles, and they met in homes. One of those homes belonged to a man named Philemon, whom Paul knew personally.

Now Paul is in chains, and Epaphras has come to him with news. Much of it was good: the Colossians' faith and love were well known. But there was also cause for concern. Some in Colossae were promoting a kind of teaching that Paul calls philosophy and empty deception. Its exact nature has been much debated, because Paul describes it only by its features. It seems to have combined Jewish observances, such as food laws, festivals, new moons and Sabbaths, with strict rules about the body, an interest in visions, and some form of worship involving angels. It may have promised a fuller spiritual experience, and protection from the spiritual powers that people in that region greatly feared. Whatever its precise form, its effect was to suggest that Christ was not enough.

Where Paul was imprisoned is uncertain, as it is for Ephesians and Philippians. Christian tradition places him in Rome, in the early sixties of the first century, under the house arrest that Acts describes. Some scholars think an earlier imprisonment, perhaps in Ephesus, more likely, since it was much closer to Colossae. A minority of modern scholars have also questioned whether Paul wrote the letter himself, though many see no strong reason to doubt it.

Colossians belongs closely with two other letters. It shares a great deal of language with Ephesians, and both were carried by Tychicus, one of Paul's trusted companions from the province of Asia. It is also tied to the short letter to Philemon, which comes later in the New Testament. Travelling with Tychicus was a man named Onesimus, a slave who belonged to Philemon in Colossae. He had left his master, found his way to Paul, and become a believer. Now Paul was sending him home, not as a fugitive, but as a faithful and beloved brother. The two letters went to the same town on the same journey, and several of the same names appear in both.

The letter moves in a clear direction. Paul begins with thanks and prayer, and then with a great passage about who Christ is: the image of the invisible God, through whom and for whom all things were created, in whom all the fullness of God dwells, and who has reconciled all things by the blood of his cross. In chapter two he turns to the teaching that troubled the church, and argues that believers are already complete in Christ, who has disarmed every spiritual power. In the last two chapters he describes the new life that follows, in the church and in the household, and he closes with news of himself and greetings from a remarkable circle of companions, including Mark and Luke.

=== 1
Paul writes with Timothy to the believers in Colossae. He has never met them, but he has heard about their faith and love from Epaphras, who first brought them the gospel and who is now with Paul. So Paul thanks God for them and tells them how he prays: that they may be filled with the knowledge of God's will and live in a way that pleases him.

The prayer leads into the heart of the chapter, a passage about Christ that many scholars think may have been an early Christian hymn. It describes the Son as the image of the invisible God and the firstborn over all creation. Everything was made through him and for him, including thrones and rulers and authorities, the spiritual powers the Colossians were being taught to fear or honour. He is the head of the church, the first to rise from the dead, and the one through whom God reconciles all things to himself.

Paul then applies this to his readers, who were once alienated from God and are now reconciled. He closes the chapter by speaking of his own sufferings and his calling, to make known a mystery hidden for ages: Christ in you, the hope of glory.

=== 2
From the work God gave him, to proclaim the mystery of Christ among the Gentiles, Paul turns to tell the Colossians how much he is struggling for them, and for the believers at Laodicea, and for all who have never seen him face to face. In Christ, he says, are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge, and he says it so that no one will deceive them with fine-sounding arguments.

This brings Paul to the danger that prompted the letter. He warns against being taken captive by philosophy and empty deception, based on human tradition and the spiritual forces of the world rather than on Christ. In Christ the whole fullness of God dwells bodily, and the Colossians have been made complete in him. They have already been circumcised in a spiritual sense, buried and raised with him in baptism, and forgiven. Paul pictures the record of their debt nailed to the cross, and the hostile powers led as captives in a victory procession.

The rest of the chapter addresses the teaching more directly. Paul mentions rules about food and drink, festivals, new moons and Sabbaths, the worship of angels, visions, and strict treatment of the body, and he argues that none of it has any value against the indulgence of the flesh.

=== 3
At the end of the last chapter Paul dismissed the rules of the false teachers. They had an appearance of wisdom, he said, but no real power to change a life. Now he describes what does. If the Colossians have died with Christ, they have also been raised with him, and he urges them to set their minds on things above, where Christ is seated at the right hand of God.

From that new life comes a new way of living. Paul uses the picture of changing clothes. The old self, with its sexual immorality, greed, anger and lies, is to be taken off, and the new self put on. In the new humanity, he says, there is no Greek or Jew, barbarian or Scythian, slave or free. The Scythians, from the steppes north of the Black Sea, were regarded by Greeks as the most uncivilised people of all. Then Paul lists the qualities believers are to put on, with love over them all.

The chapter ends with instructions for the household, the same pattern Paul uses in Ephesians: wives and husbands, children and fathers, slaves and masters. The words to slaves are the longest, and they take on a particular weight when we remember that Onesimus, a slave returning to his master in Colossae, was travelling with this letter.

=== 4
Paul's instructions to the household end with a word to masters, reminding them that they too have a Master in heaven. Then he asks the Colossians to pray for him, that God may open a door for the message, and he reminds them that he is in chains.

The rest of the chapter is news and greetings, a picture of the people around Paul at this time. Tychicus is carrying the letter, and travelling with him is Onesimus, whom Paul calls a faithful and beloved brother, one of their own. Greetings come from Aristarchus, who had been seized by the mob in the riot at Ephesus and had travelled with Paul ever since; from Mark, the cousin of Barnabas, who had once turned back early from the first journey, and whom Paul had then refused to take with him again, but who was now restored to his trust; from Epaphras, who prays constantly for them; and from Luke, the beloved physician, and Demas.

Paul asks that this letter also be read in Laodicea, and that the Colossians read a letter that will come to them from there. That Laodicean letter has not survived under that name, though some suggest it may be the letter we know as Ephesians. Paul then adds a final greeting in his own hand: remember my chains.
