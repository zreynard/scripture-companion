=== intro
Mark is the shortest of the four Gospels, and the fastest. It has no family tree, no birth in Bethlehem and no long sermons on a mountain. It begins with a single line, the beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God, and within a few verses the man from Nazareth is grown, baptised, tested in the wilderness and walking the shore of the Sea of Galilee, calling fishermen to follow him. From there the story moves at a run. One of Mark's favourite words is usually translated immediately or at once, and it appears again and again, carrying the reader from one scene to the next.

The world is the one described before Matthew: a Jewish homeland under Roman power, Galilee in the north ruled by Herod Antipas, son of Herod the Great, and Judea in the south, with Jerusalem and its temple, governed by the Roman prefect Pontius Pilate. Mark assumes that world, but he often pauses to explain it. He tells his readers what the Pharisees do about washing their hands and their cups, that Preparation Day is the day before the Sabbath, and what Aramaic words mean: Talitha koum, little girl, get up; Ephphatha, be opened; Abba, Father. Those explanations suggest that his first readers were not Jews of Judea or Galilee, but largely Gentile Christians, some way from the land where the story took place.

The book does not name its author, but early Christian tradition is unusually consistent. Early in the second century, a bishop named Papias, in Asia Minor, passed on what he had heard from an elder of the church: that Mark had been Peter's interpreter, and had written down carefully, though not in strict order, what Peter remembered of the words and deeds of Jesus. This Mark is generally identified with John Mark of the book of Acts, a young man from Jerusalem whose mother's house was a meeting place for the first believers. He set out with Paul and Barnabas on their first missionary journey and turned back partway, which caused a sharp disagreement between the two older men. Years later he is found with Paul again, and in the first letter of Peter he is with Peter in a city called Babylon, very likely a code name for Rome.

Tradition places the writing of the Gospel in Rome, and many date it to the late sixties of the first century or soon after, the years when the emperor Nero turned on the Christians of the city and Peter and Paul are believed to have died there. The date cannot be fixed with certainty. Most scholars think Mark was the first Gospel to be written, and that Matthew and Luke both knew it and used much of it, which is why so many of its scenes appear in their books too, often in very similar words.

Mark writes vividly. He remembers that Jesus slept on a cushion in the stern of the boat, that the grass was green where the crowd sat down, that a blind man threw off his cloak and jumped up. He shows Jesus' emotions plainly: compassion, anger, grief, amazement. He is just as candid about the disciples, who are often afraid, confused and slow to understand, which is all the more striking if Peter stands behind the account. And there is a recurring note of secrecy. Jesus repeatedly tells those he heals, and even the demons who recognise him, to say nothing about who he is. The question of who Jesus really is hangs over the first half of the book.

The shape of the Gospel is simple and strong. The first half belongs mostly to Galilee and the lake, with its fishing towns, synagogues and crowds, and to journeys around its shores and into Gentile territory nearby. Miracles and controversies follow one another quickly, and the opposition of the Pharisees and the scribes from Jerusalem begins early. At the centre of the book, near Caesarea Philippi in the far north, Peter declares that Jesus is the Christ. From that moment Jesus begins to teach openly that he must suffer, be killed and rise again, and the story turns south. Three times on the road he predicts his death, and three times the disciples misunderstand. Then, from chapter eleven, Mark slows right down. More than a third of the book is given to the last week in Jerusalem: the entry into the city, the confrontations in the temple, the Passover meal, the arrest, the trials before the high priest and before Pilate, and the crucifixion.

The Son of God, announced in the opening line, is confessed at the end not by a disciple but by a Roman centurion, standing at the foot of the cross. And the Gospel closes at an empty tomb, with a message for the disciples and Peter that Jesus is going ahead of them into Galilee. Even the book's final lines have raised questions, which we will come to when we reach them. Mark set out to tell the beginning of the good news, and he tells it with urgency, as if there were no time to lose.

=== 1
Mark wastes no time. Within one chapter, John the Baptist appears in the wilderness, Jesus comes from Nazareth to be baptised, the Spirit drives him into the wilderness for forty days, and he begins his work in Galilee. Mark opens with a prophecy that blends words from Malachi and Isaiah about a messenger sent ahead to prepare the way, and John is that messenger, clothed in camel's hair like Elijah of old.

The temptation, which Matthew and Luke tell at length, takes Mark only two sentences, though he alone mentions that Jesus was with the wild animals. Once John is arrested, Jesus begins to proclaim that the time has come and the kingdom of God is near. He calls two pairs of brothers from their nets beside the lake: Simon and Andrew, and James and John, the sons of Zebedee.

Then Mark gives us a whole day in Capernaum, a Sabbath that begins in the synagogue and ends after sunset, when the Sabbath is over and people can carry their sick to the door of Simon's house. Before dawn Jesus slips away to pray, and the chapter ends with a man healed of leprosy who cannot keep the news to himself.

=== 2
The healed leper has spread the news so widely that Jesus can no longer enter a town openly. A few days later he is back in Capernaum, in a house so packed that no one else can reach the door. The houses of the town had flat roofs, reached by an outside stair and made of beams, branches and packed clay, and four men have a determined plan for their paralysed friend.

This chapter begins a run of five confrontations, stretching into the next, in which the religious leaders question what Jesus does. Each one turns on a different point: his claim to forgive sins, his choice of companions at table, his disciples' failure to fast, and their behaviour on the Sabbath.

Along the way Jesus calls a tax collector, Levi son of Alphaeus, from his booth by the lake. Matthew's Gospel tells the same story and calls him Matthew. In answer to the Pharisees, Jesus speaks of a bridegroom, of patches and wineskins, and of David eating the consecrated bread, and he ends with a claim about himself: the Son of Man is Lord even of the Sabbath.

=== 3
The Sabbath dispute continues in a synagogue, where a man with a withered hand stands before a watching audience. Mark records Jesus' anger and his grief at their hardness of heart. Then comes a sobering sign of how fast things have moved: the Pharisees go out and begin to plot with the Herodians, supporters of Herod Antipas, how they might kill him. It is only the third chapter.

Jesus withdraws to the lake, and the crowds now come from far beyond Galilee, from Judea and Jerusalem, from Idumea in the far south, from across the Jordan and from the coast around Tyre and Sidon. On a mountain he appoints twelve to be with him and to be sent out, and Mark lists their names, including the nickname he gave James and John, Boanerges, Sons of Thunder.

The rest of the chapter shows a habit of Mark's storytelling, in which one story is wrapped around another. Jesus' family set out to take charge of him, convinced he is out of his mind. Before they arrive, scribes who have come down from Jerusalem accuse him of being possessed. Then his mother and brothers reach the house.

=== 4
Jesus is back by the lake, and the crowd is so large that he teaches from a boat while the people line the shore. For the first time Mark gives an extended passage of his teaching, and it is in parables.

The first is about a farmer sowing seed on different kinds of ground, and it begins with a single word, listen. Later, alone with the twelve and others around him, Jesus explains it, and he speaks of the mystery of the kingdom of God being given to them while to outsiders everything comes in parables. Other sayings follow about a lamp and a measure, and two more seed parables. One, about seed that grows by itself while the farmer sleeps, is found only in Mark. The other is the mustard seed.

That evening Jesus tells the disciples to cross to the other side. The lake lies far below sea level, ringed by hills, and violent storms can rise on it suddenly. Mark remembers the detail of Jesus asleep on a cushion in the stern while the boat fills with water. The chapter ends not with relief but with the disciples' fearful question to one another, who is this?

=== 5
The boat comes ashore on the far side of the lake in the region of the Gerasenes, part of the Decapolis, a league of Greek cities whose people were mostly Gentiles. There Jesus meets a man living among the tombs, beyond the help of chains or neighbours. The spirits call themselves Legion, the name of a Roman army unit of several thousand men, and the herd of pigs on the hillside is a reminder that this is not Jewish country. When the man asks to go with Jesus, he is sent home instead to tell his own people.

Back across the lake, the next scene shows Mark's habit of nesting one story inside another. A synagogue leader named Jairus begs Jesus to come to his dying daughter. On the way, in the pressing crowd, a woman who has suffered from bleeding for twelve years, and spent all she had on doctors, reaches out to touch his clothes. Her condition would have made her ritually unclean, and she tries to remain unseen.

The delay is costly, and news comes from Jairus's house. Mark keeps the Aramaic words Jesus speaks at the girl's bedside, and adds that she was twelve years old.

=== 6
Jesus returns to Nazareth, his hometown, with his disciples, and teaches in the synagogue on the Sabbath. The people know him as the carpenter, the son of Mary, and they know his brothers James, Joses, Judas and Simon, and his sisters. Their familiarity turns to offence, and Mark makes a remarkable admission: Jesus could do few miracles there, and he was amazed at their unbelief.

He sends the twelve out two by two, travelling light. While they are away, Mark tells how Herod Antipas came to fear that Jesus was John the Baptist raised from the dead. That leads into a flashback: the story of John's arrest, of Herodias, the wife Herod had taken from his brother Philip, and of the birthday banquet at which John was put to death. Herod was officially a tetrarch, a lesser ruler, but Mark calls him king throughout.

The apostles come back, and Jesus tries to take them somewhere quiet to rest, but the crowds run ahead around the shore. What follows are two of the best-known scenes in the Gospels: the feeding of the five thousand, sitting in groups on the green grass, and Jesus walking on the lake in the night.

=== 7
Once again Pharisees and scribes have come from Jerusalem, and this time they object that Jesus' disciples eat with unwashed hands. Mark pauses to explain to his readers the washings that devout Jews practised, of hands, cups, pitchers and kettles, all according to the tradition of the elders. Jesus answers with Isaiah and with an example: the custom of declaring money Corban, devoted to God, so that it need not be used to support one's parents. Mark adds his own comment on Jesus' teaching about what really defiles a person: in saying this, Jesus declared all foods clean, a point of great importance for Gentile believers.

Then Jesus goes north-west into the region of Tyre, on the Phoenician coast, hoping to stay hidden. A Greek woman, born in Syrian Phoenicia, finds him and begs him to free her little daughter from an unclean spirit, and their exchange is sharp and memorable.

From there Jesus takes a long, roundabout route, north through Sidon and then back down to the Sea of Galilee and into the Decapolis. There a deaf man with a speech impediment is brought to him, and Mark again preserves the Aramaic word Jesus speaks: Ephphatha, be opened.

=== 8
Jesus seems to be still in the Decapolis, the Gentile country east of the lake, and a great crowd has been with him for three days with nothing to eat. A second feeding follows, of about four thousand people, with seven loaves. Then, crossing back, he meets Pharisees demanding a sign from heaven, and he refuses with a deep sigh.

In the boat, when Jesus warns the disciples about the leaven of the Pharisees and of Herod, they think he is talking about their lack of bread, and his questions to them are pointed: do they have eyes but fail to see? At Bethsaida, at the north end of the lake, he heals a blind man in two stages, the only healing in the Gospels that happens gradually. The man first sees people like trees walking, then everything clearly.

This is the hinge of Mark's Gospel. In the villages around Caesarea Philippi, far to the north, Jesus asks his disciples who people say he is, and then who they say he is. Peter answers, you are the Christ. At once Jesus begins to teach them plainly that the Son of Man must suffer, be rejected, be killed and rise after three days. From here the road leads to Jerusalem.

=== 9
Six days after Peter's confession, Jesus takes Peter, James and John up a high mountain, and there he is transfigured before them, his clothes whiter, Mark says, than any launderer on earth could bleach them. Elijah and Moses appear, talking with him, and the voice that spoke at his baptism speaks again, now to the disciples. On the way down, Jesus tells them to say nothing until the Son of Man has risen from the dead, and they wonder what that could mean.

At the foot of the mountain they find the other disciples arguing with scribes, and a father whose son has suffered from a violent spirit since childhood. Mark's account is much fuller than the others', and it contains the father's cry, I do believe; help my unbelief.

Then Jesus passes through Galilee quietly, because he is teaching his disciples, and for the second time he tells them he will be killed and rise again. They do not understand and are afraid to ask. Back in Capernaum, in the house, he asks what they were arguing about on the road. They had been discussing which of them was the greatest. His sayings to them follow, about service, welcome and stumbling.

=== 10
Jesus now leaves Galilee and goes south into Judea and the region across the Jordan, and the teaching that follows takes place on the move.

Pharisees test him with a question about divorce, and he takes them back to the creation of man and woman. People bring little children for him to bless, and when the disciples rebuke them, Jesus is indignant. A man runs up and kneels before him to ask how to inherit eternal life, and Mark alone records that Jesus looked at him and loved him. The man goes away grieving, because he has great wealth.

Then comes one of Mark's most striking pictures. On the road up to Jerusalem, Jesus walks ahead of them, and the disciples are astonished and those who follow are afraid. He tells them for the third time what awaits him, now in full detail. Immediately James and John ask for the places of honour in his glory. Jesus answers with a saying that sums up his mission: the Son of Man came not to be served, but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many. The chapter ends in Jericho, with a blind beggar named Bartimaeus who will not keep quiet.

=== 11
The last week begins. Jesus approaches Jerusalem by way of Bethphage and Bethany, two villages on the slopes of the Mount of Olives, east of the city. He rides into the city on a colt that has never been ridden, among crowds of Passover pilgrims who spread cloaks and branches on the road and shout Hosanna, blessed is the coming kingdom of our father David.

Mark tells the next days with care. On the first evening, Jesus simply enters the temple, looks around at everything, and goes back to Bethany because it is late. The next morning, on the way into the city, he finds a fig tree with leaves but no fruit. Then he enters the temple courts and drives out the buyers and sellers, quoting Isaiah's words that God's house will be called a house of prayer for all the nations. The chief priests and scribes begin to look for a way to destroy him.

The following morning the fig tree has withered, and Jesus speaks about faith and prayer. Back in the temple, the leaders confront him with a question that will shape the coming days: by what authority is he doing these things?

=== 12
Jesus is still in the temple courts, facing the chief priests, scribes and elders who have just challenged his authority. He answers them with a parable about tenants in a vineyard who beat the owner's servants and finally kill his beloved son. The image of Israel as God's vineyard was an old one, from the prophet Isaiah, and the leaders know the story is aimed at them.

Then, one after another, they send people to catch him out. Pharisees and Herodians ask about paying taxes to Caesar. Sadducees, who deny the resurrection, bring a riddle about a woman married to seven brothers in turn. A scribe who has been listening asks which commandment is most important, and in Mark's account, unlike Matthew's, the exchange ends warmly, with Jesus telling him he is not far from the kingdom of God. Then Jesus asks a question of his own about the Messiah as David's son, and the large crowd listens with delight.

The chapter ends quietly, beside the temple treasury. Jesus warns against scribes who parade their piety and devour widows' houses, and then he watches a poor widow drop in two small copper coins, all she has.

=== 13
As Jesus leaves the temple, one of his disciples marvels at its massive stones and magnificent buildings. Herod's temple was among the most impressive buildings of the Roman world. Jesus replies that not one stone will be left on another. Roman armies would destroy it about forty years later.

He crosses the Kidron valley and sits on the Mount of Olives, opposite the temple, and four disciples, Peter, James, John and Andrew, ask him privately when this will happen and what the sign will be. His answer is the longest speech in Mark's Gospel. It speaks of false messiahs, wars and earthquakes, of the disciples being handed over to councils and governors, of the abomination of desolation, a phrase from Daniel, and of the Son of Man coming in the clouds. At one point Mark breaks in with an aside to his readers: let the reader understand.

Interpreters have long discussed how much of the discourse concerns the fall of Jerusalem and how much the end of the age. Its last word is clear enough. It closes with a short parable about a man who goes away and leaves his servants in charge, and with a single command addressed to everyone: keep watch.

=== 14
The Passover is two days away, and the chief priests and scribes are looking for a way to arrest Jesus quietly, away from the festival crowds. Mark's pace now slows almost to hour by hour.

At Bethany, in the house of Simon the Leper, a woman breaks a jar of pure nard and pours it over Jesus' head. Some present complain that it was worth over three hundred denarii, about a year's wages for a labourer. Then Judas Iscariot goes to the chief priests, who are delighted and promise him money.

The Passover meal is eaten in a large upper room in the city, found by following a man carrying a jar of water. During the meal Jesus speaks of betrayal and gives the bread and the cup. Then they cross to the Mount of Olives, to a place called Gethsemane, where Jesus prays, using the Aramaic word Abba, Father, while Peter, James and John fall asleep. Judas arrives with an armed crowd, and Mark alone mentions a young man who escapes, leaving his linen garment behind; some have wondered whether it was Mark himself. The night ends with Jesus before the high priest and the council, and with Peter in the courtyard below, warming himself by the fire.

=== 15
At dawn the whole council, the Sanhedrin, reaches its decision. They bind Jesus and hand him over to Pontius Pilate, the Roman governor, who alone could order an execution. Pilate asks whether he is the King of the Jews, and receives little answer.

It was the custom at the feast to release a prisoner, and among those being held is Barabbas, imprisoned with rebels who had committed murder in an uprising. The crowd, stirred up by the chief priests, chooses him. The soldiers mock him as a king, with a purple robe and a crown of thorns.

On the way out of the city they force a passer-by to carry the cross, Simon of Cyrene, and Mark adds that he was the father of Alexander and Rufus, names his first readers apparently knew. Mark notes the hours of the day: Jesus is crucified at the third hour, about nine in the morning, darkness falls at noon, and at three in the afternoon he cries out in Aramaic the opening words of Psalm twenty-two. Women who followed him from Galilee watch from a distance, and before the Sabbath begins at sunset, Joseph of Arimathea, a respected member of the council, asks Pilate for the body.

=== 16
The Sabbath is over. Very early on the first day of the week, three women, Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, and Salome, set out with spices to anoint the body. Their only worry on the way is who will roll the heavy stone away from the entrance of the tomb.

What they find is brief and unsettling: the stone moved, a young man in a white robe, and a message for the disciples, and for Peter by name, that Jesus is going ahead of them into Galilee, just as he told them. The women flee, trembling and bewildered, and say nothing to anyone, because they are afraid.

There the oldest surviving manuscripts of Mark end. Most later manuscripts continue with a further twelve verses, which describe appearances of the risen Jesus, his commission to preach the gospel to all creation, and his being taken up into heaven. Many scholars believe these verses were added by a later hand, drawing on the other Gospels and Acts, though they were accepted and read in the church from early times, and they are included in the reading that follows. Whether Mark intended to end so abruptly, or his ending was lost, remains an open question.
