=== intro
The book of Joshua closed with a nation at rest. The tribes had their land, the old leader had made them promise at Shechem to serve the Lord alone, and Joshua, Joseph's bones and Eleazar the priest were laid in their graves. The book of Judges opens in the generation that follows, and it tells what became of that promise.

The answer is not a happy one. Judges covers the long stretch of time between the settlement of Canaan and the rise of Israel's first king, a period of perhaps two centuries, though its exact length and dates are uncertain. Israel in these years is not a kingdom but a loose federation of tribes, each living on its own allotted land, with no capital, no standing army and no central government. The tabernacle stands at Shiloh in the hill country of Ephraim, but most of the time the tribes act alone, and sometimes against each other.

They also share the land with others. Joshua warned that the Canaanites who remained would become a snare, and Judges opens by listing, tribe by tribe, the towns they failed to take. Canaanite cities still hold the fertile valleys and the coast, with their iron chariots and their gods: Baal, the storm god who was believed to bring the rains and the harvest, and the goddesses Asherah and Ashtoreth. Around the edges of Israel's territory press other peoples, Moabites and Ammonites from across the Jordan, Midianite raiders from the desert, and, increasingly, the Philistines, who had settled on the southern coastal plain and would become Israel's most dangerous enemy.

The book takes its name from the leaders God raises up in these years. The Hebrew word usually translated judges means something broader than a magistrate. A few of them settle disputes, but most are deliverers, local champions who rally one tribe or a handful of tribes to throw off an oppressor. They are an unlikely company: Ehud, a left-handed man from the small tribe of Benjamin; Deborah, a prophetess who judges Israel beneath a palm tree; Gideon, the youngest son of an unimportant family, threshing wheat in hiding; Jephthah, the son of a prostitute, driven out by his brothers; and Samson, a man of enormous strength and very little self-control. Alongside these are several minor judges, mentioned in a sentence or two.

The stories follow a pattern that the second chapter spells out in advance. Israel does evil in the sight of the Lord and serves other gods. The Lord hands them over to an oppressor. In their distress they cry out, and the Lord raises up a deliverer, who rescues them. The land has rest, often for forty years. Then the deliverer dies, and the cycle begins again. But the cycle is not a circle. It is a downward spiral. Othniel, the first judge, is a model leader. By the time of Gideon, the deliverer himself makes an object that leads Israel astray, and his son Abimelech murders his brothers to make himself king. Jephthah's rash vow ends in grief. Samson, the last of the great judges, fights the Philistines largely alone, driven as much by personal grievance as by any sense of calling, and he never brings the land rest.

The final chapters drop the pattern altogether. There are no more deliverers, only two disturbing stories from the life of the tribes. One concerns a household shrine, a wandering Levite and the migration of the tribe of Dan. The other concerns a crime at Gibeah in Benjamin so terrible that it leads to civil war. Through these chapters runs a refrain: in those days there was no king in Israel, and everyone did what was right in his own eyes.

That refrain suggests the book took its final shape when Israel did have a king, looking back on the years before. The author is not named. Jewish tradition credited Samuel, and the book certainly belongs with the histories that follow it, but beyond that we cannot say. It is also worth knowing that the judges did not necessarily follow one another in a neat line. Most were local figures, and some of their careers may have overlapped in different parts of the land.

Judges is one of the most honest books in the Bible about the failures of God's people, and it does not soften them. Yet through all of it God keeps answering their cries and raising up rescuers, however flawed. The gentle story of Ruth is set in these same years, and the book of First Samuel will carry the history on, through the last of the judges, toward the question the refrain keeps raising: what kind of king Israel needs.

=== 1
Joshua is dead, and Israel has no single leader to take his place. The tribes have their allotted land, but much of it is still held by Canaanites, and the book begins with the people asking the Lord which tribe should go up first to fight. The answer is Judah.

The chapter is a survey of the tribes' efforts to take possession of their territory, moving roughly from south to north. Some of it repeats material from the book of Joshua, such as Caleb's offer of his daughter Acsah to whoever captures Debir, and Othniel's success there. The book is gathering the threads of the settlement together rather than telling events in strict order. Judah begins well, with victories in the hill country and the south, though its troops cannot overcome the iron chariots of the plains.

As the survey moves north, the tone changes. Benjamin does not drive the Jebusites out of Jerusalem. Then comes a list of tribes that fail, Manasseh, Ephraim, Zebulun, Asher, Naphtali, and finally Dan, which is pushed back into the hills by the Amorites. The phrase did not drive out becomes a drumbeat. The land is shared, not taken.

=== 2
The previous chapter recorded the tribes' failure to drive out the Canaanites. Now the angel of the Lord comes up from Gilgal, Israel's first camp in the land, to a place called Bochim, and he gives God's verdict. Israel has made agreements with the peoples of the land and has not torn down their altars. So the Lord will no longer drive those peoples out; they will be thorns in Israel's side. The people weep, and the place is named Bochim, which means weepers.

The chapter then steps back and restates the death of Joshua, as if beginning again. While Joshua and the elders who outlived him were alive, the people served the Lord. But a new generation grows up who do not know the Lord or what he has done.

The rest of the chapter is unusual: it summarises the whole book before the stories begin. It describes the cycle that will repeat again and again: Israel turns to the Baals, God gives them over to their enemies, they groan under oppression, God raises up a judge to save them, and after the judge's death they fall back further than before. The listener is given the key to everything that follows.

=== 3
The pattern has been laid out, and now it begins. The chapter opens by naming the nations left in the land to test Israel, the Philistines, Canaanites, Sidonians and Hivites, and it records the first step of compromise: Israelites marrying into these peoples and serving their gods.

Then come the first three deliverers, and the stories grow longer as they go. Othniel, Caleb's nephew, whom we met capturing Debir, rescues Israel from a king named Cushan-rishathaim of Aram-naharaim, the land of the two rivers in northern Mesopotamia. His story is told in a few lines and contains every element of the pattern, like a template for what follows.

The second story is very different, full of vivid and earthy detail. Eglon, king of Moab, has crossed the Jordan with his allies and occupied Jericho, the City of Palms, and for eighteen years Israel pays him tribute. The deliverer is Ehud, from the tribe of Benjamin, and his left-handedness will matter. The chapter ends with a single sentence about a third figure, Shamgar son of Anath, who strikes down six hundred Philistines with an oxgoad, the long pointed stick used for driving cattle.

=== 4
After Ehud's death, Israel again does evil, and this time the oppressor is a Canaanite king, Jabin of Hazor in the far north. A king of Hazor called Jabin was defeated in Joshua's day; this is evidently a later ruler with the same name, in a city that has risen again. His army commander is Sisera, and his strength is nine hundred iron chariots. For twenty years he oppresses the northern tribes.

The judge in these days is Deborah, a prophetess, who sits under a palm tree in the hill country of Ephraim, between Ramah and Bethel, where the Israelites come to her for judgement. She summons a man named Barak from Kedesh in Naphtali and passes on God's command: gather ten thousand men from Naphtali and Zebulun on Mount Tabor, a rounded hill that rises above the Jezreel Valley. Barak's hesitation, and Deborah's answer to it, set up a twist at the end of the story.

The battle takes place by the River Kishon, which flows through the valley toward the sea. Watch also for a minor character introduced in passing: Heber the Kenite, distantly related to Moses' father-in-law, who has pitched his tent near Kedesh. His wife's name is Jael.

=== 5
The battle by the Kishon is over. Sisera is dead at the hand of Jael, and Jabin's grip on the north is broken. Now Deborah and Barak sing.

This song is one of the oldest pieces of Hebrew poetry in the Bible, and it tells the same story as the chapter before, but in a very different way. Where chapter four was prose and narrative, chapter five is celebration, full of sound and movement. It begins with the Lord marching out from Seir, in the south, as at Sinai, and the earth trembling before him.

The song also fills in details the prose left out. It describes how dangerous life had become under the Canaanites, with roads deserted and village life at a standstill. It calls the roll of the tribes, praising those who came to fight and rebuking those who stayed at home, among them Reuben, Dan and Asher. It hints that a storm turned the battle, with the stars fighting from heaven and the river sweeping the enemy away.

At its close the song turns to two women. One is Jael, celebrated in vivid verse. The other is Sisera's mother, looking out through her lattice window, waiting for a son who will never come home.

=== 6
The land has had forty years of rest since the song of Deborah. Then Israel again does evil, and this time the oppression comes from the desert. The Midianites, with the Amalekites and other peoples of the east, sweep in every year at harvest time, like a swarm of locusts, with their tents, their flocks and their camels. They strip the land as far as Gaza on the coast and leave nothing. The Israelites retreat to caves and mountain strongholds.

When the people cry out, God first sends a prophet to remind them why this is happening. Then the angel of the Lord appears at Ophrah, in the territory of Manasseh, under an oak belonging to a man named Joash. His son Gideon is threshing wheat, not on an open threshing floor where the wind would carry away the chaff, but down in a winepress, out of sight of the raiders.

The angel greets him as a mighty man of valour, and the irony is not lost on Gideon. What follows is a call, a sign, and a dangerous first assignment close to home, because Gideon's own father keeps an altar to Baal. By the end of the chapter the Midianite armies have crossed the Jordan and camped in the Valley of Jezreel, and Gideon, still uncertain, asks God for signs with a fleece of wool.

=== 7
Gideon has his answer from the fleece, and he and his men camp by the spring of Harod, on the slopes above the Valley of Jezreel. Below them, near the hill of Moreh, lies the vast camp of Midian.

Gideon has gathered more than thirty thousand men from Manasseh and the northern tribes. It seems far too few against so great a host. God's view is the opposite: there are too many, and if Israel wins with an army this size, they will think they saved themselves. So the army is reduced, first by sending home everyone who is afraid, and then by a strange test at the water's edge, until only three hundred remain.

Then, by night, God tells Gideon to go down to the enemy camp, and gently adds that if he is afraid he may take his servant Purah with him. What Gideon overhears among the Midianite tents gives him the courage he needs. The attack that follows uses rams' horns, clay jars and torches rather than swords, and by the end of the chapter the men of Ephraim have joined the pursuit and captured two Midianite princes at the fords of the Jordan.

=== 8
The Midianite army has broken and fled, but the fighting is not over. The chapter opens with a quarrel. The proud tribe of Ephraim, which has just captured the Midianite princes Oreb and Zeeb, is angry that Gideon did not call them sooner, and Gideon calms them with a gracious answer.

Then he crosses the Jordan in pursuit of the two kings of Midian, Zebah and Zalmunna, with his three hundred exhausted men. On the eastern side, the Israelite towns of Succoth and Penuel refuse to feed his troops, unwilling to risk Midian's revenge before the war is won. Gideon warns them that he will be back.

The second half of the chapter shows a harder, more complicated man than the hesitant farmer of chapter six. Gideon has scores to settle, and the reason for his pursuit of the two kings turns out to be personal. When the people offer him and his descendants the right to rule over them, his answer sounds exactly right. But what he does next with the gold of Midian casts a long shadow. The chapter ends with Gideon's death, a note about a son named Abimelech, born to a concubine in Shechem, and Israel's swift return to Baal.

=== 9
Gideon refused to be king, but one of his sons has no such hesitation. Abimelech, the son of Gideon's concubine in Shechem, goes to his mother's relatives there and makes them an offer: would they rather be ruled by the seventy sons of Gideon, also called Jerubbaal, or by one man who is their own flesh and blood?

Shechem is an old Canaanite city in the hill country of Manasseh and Ephraim, the same place where Joshua renewed the covenant. Its leaders fund Abimelech from the temple of Baal-berith, and with that money he hires a band of reckless men. What he does at his father's house in Ophrah leaves only one brother alive, the youngest, Jotham.

From the slopes of Mount Gerizim, above Shechem, Jotham shouts a parable to the city, the first fable in the Bible, about trees looking for a king. It ends with a curse. The rest of the chapter traces how that curse works itself out over the three years of Abimelech's rule, through suspicion, rebellion and burning towers. This is the first attempt at kingship in Israel, and it is a dark one.

=== 10
Abimelech is dead, killed by a millstone dropped from a tower, and his short and violent reign is over. The chapter begins quietly, with two of the so-called minor judges. Tola, from the tribe of Issachar, judges Israel for twenty-three years from Shamir in the hill country of Ephraim. Jair, from Gilead east of the Jordan, judges for twenty-two. His thirty sons, riding thirty donkeys and holding thirty towns, suggest wealth and status, the trappings of a local dynasty.

Then the cycle turns again, and more darkly than before. The list of foreign gods Israel now serves is longer than ever: the Baals and Ashtoreths, and the gods of Aram, Sidon, Moab, Ammon and the Philistines. Two oppressors rise at once, the Philistines in the west and the Ammonites in the east. The chapter concentrates on the Ammonites, who press hard on the Israelites in Gilead for eighteen years and even cross the Jordan to raid Judah, Benjamin and Ephraim.

When Israel cries out this time, God's reply is unexpectedly sharp. The exchange that follows is one of the most revealing conversations in the book. It ends with the armies of Ammon and Israel facing each other in Gilead, and the leaders of Gilead searching for someone, anyone, willing to lead the attack.

=== 11
The Ammonites are camped in Gilead, and the Gileadites at Mizpah, without a commander. The man they turn to is Jephthah, a mighty warrior with a troubled past. He is the son of a man named Gilead and a prostitute, and his half-brothers drove him out to keep him from any share of the inheritance. He has since lived in the land of Tob, to the north-east, at the head of a band of outlaws.

Now the elders who rejected him come asking for help, and Jephthah drives a hard bargain. If he fights, he will be their head.

Before going to war, Jephthah tries diplomacy. His message to the king of Ammon is a careful history lesson, recalling Israel's journey from Egypt, the refusal of Edom and Moab to let them pass, and the defeat of the Amorite king Sihon, whose land Israel took. He claims Israel has held that land for three hundred years.

Then the Spirit of the Lord comes on Jephthah, and he marches. But before the battle he makes a vow, and its consequences turn his victory into one of the saddest scenes in the Old Testament. Interpreters have long debated exactly what the vow led to; the text itself is spare and restrained.

=== 12
Jephthah has defeated the Ammonites, but there is no peace. Once again the tribe of Ephraim feels slighted. They complained to Gideon for not calling them sooner, and Gideon calmed them with a soft answer. Now they cross the Jordan to confront Jephthah with the same complaint, and with a threat to burn his house down around him.

Jephthah is not Gideon, and this time the quarrel turns into war between Israelite tribes. The fighting ends at the fords of the Jordan with a test of pronunciation. Ephraimites spoke with a different accent from the men of Gilead, and a single word, shibboleth, gives them away. The word has passed into the English language as a test that marks out who belongs.

Jephthah judges Israel for six years. The chapter then records three more minor judges in quick succession: Ibzan of Bethlehem, Elon of Zebulun, and Abdon of Pirathon in Ephraim. Their large families, arranged marriages and many donkeys suggest leaders of local prominence and wealth. None of them leads Israel into battle, and none is said to have saved anyone.

=== 13
After the minor judges, the familiar opening returns: the Israelites again do evil, and the Lord gives them into the hands of the Philistines for forty years. This is the longest oppression in the book, and for the first time there is no mention of Israel crying out for help.

The Philistines were a seafaring people who had settled on the southern coastal plain, in five cities ruled by five lords. They were skilled in working iron, and they were steadily pushing inland into the foothills where the tribe of Dan was trying to settle.

The story begins in the Danite town of Zorah, in the foothills west of Jerusalem, with a couple who have no children. Like Sarah, Rebekah and Rachel before her, Manoah's wife is barren, and like them she receives a promise. The angel of the Lord tells her she will have a son, who must be a Nazirite from birth. In the law of Moses, a Nazirite was someone set apart to God by a special vow: no wine or grapes, no contact with the dead, and no razor on the head. This child will be set apart for life, and he will begin to rescue Israel from the Philistines.

Manoah's longing to understand, and his wife's calm good sense, make this one of the gentlest scenes in Judges. The child's name is Samson.

=== 14
Samson has grown up in Zorah, and the Spirit of the Lord has begun to stir him. Now he goes down from the hills to Timnah, a town in the valley below, on the border with Philistine territory, and sees a young Philistine woman he wants to marry.

His parents object. Israel had been warned against marrying into the peoples of the land, and the Philistines, uncircumcised and ruling over Israel, were the last family they would choose. But Samson insists, because she is pleasing in his eyes, a phrase worth remembering in a book that ends with everyone doing what is right in his own eyes. The narrator adds that, unknown to them, the Lord is using this to bring about a confrontation with the Philistines.

What follows is the first of Samson's adventures, and it introduces his character: enormous physical strength, given by the Spirit of the Lord, and a talent for riddles and wordplay, joined to impulsiveness, secrecy and a quick temper. A lion in the vineyards, a wedding feast, a riddle and a wager lead, by the seventh day of the celebration, to violence and a broken marriage.

=== 15
Samson left Timnah in a rage after the wedding feast, and his bride was given to one of his companions. Some time later, at the wheat harvest in early summer, he comes back with a young goat as a gift, expecting to go in to his wife. Her father refuses, and offers her younger sister instead.

What follows is a spiral of revenge, each act provoking a worse one. Samson's answer involves three hundred foxes, torches and the Philistines' ripe fields, vineyards and olive groves. The Philistines strike back at the woman and her father. Samson strikes back again, and then withdraws to a cave in the rock of Etam, in the territory of Judah.

The most revealing moment comes when the Philistines march into Judah to take him. Three thousand men of Judah go down to Samson, not to join him but to hand him over. Do you not realise, they ask, that the Philistines rule over us? Israel has grown used to its oppressors. Samson lets himself be bound, and is led to a place called Lehi, where the Philistines are waiting, shouting. The chapter ends with Samson, for the first time, calling on the Lord, and with a summary: he judged Israel for twenty years in the days of the Philistines.

=== 16
Samson has judged Israel for twenty years, and this chapter tells the end of his story. It opens in Gaza, the southernmost of the Philistine cities, near the coast, where Samson spends the night with a prostitute. The men of the city wait at the gate to kill him at dawn, but by morning the gate itself is gone, carried off on his shoulders.

Then Samson falls in love with a woman named Delilah, who lives in the Valley of Sorek, the broad valley that runs down from Samson's home country toward Philistine territory. She is the only woman in his story who is named, and she is never said to love him. The lords of the Philistines, the rulers of their five cities, offer her a fortune to discover the secret of his strength.

The scene repeats itself, with growing tension: a question, a false answer, a trap, and the cry, Samson, the Philistines are upon you. The listener knows what Samson seems to forget, that his strength is bound to his calling as a Nazirite, set apart to God from birth. The last part of the chapter moves to the temple of Dagon, the chief god of the Philistines, in Gaza, where his enemies gather to celebrate.

=== 17
Samson is dead, buried in his father's tomb between Zorah and Eshtaol. With him the stories of the judges come to an end. No more deliverers are raised up, and the last five chapters of the book are two longer stories, set at some unspecified time during this period, that show what life in Israel had become.

The first begins with a household in the hill country of Ephraim. A man named Micah has stolen a large amount of silver from his own mother, and returns it after hearing her curse the thief. Her response is to dedicate some of the silver to the Lord, by having it made into an idol. Soon Micah has a whole private shrine, with an ephod, household gods, and one of his own sons as priest.

Every part of this breaks the law of Moses, and yet the family appears to believe it is honouring the Lord. The narrator offers only one comment, and it will become the refrain of these final chapters: in those days there was no king in Israel; everyone did what was right in his own eyes. Then a young Levite from Bethlehem arrives at the door, looking for somewhere to settle, and Micah offers him a job.

=== 18
Micah now has his shrine, his idols and a Levite as his private priest, and he believes God will bless him for it. Meanwhile, elsewhere in Israel, the tribe of Dan is in trouble. Its allotted territory in the foothills and the coastal plain has proved impossible to hold, against the Amorites and then the Philistines, and the tribe is looking for somewhere else to live.

The Danites send five scouts from Zorah and Eshtaol, Samson's home district. Their route takes them north through the hill country of Ephraim, past Micah's house, where they recognise the young Levite's voice. Then they travel on to the far north of the land, to a town called Laish, near the springs of the Jordan at the foot of Mount Hermon. Its people live in peace and security, far from any ally who might help them.

The story that follows echoes the conquest under Joshua: spies, a good report, an army of six hundred, a town taken. But everything is turned upside down. The Danites steal their gods, and the priest happily goes with them. At the end of the chapter the Levite is finally named, and his ancestry is startling: he is a grandson of Moses. The town of Dan will become a centre of worship in the northern kingdom long after this, and not of the kind Moses taught.

=== 19
The second of the closing stories opens, like the first, with the reminder that there was no king in Israel, and, like the first, it involves a Levite and the towns of Ephraim and Bethlehem. This Levite lives in a remote part of the hill country of Ephraim. His concubine, a secondary wife, leaves him and goes home to her father's house in Bethlehem, and after four months he travels south to bring her back.

The first part of the story is almost leisurely. The young woman's father is a generous host, and he keeps persuading his son-in-law to stay one more day. When the Levite finally leaves, late in the afternoon, the light is fading. They pass Jebus, the city that will one day be Jerusalem, but he refuses to stay among foreigners. He presses on a few miles north to Gibeah, an Israelite town in the tribe of Benjamin.

There, where he expects safety among his own people, no one offers him a place to sleep until an old man, himself an outsider from Ephraim, takes them in. What happens that night deliberately recalls the story of Sodom in Genesis, almost word for word in places, except that this time it happens in Israel. It is one of the most harrowing passages in Scripture, and the text tells it with restraint and without comment. The chapter ends with a gruesome summons sent to all the tribes, and the people's stunned response: nothing like this has happened since Israel came out of Egypt.

=== 20
The Levite's terrible summons has gone out to all the tribes, and Israel responds as it has done in no other story in this book: together. From Dan in the far north to Beersheba in the south, and from Gilead across the Jordan, the tribes assemble as one man before the Lord at Mizpah, in the territory of Benjamin. Ironically, the unity that was so often missing against foreign enemies is now turned against one of their own.

The Levite tells his story to the assembly, and the tribes demand that Benjamin hand over the men of Gibeah who did this. Benjamin refuses, and instead the whole tribe gathers to defend the town. Among its soldiers are seven hundred left-handed slingers who never miss, a reminder of Ehud, the left-handed Benjamite who once saved Israel.

The war that follows is fought over three days. Israel inquires of God at Bethel, where the ark of the covenant is kept at this time, and where Phinehas, the grandson of Aaron, serves as priest. That detail suggests these events took place early in the period of the judges, even though they are told at the end. The losses on both sides are staggering, and the chapter ends with Benjamin almost wiped out.

=== 21
The war is over, and Israel's victory has left a hole in the nation. Only six hundred men of Benjamin survive, hiding at the rock of Rimmon in the wilderness, and the other tribes have sworn an oath at Mizpah that none of them will give a daughter in marriage to a Benjamite. Now they realise what that means: one of the twelve tribes is about to disappear.

The people gather at Bethel and weep before the Lord, asking why this has happened. But the solutions they find are their own. The first involves Jabesh-gilead, a town east of the Jordan whose people did not come to the assembly. The second involves the yearly feast at Shiloh, where the young women come out to dance in the vineyards. Both solutions keep the letter of their oaths while causing fresh harm.

Both places will reappear in the story of Israel's first king, Saul, who will come from the tribe of Benjamin, from the town of Gibeah, and whose first act as king will be to rescue Jabesh-gilead. The book ends with the people going home, and with its refrain, now heard for the last time: in those days there was no king in Israel, and everyone did what was right in his own eyes.
