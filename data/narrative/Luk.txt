=== intro
The Gospel of Luke is the first half of a two-part work. Its sequel, the book of Acts, carries the story on from Jerusalem to Rome, and together the two volumes make up more than a quarter of the New Testament, more than any other single writer contributed. Luke's part is to tell the story of Jesus from the first announcement of his coming to the day he is taken up into heaven, and to tell it carefully and in order.

Neither volume names its author, but both are addressed to the same man, Theophilus, and from the second century onwards the church attributed them to Luke. He appears by name only three times, all in Paul's letters. Paul calls him the beloved physician, counts him among his fellow workers, and, writing from prison near the end of his life, says that only Luke is with him. In several stretches of Acts the narrative shifts quietly from they to we, as though the writer had joined Paul on the road, and that fits the tradition well. From the way Paul lists his companions, many readers have concluded that Luke was a Gentile, which would make him the only Gentile writer in the New Testament, though that cannot be proved.

Of Theophilus we know almost nothing. The title Luke gives him, most excellent, was used for men of rank, and Luke later uses it of the Roman governors Felix and Festus. The name means friend of God. Luke tells him plainly what he has set out to do. Many others have already written accounts of these events. Luke has gone over everything from the beginning, drawing on those who were eyewitnesses from the start, so that he can write an orderly account, and Theophilus can be sure of what he has been taught. Luke does not claim to have been there himself. He belongs to the next generation, and he has done his research. Much of what he tells is shared with Matthew and Mark, and many scholars think Mark's gospel was among his sources, but a large part of his material appears nowhere else. When he wrote is uncertain. Some date the book to the early sixties of the first century, while Paul was still awaiting trial in Rome; many place it some years later.

Luke sets his story firmly in the Roman world. He names emperors and governors and dates events by them: Caesar Augustus and his census, the fifteenth year of Tiberius, Pontius Pilate governor of Judea. The Jewish homeland is a small and restless corner of the empire. As the story opens it is ruled by Herod the Great, a king who holds his throne by Rome's favour. After his death his kingdom is divided among his sons, and by the time Jesus is grown, Galilee in the north belongs to Herod Antipas, while Judea, with Jerusalem at its heart, is governed directly by a Roman official. The temple, rebuilt on a magnificent scale by Herod, stands at the centre of Jewish life, in the hands of the high priestly families.

Luke's story begins and ends in that temple. It opens with an elderly priest burning incense in the sanctuary and closes with the disciples there, praising God. The first two chapters, full of angels and songs, set the births of John the Baptist and of Jesus side by side. Then, after John's preaching and Jesus' baptism, the scene moves north to Galilee, to Nazareth, where Jesus grew up and is turned away, to Capernaum on the lakeshore, where he makes his base, and to the towns and villages where he teaches, heals and gathers his followers.

The great turning comes in chapter nine, when Jesus resolutely sets out for Jerusalem. For the next ten chapters Luke tells the story as one long journey toward the city. The route is hard to trace on a map, and the travelling is more a frame than an itinerary, but the direction never changes. Along the way comes much of what only Luke records: the good Samaritan, the lost sheep, the lost coin and the lost son, the rich man and Lazarus, the Pharisee and the tax collector at prayer, and Zacchaeus up a tree in Jericho.

In chapter nineteen Jesus rides down the Mount of Olives into Jerusalem, and the closing chapters follow his last days: teaching in the temple courts, the Passover meal with his disciples, his arrest, his hearings before the Jewish council, before Pilate and, in a scene only Luke records, before Herod Antipas, and his crucifixion outside the city. The final chapter brings the empty tomb, a stranger who walks with two disciples on the road to Emmaus, and Jesus' parting from his followers near Bethany.

Throughout, Luke's eye lingers on people at the edges of society: shepherds and widows, lepers and tax collectors, Samaritans, the women who follow Jesus and support him from their own means, and Gentiles like the Roman centurion at Capernaum. And he shows Jesus at prayer again and again, often on the eve of what comes next.

In our Bibles Luke stands third among the four gospels, and John's gospel separates it from its sequel. But Luke meant the two volumes to be read together. The story that ends here, with the disciples waiting in Jerusalem for power from on high, will begin again in Acts at exactly that point.

=== 1
Luke opens as a cultured historian of his day would, with a single polished sentence addressed to Theophilus. Then the style changes, and we are in a world that sounds like the Old Testament: an elderly priest and his wife, upright and childless, in the days of Herod king of Judea. This is Herod the Great, who will die not long after the births this chapter announces.

Zechariah belongs to one of the priestly divisions that took turns serving in the temple. Chosen by lot to burn incense in the holy place, an honour a priest might receive only once in his life, he finds an angel standing beside the altar.

Six months later the same messenger, Gabriel, is sent north to Nazareth, an obscure village in Galilee, to a young woman named Mary, pledged to marry Joseph, a man of David's line. Luke weaves the two announcements together, and then brings the two expectant mothers, who are relatives, face to face in the hill country of Judah. Before the chapter ends two songs are sung, Mary's and Zechariah's, both steeped in the language of Israel's psalms and prophets.

=== 2
John has been born and named, and Zechariah's tongue has been loosed. Now Luke widens the frame to the whole empire. Far away in Rome, Caesar Augustus orders a census, and the order reaches all the way to a carpenter's household in Nazareth. Joseph must travel south with Mary to Bethlehem, the town of David, a journey of several days. Historians have long discussed how this census fits with what is known of Quirinius, governor of Syria, and the question is not fully settled.

The birth itself is told in a few plain sentences. The announcement goes not to the powerful but to shepherds in the fields outside the town at night.

Forty days later, as the law of Moses required, the family comes up to the temple in Jerusalem. Their offering of two birds was the one the law allowed for those who could not afford a lamb. There two elderly people are waiting, Simeon and the prophetess Anna, and both recognise the child. The chapter closes with the only story the gospels tell of Jesus' boyhood, a Passover visit to Jerusalem when he is twelve years old.

=== 3
Some eighteen years have passed since the boy Jesus sat among the teachers in the temple. Luke marks the moment like a historian, with a row of rulers: the fifteenth year of the emperor Tiberius, which falls in the late twenties of the first century; Pontius Pilate governing Judea; Herod Antipas, a son of Herod the Great, ruling Galilee; Annas and Caiaphas holding the high priesthood. Annas, a former high priest, still held great influence through his son-in-law Caiaphas.

Into that world, and past all those names, the word of God comes to John, Zechariah's son, in the wilderness. He preaches by the Jordan, and crowds come out to him, including tax collectors and soldiers, each asking what they should do. Luke follows John's story forward to his imprisonment by Herod before turning to the baptism of Jesus.

Then Luke pauses to give Jesus' family line. Matthew's gospel traces Jesus' ancestry forward from Abraham; Luke traces it backward, past David and Abraham, all the way to Adam, the son of God. The two lists differ in the generations after David, and readers since early times have suggested ways of reconciling them.

=== 4
Jesus has been baptised in the Jordan, and the voice from heaven has called him beloved Son. Now, full of the Holy Spirit, he is led into the wilderness for forty days, and there the devil comes to him with three temptations. Each one is answered with words from Deuteronomy, the book of Israel's own wilderness years.

Then Jesus returns to Galilee, and Luke takes us to the synagogue in Nazareth. Luke places this scene at the very start of the ministry, as a kind of opening statement, though Jesus' own words show that he has already been at work in Capernaum. He is handed the scroll of Isaiah, and he reads a passage about one anointed by the Spirit to bring good news to the poor. The warm welcome turns to fury.

The rest of the chapter moves to Capernaum, a fishing town on the northern shore of the Sea of Galilee, which becomes his base. There, on a single Sabbath, he teaches in the synagogue, frees a man from a demon, and heals the mother-in-law of a man named Simon, of whom we will soon hear much more.

=== 5
Jesus has been teaching and healing around Capernaum, and the crowds are growing. Now he is standing by the Lake of Gennesaret, Luke's name for the Sea of Galilee, and the people are pressing so close that he climbs into a fishing boat to speak to them. The boat belongs to Simon, whose household Jesus has already visited. What happens after the teaching changes Simon's life, and the lives of his partners James and John, the sons of Zebedee.

The chapter then gathers a series of encounters. A man with leprosy, cut off from ordinary life by his disease, kneels before Jesus. Then, for the first time in Luke, the Pharisees appear, a movement of devout laymen known for their strict keeping of the law, together with teachers of the law who have come from as far as Jerusalem. They are present when a paralysed man is lowered through a roof, and they hear Jesus say something only God can say.

Last comes Levi, a tax collector, one of the toll collectors widely despised as collaborators and cheats. Jesus calls him, and then joins him at a crowded banquet, and the questions begin.

=== 6
The criticism that began at Levi's banquet now sharpens. Two Sabbath incidents follow one another. In the first, Jesus' disciples pick and eat grain as they walk through the fields, and some Pharisees object. In the second, in a synagogue, the scribes and Pharisees watch to see whether Jesus will heal a man with a withered hand on the holy day. By the end of that scene they are discussing what might be done about him.

Then Luke shows Jesus withdrawing to a mountain and praying through the night. In the morning he chooses twelve of his disciples and names them apostles. The list is given in full, and it ends, ominously, with Judas Iscariot.

Coming down to a level place, Jesus finds a great crowd gathered from Judea and Jerusalem and from the coastal cities of Tyre and Sidon. What follows is Luke's account of the sermon that Matthew sets on a mountain. Luke's version is shorter, and it has its own shape: four blessings answered by four woes, followed by a demanding call to love enemies, to show mercy and to build on a sure foundation.

=== 7
The sermon is over, and Jesus returns to Capernaum. The first visitor in this chapter is not a Jew at all but a Roman centurion, a Gentile officer who has won the respect of the local Jewish elders and even built their synagogue. His request about a dying servant, sent through others, prompts Jesus' remark that he has not found such faith in Israel.

Then the scene moves a day's walk south-west to Nain, a small town in the hills of lower Galilee. At its gate Jesus meets a funeral procession: a widow following the body of her only son.

News of all this reaches John the Baptist, still in Herod's prison, according to the historian Josephus in the fortress of Machaerus east of the Dead Sea. John sends two of his disciples with a searching question, and when they leave, Jesus speaks to the crowd about John. The chapter ends at a dinner in the house of a Pharisee named Simon, where a woman known in the town as a sinner comes in with a jar of perfume and kneels weeping at Jesus' feet.

=== 8
Luke opens this chapter with a glimpse of how Jesus' travelling company is made up and supported. With the Twelve go a number of women who have been healed, among them Mary called Magdalene, probably after Magdala, a town on the western shore of the lake, and Joanna, whose husband manages Herod's household. They provide for the group from their own means. Some of these women will be standing at the cross and at the empty tomb.

Then Jesus tells the parable of the sower and explains it to his disciples, and he speaks about the light that cannot be hidden and how people listen.

The second half of the chapter is a sequence of mighty works around the Sea of Galilee. A storm breaks over the boat as Jesus sleeps. On the far, eastern shore, in Gentile territory where pigs are herded, he meets a man tormented by many demons and living among the tombs. Back on the western side, a synagogue leader named Jairus begs him to come to his dying daughter, and on the way a woman who has been ill for twelve years reaches out to touch his cloak.

=== 9
This chapter is the hinge of Luke's gospel. It gathers up the ministry in Galilee and turns it, at the end, toward Jerusalem.

First Jesus sends the Twelve out on their own to preach and heal. Their mission troubles Herod Antipas, who has already beheaded John and now wonders who this man can be. When the apostles return, Jesus withdraws with them toward Bethsaida, near the northern end of the lake, and there five thousand people are fed.

Then come three moments close together. Jesus asks his disciples who they say he is, and Peter answers, the Christ of God. For the first time Jesus tells them plainly that he must suffer, be killed and be raised. About a week later, on a mountain, Peter, James and John see him transfigured, talking with Moses and Elijah about the departure he will accomplish at Jerusalem. The word Luke uses for departure is exodus.

Near the end of the chapter comes the sentence that shapes the next ten: as the time of his being taken up approaches, Jesus resolutely sets out for Jerusalem. Almost at once a Samaritan village refuses to receive him, and the long road south begins.

=== 10
Jesus is now on the road to Jerusalem, and the chapter opens with a wider mission than the one in chapter nine. Seventy-two others are sent ahead in pairs to prepare the towns he means to visit, with sober warnings about the places that have seen his works in Galilee and turned away, Chorazin, Bethsaida and even Capernaum. They come back full of joy.

Then an expert in the law stands up to test Jesus with a question about eternal life, and follows it with another: who is my neighbour? Jesus answers with a story set on the road from Jerusalem down to Jericho, a lonely route that drops more than three thousand feet through desert hills in under twenty miles and was known for its robbers. Its hero is a Samaritan, and Jews and Samaritans had been estranged for centuries, divided over their ancestry, their temple and their scriptures.

The chapter ends in the home of two sisters, Martha and Mary. Luke leaves the village unnamed; John's gospel places them at Bethany, close to Jerusalem.

=== 11
The journey continues, and Luke shows Jesus at prayer once again. When he finishes, one of his disciples asks him to teach them to pray, as John the Baptist taught his own followers. The answer is Luke's version of the Lord's Prayer, shorter than the one most people know from Matthew, followed by a story about a friend knocking at midnight.

Then the mood darkens. Jesus frees a man who could not speak, and some in the crowd claim that he is driving out demons by the power of Beelzebul, the prince of demons. Others demand a sign from heaven. Jesus answers both groups, and he speaks of the only sign this generation will be given, the sign of Jonah, with the Queen of the South and the people of Nineveh as witnesses against it.

The chapter closes over a meal. A Pharisee invites Jesus to dine, and is surprised that he does not first perform the customary ritual washing. What Jesus says at that table is a series of woes, and when he leaves the house the opposition has become open and bitter.

=== 12
The confrontation at the Pharisee's table is barely over when a crowd of many thousands gathers, so large that people are trampling one another. Jesus speaks first to his disciples, and much of this chapter is his teaching to them, with the crowd listening in.

It begins with warnings about the hypocrisy of the Pharisees and about whom to fear, along with the assurance that God does not forget even a sparrow. Then a man in the crowd asks Jesus to settle a quarrel over an inheritance. Jesus refuses to act as judge, and instead tells the parable of a rich farmer planning bigger barns. That leads into teaching about anxiety and possessions, the ravens and the lilies, and treasure in heaven.

The second half looks ahead. Jesus tells of servants waiting through the night for their master to come home, and of a thief who comes when least expected. Jesus then speaks of the fire he has come to kindle and of a baptism he must undergo, words that look toward Jerusalem. He ends by rebuking the crowd for reading the weather so well and the times so poorly.

=== 13
Jesus is still teaching on the road when some in the crowd bring him news: Galileans killed by Pilate while they were offering their sacrifices. The incident is known only from this passage, though it fits what other sources say of Pilate's harshness. Jesus adds a second recent disaster, eighteen people killed when a tower collapsed at Siloam in Jerusalem, and uses both to call his hearers to repentance. A parable about a fruitless fig tree, given one more year, follows.

Then comes another Sabbath healing, the last Luke records in a synagogue: a woman bent double for eighteen years, and an indignant synagogue leader. Two short parables of the kingdom follow, the mustard seed and the yeast.

Luke reminds us that Jesus is making his way toward Jerusalem, and the closing scenes turn on that city. Someone asks whether only a few will be saved. Then some Pharisees warn Jesus that Herod Antipas wants to kill him, which suggests he is still within Herod's lands, in Galilee or in Perea beyond the Jordan. His reply, and his lament over Jerusalem that follows, show that he knows exactly where the road ends.

=== 14
For the third time in Luke's gospel, Jesus is a guest at a Pharisee's table. This time it is the home of a leading Pharisee, on a Sabbath, and the other guests are watching him closely. Before the meal begins, a man suffering from dropsy, a painful swelling of the body, is standing in front of him, and the old question of healing on the Sabbath is raised once more, this time by Jesus himself.

The first half of the chapter is table talk. Dinners in that world were carefully graded occasions, and the seat a guest took showed his rank. Jesus watches the guests jostle for the places of honour and has something to say to them, and then something to the host about whom he invites. When another guest makes a pious remark about the feast in the kingdom of God, Jesus answers with a parable about a great banquet and the excuses of those invited.

Then the scene opens out to the road again. Large crowds are travelling with Jesus, and he turns to them with some of the hardest words in the gospel about what it costs to follow him.

=== 15
The crowds following Jesus now include many tax collectors and others whom respectable people called sinners, and they are drawing close to hear him. The Pharisees and scribes look on and grumble that he welcomes such people and even eats with them. To share a meal was a sign of acceptance, and that is what offends them.

This one complaint is the setting for the whole chapter. Jesus answers it with three parables, each about something lost and then found, and each ending in celebration. The first two are brief: a shepherd with a hundred sheep and a woman with ten silver coins.

The third, often called the parable of the prodigal son, is the longest and most loved of all the parables in Luke. In the world of Jesus' hearers, a younger son asking for his inheritance while his father was alive would have seemed a deep insult, and a Jew reduced to feeding pigs, unclean animals under the law, could hardly sink lower. But the story does not end with the younger son. It ends with his older brother standing outside, angry, refusing to come in, and the grumbling Pharisees would have known whom he resembled.

=== 16
Jesus has just answered the Pharisees with the parables of the lost. Now he turns to his disciples, and the subject becomes money.

The chapter opens with one of the most puzzling parables in the gospels, the story of a dishonest manager who is about to lose his position and quickly reduces the debts owed to his master, so that he will have friends when he is out of work. The master commends him for his shrewdness. Jesus draws out a series of sayings about faithfulness with wealth, ending with the warning that no one can serve both God and money.

Luke tells us the Pharisees loved money, and they scoff. Jesus answers with a few brief sayings about the law and about divorce, and then with a second story. A rich man feasts in splendour every day, while at his gate lies a beggar covered with sores. This is the only parable in which Jesus gives one of the characters a name, Lazarus. When both men die, the scene moves beyond the grave, and the story closes with a sentence that will echo once the gospel reaches its final chapter.

=== 17
The teaching on the road continues, and this chapter turns back to the disciples. It opens with short sayings: on causing others to stumble, on forgiving a brother again and again, on faith as small as a mustard seed, and on a servant who has simply done his duty.

Then Luke reminds us of the journey. Jesus is on his way to Jerusalem, travelling along the borderland between Samaria and Galilee. At the edge of a village ten men with leprosy call to him from a distance, as the law required them to keep apart. He sends them to show themselves to the priests, who alone could declare a person clean. Only one comes back, and Luke saves until last the detail that matters: he is a Samaritan.

The rest of the chapter answers a question from the Pharisees about when the kingdom of God will come. Jesus speaks of the kingdom already among them, and then, to his disciples, of the day when the Son of Man will be revealed, as sudden as lightning and as unexpected as the flood in Noah's day or the fire that fell on Sodom.

=== 18
Jesus has just been speaking about the day of the Son of Man, and he follows it with two parables about prayer. The first is about a widow with no one to defend her, who keeps coming back to a judge who cares nothing for God or people. The second is set in the temple courts in Jerusalem, where two men go up to pray, a Pharisee and a tax collector.

At this point Luke's account rejoins the material shared with Matthew and Mark, and familiar scenes follow one another: little children brought to Jesus, and a wealthy ruler asking what he must do to inherit eternal life.

Then Jesus takes the Twelve aside and tells them, for the third and plainest time, what is waiting in Jerusalem. He will be handed over to the Gentiles, mocked, flogged and killed, and on the third day he will rise. They understand none of it.

As Jesus nears Jericho, an oasis town low in the Jordan valley some fifteen miles from Jerusalem, a blind beggar hears the crowd go by and begins to shout.

=== 19
Jesus is passing through Jericho, a wealthy town of palms and balsam on a busy route, and a natural place for collecting taxes. Zacchaeus, a chief tax collector and a rich man, is the last of the outsiders Jesus meets on the road.

While the crowd listens, Jesus tells a parable, because they are near Jerusalem and many expect the kingdom of God to appear at once. It concerns a nobleman who travels to a distant country to be made king, and whose subjects send a delegation after him to say they do not want him. His hearers may have remembered how Archelaus, a son of Herod the Great, had gone to Rome to have his rule confirmed while a delegation of his own people followed to oppose him.

Then the journey that began in chapter nine reaches its end. Jesus climbs the road to the villages of Bethphage and Bethany on the Mount of Olives, the ridge east of the city, and sends two disciples to fetch a colt. As he rides down toward the city, his followers break into praise. What he does when Jerusalem comes into view, and then in the temple courts, opens the last week of his life.

=== 20
Jesus is now teaching daily in the temple courts, and the leaders of Jerusalem, the chief priests, scribes and elders, have resolved to destroy him. They cannot simply seize him, because the people are hanging on his words. So they try another way: a series of questions, put in public, designed to discredit him.

The first is a direct challenge. By what authority is he doing these things, the clearing of the temple above all? Jesus answers with a question about John the Baptist, and then tells the people a parable about tenants in a vineyard, which the leaders understand at once is aimed at them.

Next come spies, who ask whether it is right to pay taxes to Caesar. The question is a trap. To say yes would anger many who resented Roman rule; to say no would give grounds to report him to the governor. Then some Sadducees approach, a party linked with the priestly aristocracy, who denied that there would be any resurrection. Their question about a woman married to seven brothers is meant to make the idea look absurd.

When the questions stop, Jesus asks one of his own, about a verse in the Psalms, and warns the crowd about the scribes.

=== 21
Jesus is still in the temple courts. Near the treasury, where worshippers placed their offerings, he watches the wealthy giving their gifts, and then a poor widow who puts in two small copper coins.

Then some of the disciples begin to admire the temple itself. Herod's rebuilding had made it one of the wonders of the eastern Mediterranean, with vast pale stones and gifts dedicated by the wealthy, and the work was still going on. Jesus tells them that not one stone will be left on another. Their question, when and with what signs, opens the long discourse that fills the rest of the chapter.

Jesus speaks of deceivers and wars, of the persecution his followers will face before kings and governors, and of Jerusalem surrounded by armies. About forty years later, in the year seventy, Roman legions besieged the city and destroyed the temple. Beyond that, the discourse looks further still, to signs in the heavens and the coming of the Son of Man.

Luke closes the chapter with a picture of these days: teaching in the temple from early morning, and each night spent out on the Mount of Olives.

=== 22
The days of teaching in the temple are over. It is Passover time, and Jerusalem is crowded with pilgrims. The leaders want Jesus dead, but they fear the crowds, and they need a way to take him quietly. Judas Iscariot, one of the Twelve, gives them one.

Jesus sends Peter and John into the city to prepare the Passover meal in a large upper room. Luke's account of the evening includes a dispute among the disciples, at the very table, over which of them is the greatest, and Jesus' words to Simon that Satan has asked to sift them like wheat.

After the meal Jesus goes out, as he has done each night, to the Mount of Olives. There he prays in anguish, and there Judas arrives with a crowd. Jesus is taken to the house of the high priest, and Peter follows at a distance into the courtyard. At daybreak the council, the Sanhedrin, meets to question Jesus. It is the supreme Jewish court, but under Roman rule it could not carry out an execution, and so the next step must be the governor.

=== 23
It is early morning. The council has heard Jesus' answer, and now the whole body brings him to Pontius Pilate, the Roman governor, who normally governed from Caesarea on the coast but came up to Jerusalem at festival time. Before Pilate the charge is changed into something Rome must take seriously: that Jesus forbids paying taxes to Caesar and claims to be a king.

Luke alone tells how Pilate, hearing that Jesus is a Galilean, sends him to Herod Antipas, who is also in Jerusalem for the feast and has long wanted to see Jesus. Three times Pilate declares Jesus not guilty, and each time the crowd shouts him down.

A man from Cyrene in North Africa is pressed into carrying the cross, and women of Jerusalem follow, weeping. At the place called The Skull, Jesus is crucified between two criminals. Luke records words spoken from the cross that appear in no other gospel. From about noon until three in the afternoon, darkness covers the land.

The chapter ends quietly, with a member of the council who had opposed its decision, Joseph from the Judean town of Arimathea, and with the women from Galilee watching where the body is laid as the Sabbath begins.

=== 24
The Sabbath has passed. Very early on the first day of the week, the women who saw where Jesus was buried return to the tomb with the spices they have prepared. Among them are Mary Magdalene and Joanna, who have followed him since Galilee.

Most of the chapter belongs to a single day. After the scene at the tomb comes one of the best-loved stories in the gospels, found only in Luke. That afternoon two disciples, one of them named Cleopas, are walking home to a village called Emmaus, about seven miles from Jerusalem. A stranger joins them on the road.

By evening they are back in Jerusalem with the Eleven, and Jesus appears among them. He shows them his hands and feet, eats in front of them, and opens the Scriptures to them. He tells them to wait in the city until they are clothed with power from on high. Then he leads them out toward Bethany and is carried up into heaven.

Luke ends where he began, in the temple, with people praising God. He tells these last events as if on one day; at the start of Acts he will explain that Jesus appeared to his followers over forty days, and take up the story from there.
