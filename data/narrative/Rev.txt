=== intro
The last book of the Bible takes its name from its first word. In Greek it is apokalypsis, an unveiling, the drawing back of a curtain, and the book calls itself the revelation of Jesus Christ. It is the record of a series of visions given to a man named John, who tells us exactly where he was when they came. He was on Patmos, a small, rocky island in the Aegean Sea, off the western coast of what is now Turkey, a short sea journey from the great city of Ephesus. He was there, he says, because of the word of God and his testimony about Jesus.

John calls himself simply your brother and partner in the tribulation. He is the apostle John, the same John who wrote the Gospel and the three letters, living out his old age among the churches of Asia. His readers knew him and trusted him. John saw these visions in the reign of the emperor Domitian, in the nineties of the first century, as the early writer Irenaeus records.

The book was sent to seven churches in the Roman province of Asia: Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamum, Thyatira, Sardis, Philadelphia and Laodicea. They lie on a rough circle of roads, and a messenger setting out from Ephesus could have visited them in exactly the order they are named. These were prosperous cities, proud of their temples, their trade and their loyalty to Rome. Several of them had temples dedicated to the emperor, and public life, from civic festivals to the meetings of the trade guilds, was bound up with honouring the gods and the emperor. For believers, refusing to take part could mean suspicion, lost business, hostility from neighbours, and sometimes death. Some of the churches were suffering. Others had grown comfortable and were quietly making their peace with the world around them. Revelation speaks to both.

The book is a letter, a prophecy and an apocalypse all at once. It opens and closes like a letter, it calls itself prophecy, and its central chapters belong to a kind of Jewish writing known as apocalyptic, which describes heavenly realities and the end of history through visions, symbols and numbers, often explained by an angel. The book of Daniel is the great Old Testament example, alongside parts of Ezekiel and Zechariah. Revelation's imagery comes almost entirely from the Scriptures of Israel. It rarely quotes them directly, but it echoes them on nearly every page: the plagues of Egypt, the tabernacle and temple, the visions of Isaiah, Ezekiel and Daniel, the fall of Babylon, the garden of Eden. Numbers carry meaning. Seven, the number of completeness, runs through the whole book. John is told to write what he sees, and the book is meant to be heard: it begins with a blessing on the one who reads it aloud to the congregation, and on those who hear.

Its visions come in a clear order. First John sees the risen Christ standing among seven lampstands, and hears seven messages, one to each church. Then a door opens in heaven, and John is taken up into the throne room of God, where a Lamb who has been slain takes a sealed scroll. As its seven seals are broken, judgments are released on the earth. Seven trumpets follow, and then, after a pause, the story steps back to show the conflict behind it all: a woman, a dragon and two terrible beasts. Seven bowls of wrath are poured out. The great city called Babylon falls, and its kings and merchants mourn her. Then heaven opens, and Christ rides out as a conqueror. There is a thousand-year reign, a final judgment, and at last a new heaven and a new earth, with the holy city coming down from God. Each series of seven has its own pattern, and between the sixth and seventh of the seals and the trumpets come pauses, scenes that hold back the end for a moment.

Revelation stands at the end of the Bible, and it is full of endings. The tree of life, last seen in the garden in Genesis, appears again beside the river of the city of God. The story that began with God creating the heavens and the earth closes with God making all things new.

=== 1
The book opens with its own title page. This is the revelation of Jesus Christ, given by God, passed through an angel to John, and written down as a prophecy for the churches. A blessing rests on the one who reads it aloud, and on those who hear.

Then comes a letter's greeting from John to the seven churches of Asia, and a burst of praise to Christ. God speaks, calling himself the Alpha and the Omega, the first and last letters of the Greek alphabet.

Now John explains where he is. He is on Patmos, on the Lord's day, when he hears a voice behind him like a trumpet. Turning, he sees seven golden lampstands, and among them a figure like a son of man, the title from Daniel's vision of one who receives an everlasting kingdom. His hair is white as wool, his eyes blaze like fire, his voice roars like many waters, and a sword comes from his mouth. John falls at his feet as though dead. The risen Christ lays a hand on him, tells him not to be afraid, and explains part of what he has seen.

=== 2
The figure John saw among the lampstands now speaks to the seven churches in turn, beginning with four of them. Each message opens with a title drawn from the vision in chapter one, offers words of praise or rebuke or both, and ends with a promise to the one who overcomes.

First comes Ephesus, the great port and capital of the province, home of the famous temple of Artemis, where Paul had once spent some three years. The church there has tested false teachers and endured, but it has abandoned its first love. Next is Smyrna, a proud city along the coast to the north, where believers are poor and slandered and about to face prison. They receive no rebuke at all.

Pergamum follows, a city of temples and the oldest centre of emperor worship in the province, described as the place where Satan has his throne. A believer named Antipas has died there for his faith, yet some in the church have accepted teaching that allows eating food sacrificed to idols. Last in this chapter is Thyatira, a city of trade guilds, where a woman the message calls Jezebel, after the idolatrous queen of Israel, is leading believers astray.

=== 3
The messages continue with the last three churches. Sardis had once been the capital of the ancient kingdom of Lydia, with a citadel that seemed impregnable, yet twice in its history it had fallen to enemies who climbed the cliffs while its guards were not watching. The church there has a reputation for being alive, but it is dead, and Christ warns that he will come like a thief. A few have not soiled their garments.

Philadelphia, a smaller city often shaken by earthquakes, receives only encouragement. The church has little strength but has kept Christ's word, and he has set before it an open door that no one can shut.

The last message goes to Laodicea, a wealthy banking centre, known for its black wool and for a medical school with a famous eye salve. Its water came by aqueduct and arrived lukewarm. The church is like its water, neither hot nor cold. It says it is rich and needs nothing, and Christ answers it in the language of its own trades. He is standing at the door, knocking.

=== 4
With the seven messages delivered, the scene changes completely. John sees a door standing open in heaven, and the trumpet-like voice from chapter one calls him to come up and see what must happen after these things.

What he sees first is a throne, and someone seated on it. John does not describe the one on the throne except by the colours of precious stones and a rainbow like an emerald around him. Around the throne sit twenty-four elders on their own thrones, robed in white and crowned with gold. Lightning and thunder come from the throne, seven torches burn before it, and a sea of glass stretches out in front of it.

Closest to the throne are four living creatures, like a lion, a calf, a man and an eagle, covered with eyes and with six wings each. They recall the creatures in Ezekiel's vision and the seraphim Isaiah saw in the temple, and like Isaiah's seraphim they cry holy, holy, holy. The elders respond by casting their crowns before the throne and praising God as Creator. Nothing has yet happened on earth, but John has been shown from where everything that follows will be governed.

=== 5
John is still in the throne room, and now he notices something in the right hand of the one seated on the throne: a scroll, written on both sides and sealed with seven seals. A mighty angel asks who is worthy to break the seals and open it, and no one in heaven or on earth or under the earth can be found. John begins to weep.

One of the elders tells him to stop, because the Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Root of David, has triumphed and can open the scroll. Both titles come from old promises about the royal line of Israel. But when John looks, he does not see a lion. He sees a Lamb standing in the centre of the throne, looking as though it had been slain. That single moment shapes the whole book.

The Lamb takes the scroll, and the throne room erupts into worship. The living creatures and elders sing a new song, praising the Lamb because by his blood he has purchased people from every tribe and nation. Then the circle widens to countless angels, and finally to every creature in all creation.

=== 6
The Lamb now begins to open the seals of the scroll, one by one, and with each seal something is released on the earth. The first four bring out four horsemen, each summoned by one of the living creatures with a single word: come.

Horses of different colours had appeared in the visions of the prophet Zechariah. A rider on a white horse goes out to conquer. A rider on a red horse takes peace from the earth. A rider on a black horse carries scales, and a voice announces prices so high that a whole day's wage would buy only a day's food. The fourth, on a pale horse, is named Death, and Hades follows him.

The fifth seal shows something different: the souls of those killed for their testimony, gathered under the altar in heaven, crying out how long until God judges. They are given white robes and told to wait a little longer. The sixth seal shakes the whole creation in language taken from the prophets, sun darkened, moon like blood, stars falling. People of every rank hide among the rocks, and the chapter ends with their question: who is able to withstand it?

=== 7
Six seals have been opened, and the seventh is held back while the question at the end of chapter six is answered. John sees four angels at the four corners of the earth holding back its winds, until God's servants have been marked with a seal on their foreheads. Ezekiel had seen something similar, a man with a writing case marking the foreheads of the faithful before Jerusalem was judged.

John hears the number of those sealed: one hundred and forty-four thousand, twelve thousand from each of the tribes of Israel, and the list is given tribe by tribe. Then, as in chapter five, where he heard of a lion and turned to see a lamb, what he sees is different from what he heard. Before the throne stands a multitude too large to count, from every nation and people and language.

They wear white robes and hold palm branches, like worshippers at the great feasts of Israel. One of the elders explains who they are, and the chapter ends with words gathered from Isaiah about the Lamb as their shepherd, and God wiping away every tear.

=== 8
At last the Lamb opens the seventh seal, and there is silence in heaven for about half an hour. After all the singing of the earlier chapters, the stillness is striking. John sees seven angels standing before God, and each is given a trumpet.

Before they sound, another angel stands at the golden altar with a censer. Incense rises before God together with the prayers of the saints. Then the angel fills the censer with fire from the altar and hurls it to the earth, and thunder and an earthquake follow.

The first four trumpets sound in quick succession, and each one strikes a third of creation: the land and trees, the sea and its ships, the rivers and springs, and the lights of the sky. The hail and fire, the waters turned to blood and the darkness are the plagues of Egypt in the time of Moses, now falling on a wider world. These are severe, but still partial. The chapter ends with an eagle flying overhead, crying woe three times for the trumpets still to come.

=== 9
The eagle's cry of woe is now explained. The fifth and sixth trumpets are the first two of three woes, and they are darker than anything that has come before.

At the fifth trumpet a star fallen from heaven is given the key to the Abyss, the bottomless pit, and from its smoke pour out locusts unlike any on earth: shaped like war horses, with human faces, lions' teeth and scorpions' stings. They are forbidden to touch those who carry God's seal. Locusts were one of the plagues of Egypt, and the prophet Joel had described a locust swarm as an invading army. Their king is the angel of the Abyss, whose name in Hebrew and in Greek means destruction and destroyer.

The sixth trumpet releases four angels bound at the Euphrates. For John's first hearers, that river was the eastern frontier of the Roman world, beyond which lay the feared horsemen of Parthia. A vast cavalry rides out, with horses breathing fire, smoke and sulphur. The chapter closes with the survivors, who still refuse to turn from their idols, like Pharaoh hardening his heart.

=== 10
Six trumpets have sounded, and as with the seals, there is a pause before the seventh. A mighty angel comes down from heaven, wrapped in a cloud, with a rainbow over his head and a face like the sun. He sets one foot on the sea and one on the land, and in his hand is a small scroll lying open.

When he shouts, seven thunders speak. John is about to write down what they said, but a voice from heaven tells him to seal it up. Then the angel raises his hand and swears by the Creator that there will be no more delay. When the seventh trumpet sounds, the mystery of God, announced to the prophets, will be fulfilled.

Before that happens, John is given a task of his own. He is told to take the little scroll and eat it. Centuries earlier, Ezekiel had been given a scroll to eat at his call, and had found it sweet as honey. John finds the same sweetness, but it turns bitter in his stomach. He is told that he must prophesy again, about many peoples, nations, languages and kings.

=== 11
The pause before the seventh trumpet continues. John is handed a measuring rod and told to measure the temple of God and its worshippers, but to leave out the outer court, which is given over to the nations to trample the holy city for forty-two months. The same period appears as twelve hundred and sixty days, three and a half years, half of seven, a period drawn from Daniel's visions.

Two witnesses then prophesy, dressed in sackcloth. They are called the two olive trees and the two lampstands, an image from Zechariah, and their powers recall Elijah, who shut the sky, and Moses, who turned water to blood. When their testimony is complete, a beast from the Abyss, mentioned here for the first time, kills them, and their bodies lie in the street of the great city where their Lord was crucified. After three and a half days God raises them.

Then the seventh trumpet sounds at last, and voices in heaven declare that the kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ.

=== 12
The seventh trumpet has sounded, but now the book steps back to show the conflict behind everything John has seen. A great sign appears in heaven: a woman clothed with the sun, the moon under her feet, and a crown of twelve stars, an image reminiscent of Joseph's dream of the sun, moon and stars bowing to him. She is about to give birth. Facing her is a huge red dragon, with seven heads and ten horns, waiting to devour her child.

The child is a son who will rule the nations with an iron sceptre, the words of the second psalm about God's anointed king. He is caught up to God's throne, and the woman flees into the wilderness.

War breaks out in heaven. The archangel Michael, known from Daniel, defeats the dragon, who is now named plainly as the ancient serpent, the devil and Satan, the serpent of the garden in Genesis. He is thrown down to earth, furious, knowing his time is short, and he pursues the woman. She is given the wings of a great eagle to fly to her place in the wilderness, and the earth itself swallows the river the dragon pours out after her. Enraged, he goes off to make war on the rest of her children, and the chapter ends with the dragon standing on the shore of the sea.

=== 13
From the shore where the dragon waits, John sees a beast rising out of the sea, with ten horns and seven heads. It looks like a leopard, with a bear's feet and a lion's mouth, combining the features of the four beasts Daniel saw rising from the sea, each of them an empire. The dragon gives it his power and throne. One of its heads seems to have received a fatal wound that has healed, and the whole world follows it in amazement.

Then a second beast rises from the earth. It has horns like a lamb but speaks like a dragon, and its work is to make the world worship the first beast. It performs signs, sets up an image that speaks, and requires everyone to receive a mark on hand or forehead before they can buy or sell.

The chapter ends with a riddle and a call for wisdom: the number of the beast is the number of a man, six hundred and sixty-six. In Hebrew and Greek, letters also served as numbers.

=== 14
After the terror of the two beasts, John sees something very different. The Lamb stands on Mount Zion, the hill of Jerusalem, and with him the one hundred and forty-four thousand last seen in chapter seven. Where the beast's followers wear its mark, these bear the name of the Lamb and his Father on their foreheads, and they sing a new song that only they can learn.

Three angels follow, flying through the sky with messages for the whole earth. The first calls every nation to fear God and worship the Creator. The second announces that Babylon the great has fallen, the first time that city is named in the book. The third warns that anyone who worships the beast and receives its mark will drink the wine of God's wrath. A voice from heaven pronounces a blessing on those who die in the Lord.

The chapter closes with two harvests. One like a son of man, seated on a white cloud, swings a sickle over the earth. Then an angel gathers the grapes of the earth into the winepress of God's wrath, an image taken from the prophet Joel, and the blood flows for nearly two hundred miles.

=== 15
This is the shortest chapter in Revelation, and it prepares for the last series of seven. John sees another sign in heaven: seven angels holding the seven last plagues, with which the wrath of God is completed.

Before the plagues are poured out, he sees again the sea of glass from the throne room, now mixed with fire, and beside it stand those who have conquered the beast. They hold harps and sing the song of Moses and of the Lamb. The scene deliberately recalls the shore of the Red Sea, where Moses and the Israelites sang after God brought them through the waters and overthrew Pharaoh's army. Their song, woven from phrases in the Psalms and the prophets, praises God as King of the nations.

Now the heavenly temple, called the tabernacle of the testimony, is opened. The seven angels come out dressed in bright linen, and one of the living creatures hands them seven golden bowls full of the wrath of God. The temple fills with smoke from his glory, as the tabernacle and Solomon's temple once filled with the glory of the Lord, and no one can enter until the plagues are complete.

=== 16
A loud voice from the temple sends the seven angels out, and the bowls are poured, one after another, with no pause between them. Their targets echo the trumpets of chapters eight and nine, the land, the sea, the rivers, the sun, but this time the judgment is not limited to a third. Several are plagues of Egypt once more: painful sores, water turned to blood, darkness.

After the third bowl the angel in charge of the waters declares God's judgments just, and the altar itself replies. Yet those who suffer curse God and refuse to repent.

The sixth bowl dries up the Euphrates for the kings of the east, and three unclean spirits like frogs come from the mouths of the dragon, the beast and the false prophet, the name now given to the second beast. They gather the kings of the world to a place called in Hebrew Armageddon, the mountain of Megiddo, a city overlooking the great valley in northern Israel where many battles had been fought. Then the seventh bowl is poured into the air, a voice from the throne cries it is done, and the great city of Babylon is split apart.

=== 17
The seventh bowl ended with Babylon. Now one of the angels who poured the bowls takes John aside to show him her judgment.

John is carried into a wilderness, where he sees a woman sitting on a scarlet beast with seven heads and ten horns. She is dressed in purple and scarlet, glittering with gold, and holding a golden cup full of abominations. On her forehead is written Babylon the great, the mother of prostitutes. She is drunk with the blood of God's people. The prophets of Israel had used the image of a prostitute for proud and corrupt cities, among them Nineveh and Tyre.

The angel explains what John has seen. The seven heads are seven mountains on which the woman sits, and also seven kings, five fallen, one reigning, one still to come. The ten horns are kings who will make war on the Lamb, and lose, and who will in the end turn on the woman herself. The chapter closes by naming her as the great city that rules over the kings of the earth. John's first hearers lived under Rome, the city built on seven hills.

=== 18
The fall of Babylon, announced in chapter fourteen and seen in chapter seventeen, is now mourned. The chapter is a great lament, modelled on the laments of the prophets over Babylon and over Tyre, the trading city of the ancient world.

An angel whose glory lights up the earth cries out that Babylon has fallen. Then another voice calls God's people to come out of her, echoing Jeremiah's warning to the exiles to flee ancient Babylon before its judgment.

Three groups stand at a distance and mourn: the kings who shared her luxury, the merchants who grew rich from her, and the sea captains and sailors who carried her trade. The merchants' cargo list reads like an inventory of a Roman port, from gold, silk, ivory and citron wood to cinnamon, wheat and horses, ending with the bodies and souls of slaves.

Finally a mighty angel throws a great millstone into the sea, as Jeremiah once had a scroll tied to a stone and sunk in the Euphrates. The music, the craftsmen, the lamps and the wedding voices of the city fall silent.

=== 19
After the mourning on earth comes rejoicing in heaven. A great multitude shouts hallelujah, a Hebrew word meaning praise the Lord, which appears in the New Testament only in this chapter. They praise God for his judgment on the great prostitute, and then they announce that the marriage of the Lamb has come and his bride has made herself ready. Overwhelmed, John falls at the angel's feet to worship him, and is told firmly to worship God alone.

Then heaven stands open, and John sees a rider on a white horse. He is called Faithful and True, and the Word of God. His eyes are like fire, as in the vision of chapter one, his robe is dipped in blood, and a sharp sword comes from his mouth. On his robe is written King of kings and Lord of lords. The armies of heaven follow him.

An angel standing in the sun calls the birds to a very different supper, a scene drawn from Ezekiel's prophecy against Gog. The beast and the kings of the earth gather for battle, but there is no description of the fighting. The beast and the false prophet are simply captured.

=== 20
With the beast and the false prophet gone, one enemy remains: the dragon. An angel comes down from heaven with the key to the Abyss and a great chain, seizes the dragon, the ancient serpent, and locks him away for a thousand years.

John sees thrones, and the souls of those who had been beheaded for their testimony about Jesus and had refused the mark of the beast. They come to life and reign with Christ for the thousand years. This is the only passage in the Bible that speaks of a thousand-year reign, often called the millennium.

When the thousand years end, Satan is released and gathers the nations, called Gog and Magog after the enemies in Ezekiel's prophecy, against the camp of the saints. Fire from heaven consumes them, and the devil joins the beast and the false prophet. Last of all, John sees a great white throne. The dead, great and small, stand before it, the books are opened, and even Death and Hades are thrown into the lake of fire.

=== 21
With the final judgment over, John sees a new heaven and a new earth. The first heaven and earth have passed away, and there is no longer any sea, the place from which the beast rose and, in the ancient imagination, a realm of chaos. The words echo Isaiah's promise of new heavens and a new earth.

Then John sees the holy city, the new Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven like a bride dressed for her husband, and a voice announces that God's dwelling place is now with his people. He will wipe away every tear, and death will be no more. The one on the throne speaks: behold, I make all things new.

Then, in a deliberate contrast with chapter seventeen, one of the same seven angels who showed John the prostitute city now shows him the bride. The city has twelve gates named for the tribes of Israel and twelve foundations named for the apostles. It is measured, and found to be a perfect cube, about fourteen hundred miles in each direction, the shape of the Most Holy Place in Solomon's temple. There is no temple in it, because God and the Lamb are its temple.

=== 22
The angel's tour of the city ends at its heart. A river of the water of life, clear as crystal, flows from the throne of God and of the Lamb down the middle of the main street. On each side of it grows the tree of life, bearing fruit every month, with leaves for the healing of the nations. The picture draws on the river Ezekiel saw flowing from the temple, and beyond that on the garden of Eden, where the first man and woman were barred from the tree of life. Now there is no more curse, and God's servants will see his face.

The rest of the chapter is the book's closing, and it gathers several voices: the angel, John, and Jesus himself, who says three times that he is coming soon. John falls before the angel once more and is again told to worship God. Unlike Daniel, who was told to seal up his vision, John is told not to seal his, because the time is near.

The Bible's final pages close with an invitation to the thirsty to come, a warning not to add to or take from the book, a prayer, Come, Lord Jesus, and a blessing of grace.
