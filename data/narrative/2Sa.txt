=== intro
Second Samuel opens with a messenger running from a lost battle. Saul, the first king of Israel, lies dead on Mount Gilboa, and so does his son Jonathan, David's closest friend. Far to the south, in the Philistine town of Ziklag, David has just returned from rescuing his people from an Amalekite raid. He does not yet know that the throne he was promised years before is now empty.

The listener who has come through First Samuel knows how we got here. The prophet Samuel anointed Saul as Israel's first king, and then, when Saul turned from the Lord's commands, anointed a shepherd boy from Bethlehem in his place. David killed Goliath, served at Saul's court, married Saul's daughter Michal, and became so popular that Saul tried again and again to kill him. For years David lived as a fugitive in the wilderness of Judah, gathering a band of outcasts and fighting men, twice sparing Saul's life when he could have taken it, and finally taking refuge among the Philistines themselves. Samuel has died. Now Saul is gone too, and this book tells what David does with the kingdom.

In the Hebrew Bible, First and Second Samuel were a single book. The division came later, when the work was translated into Greek and copied onto scrolls of manageable length. The book takes its name from Samuel, but he has died before this second part begins, and no author is named. Later Scripture says that David's acts were written in the records of Samuel, of the prophet Nathan and of the seer Gad, and Jewish tradition holds that these men gave us the book. The account itself has the closeness of eyewitness memory: private conversations, family quarrels, the exact words of messengers and the grief of a father.

The setting is Israel in the time around a thousand years before Christ. The great powers of the age, Egypt to the south and Assyria in the north-east, are in a period of weakness, and that leaves room for a small kingdom to grow. The tribes of Israel are still a loose alliance, with Judah in the south often standing apart from the northern tribes. The Philistines hold the coastal plain and have just crushed Saul's army. In the hill country stands a Canaanite fortress that no Israelite tribe has ever taken, a city called Jebus, which David will make his own as Jerusalem.

The book has a clear shape. In its first half David rises. He is anointed king over Judah at Hebron, while Saul's general Abner sets up Saul's surviving son Ish-bosheth over the northern tribes. After years of civil war and two political murders, all Israel comes to David. He captures Jerusalem, brings the ark of the covenant there, and receives from God, through Nathan, a promise that will run through the rest of the Bible: his house and his kingdom will endure forever. His armies defeat the surrounding nations, and the little kingdom becomes a regional power.

Then, at the height of that success, comes chapter eleven. David stays home while his army is at war, takes Bathsheba, the wife of one of his own soldiers, and arranges her husband's death. Nathan confronts him, and David repents, but the consequences are spoken over his house: the sword will never depart from it. The second half of the book follows that word as it comes true inside David's own family. One son rapes his half-sister, another son murders him, and that same son, Absalom, later drives his father out of Jerusalem in an armed rebellion. David returns to his throne, but the kingdom is shaken, and old tensions between Judah and the northern tribes break into the open.

The last four chapters step outside the main story and gather other material from David's reign: a famine and its aftermath, the deeds of his mighty warriors, a great psalm of thanksgiving, his last words, and a census that brings a plague. The book ends at a threshing floor outside Jerusalem, where David builds an altar. That ground will later become the site of the temple.

Second Samuel is unusually frank about its hero. David is brave, generous, poetic and devout, and he is also capable of terrible wrong and of weakness as a father. Several psalms carry titles that link them to moments in these chapters, and the book of First Chronicles later retells David's reign from a different angle. The story continues directly in First Kings, which opens with David old and cold in his bed, and the question of who will succeed him still unsettled.

=== 1
David is back in Ziklag, the Philistine town where he and his men have been living. Not long ago they came home to find it burned and their families carried off by Amalekite raiders, and they have only just returned from pursuing the raiders and recovering everything. Meanwhile, to the north, Saul's army has been routed on Mount Gilboa. David has heard nothing yet.

On the third day a young man arrives from the battlefield with his clothes torn and dust on his head, the signs of mourning. He bows before David and tells him that Saul and Jonathan are dead. He has a story about how Saul died, and he has brought Saul's crown and armband with him, evidently expecting a reward. The listener who remembers the end of First Samuel will notice that his account does not match what was told there.

David's response is not what the messenger expects. The chapter ends with one of the most famous laments in the Bible, a poem David composes for Saul and Jonathan and orders to be taught to the people of Judah. It was preserved, the text tells us, in an old collection called the Book of Jashar. Its refrain has passed into common speech: how the mighty have fallen.

=== 2
Saul is dead and the land is leaderless. David does not seize the moment. He asks the Lord whether he should go up to one of the towns of Judah, and he is sent to Hebron, the old city of Abraham in the southern hill country. There the men of Judah, his own tribe, anoint him king.

But Judah is not all Israel. Abner son of Ner, Saul's cousin and the commander of his army, has survived Gilboa. He takes Saul's son Ish-bosheth to Mahanaim, east of the Jordan and away from the Philistines, and makes him king over the northern and eastern tribes. The nation now has two kings.

It is not long before their forces meet. Joab, David's nephew and his commander, faces Abner's men at the pool of Gibeon, north of Jerusalem. With Joab are his two brothers, Abishai and Asahel, the sons of David's sister Zeruiah, and all three will matter a great deal in this book. What begins almost as a contest between champions turns into a running battle, and by sunset a death has taken place that will cast a long shadow over the chapters to come.

=== 3
The war between the house of Saul and the house of David drags on, and the chapter opens by noting which way it is going: David grows stronger, and Saul's house grows weaker. A list of the sons born to David in Hebron follows. Several of these names, Amnon, Absalom and Adonijah in particular, will return much later in the story, and not happily.

The real power in the north is Abner, not the king he has set up. When Ish-bosheth accuses him of sleeping with Saul's concubine Rizpah, a serious charge, since to take a king's concubine was to lay claim to his throne, Abner is furious and decides to change sides. He opens negotiations with David. David names one condition: the return of his first wife Michal, Saul's daughter, who was given to another man while David was a fugitive.

Abner begins to bring the northern elders round, and he comes to Hebron to make terms. But Joab has not forgotten what happened at Gibeon, and he does not trust Abner. The rest of the chapter shows David trying to make it plain to the whole nation where he stands.

=== 4
Abner is dead, and with him the strength of Saul's house. In Mahanaim, Ish-bosheth loses heart, and all Israel is uneasy. The chapter introduces two brothers, Rechab and Baanah, captains of raiding bands in Ish-bosheth's service, from the Benjamite town of Beeroth.

Before their story continues, the writer pauses to introduce someone else, almost in passing. Jonathan left a son named Mephibosheth. He was five years old when the news came from Jezreel that his father and grandfather had fallen, and his nurse, fleeing in haste, dropped him, leaving him lame in both feet. He is the son of Saul's heir, and the book will return to him.

Then the brothers act. They come to Ish-bosheth's house in the heat of the day, while he rests, and afterwards they travel through the night to Hebron, sure that David will be grateful for what they bring. David has dealt with such a messenger before, at Ziklag, and his answer to them is just as clear.

=== 5
With Abner and Ish-bosheth both dead, the northern tribes have no one left to follow. Their elders come to Hebron and acknowledge what many of them had long known: David led Israel's armies even in Saul's day, and the Lord had marked him out as shepherd of his people. They make a covenant with him and anoint him king over all Israel. He is thirty years old, and he will reign for forty years in all.

His first act as king of the whole nation is to take a city. Jerusalem, held by the Jebusites, sits on a ridge between Judah and the northern tribes, held by neither. Its defenders are so confident that they mock David: even the blind and the lame could keep him out. David takes the stronghold of Zion and makes it his capital, the City of David.

The rest of the chapter shows the kingdom taking shape. Hiram, king of the Phoenician port of Tyre, sends cedar and craftsmen to build a palace. More sons are born in Jerusalem, among them one named Solomon. And the Philistines, hearing that David has been anointed king over all Israel, march into the Valley of Rephaim, just south-west of the city, to deal with this new king.

=== 6
David now has a capital, and he wants the Lord's presence at the heart of it. The ark of the covenant, the gold-covered chest that stood in the inner sanctuary of the tabernacle, has been neglected for a generation. It was captured by the Philistines in Samuel's early days, returned, and left at the house of Abinadab in Kiriath-jearim, here called Baale of Judah, some miles west of Jerusalem, and there it has stayed, largely forgotten, through the years of Saul.

David gathers thirty thousand chosen men and sets out to bring it up to Jerusalem with music and celebration. The ark is placed on a new cart, guided by Abinadab's sons, Uzzah and Ahio. The law of Moses had laid down that the ark was to be carried on poles by the Levites, and the journey is cut short in a way that leaves David both angry and afraid.

Three months pass before he tries again. The second procession is very different, and David himself, dressed in a linen ephod, dances before the Lord with all his might. Watching from a window is Michal, Saul's daughter, and the chapter ends in a bitter exchange between husband and wife.

=== 7
The ark is in Jerusalem, resting in the tent David pitched for it. The king is settled in his cedar palace, and the writer notes that the Lord has given him rest from his enemies on every side. This is the high point of David's rise.

For the first time we meet Nathan the prophet, who will be a steady presence at David's court for the rest of his life. David shares a thought with him that seems entirely natural. It is not right that the king lives in a house of cedar while the ark of God stays in a tent. Nathan's first answer is warm approval. But that night the word of the Lord comes to him, and the message he brings back turns David's plan on its head.

The chapter turns on the word house, which can mean a building, a family or a royal dynasty. What God says to David here, and David's long prayer in response, became one of the foundations of Israel's hope, and the prophets and psalmists would return to this promise again and again.

=== 8
After the promise of chapter seven, the book gathers David's wars into a single sweeping chapter. It moves like a royal record, campaign after campaign, and twice it gives the reason in the same words: the Lord made David victorious wherever he went.

The Philistines, who have pressed on Israel since the days of Samson, are subdued at last. Moab, the homeland of David's great-grandmother Ruth, where he once sheltered his parents, is beaten and made to pay tribute. In the north David meets the Aramean kingdoms, the peoples of what is now Syria, and breaks the army of Hadadezer, king of Zobah, and the Arameans of Damascus who march to help him. Gold shields taken from Hadadezer's officers are carried back to Jerusalem, and the king of Hamath, further north still, sends his own son with gifts. In the south, Edom is filled with David's garrisons. The scattered tribes Saul struggled to defend have become the strongest power in the region.

The chapter closes with a list of David's officials. Joab commands the army, Zadok and Ahimelech serve as priests, and Benaiah son of Jehoiada commands the Cherethites and Pelethites, foreign soldiers who formed the king's personal guard. Several of these men will play large parts in the troubled years ahead.

=== 9
David's wars are behind him for the moment, and his thoughts turn to an old promise. Years earlier, when Saul was hunting him, David and Jonathan made a covenant of friendship, and David swore that he would never cut off his kindness from Jonathan's family. Now he asks whether anyone is left from the house of Saul to whom he can show that kindness.

In the ancient world a new dynasty often removed every possible rival from the old royal line, and the surviving members of Saul's family have reason to keep out of sight. A former servant of Saul's household named Ziba is found and brought before the king. He knows of one: Jonathan's son Mephibosheth, the boy who was dropped by his nurse, now grown and lame in both feet. He is living east of the Jordan at Lo-debar, in the house of a man called Machir.

Mephibosheth comes before David expecting the worst. Both he and Ziba will reappear later, at the most dangerous moment of David's reign, and what is arranged in this chapter will matter then.

=== 10
The story turns east, across the Jordan, to the kingdom of Ammon and its capital Rabbah, on the site of modern Amman. The Ammonite king Nahash has died, a king who had once shown David kindness. David sends envoys to console his son Hanun.

Hanun's advisers persuade him that the envoys are spies, and he sends them home humiliated, with half their beards shaved off and their garments cut short. In that culture it was an insult that could only mean war. The Ammonites know it, and they hire Aramean troops from the kingdoms to the north, including the forces of Hadadezer of Zobah, whom David has already defeated once.

Joab and his brother Abishai lead Israel's army into a battle on two fronts, and later David himself takes the field. The chapter matters for more than its battles. The war with Ammon is still going on when the next chapter opens, and it provides the setting for the darkest episode of David's life.

=== 11
It is spring, the season when the winter rains have ended and kings lead their armies out to war. Joab and the whole army of Israel are across the Jordan, besieging the Ammonite capital of Rabbah. But David, the narrator remarks, stays in Jerusalem.

The palace stood on high ground, and from its roof David could look down into the courtyards of the houses below. One evening he sees a woman bathing. She is Bathsheba, the daughter of Eliam and the wife of Uriah the Hittite, a foreigner serving in Israel's army and, as we learn later, one of David's own chosen warriors. Uriah is away at the siege.

The writer tells what follows with spare, unflinching restraint. One decision leads to another, each one an attempt to hide the last, and the sending of messages and messengers runs through the whole chapter. Uriah, who never says a wrong word, proves more loyal than his king. The chapter does not pass judgement until its final sentence, and then the judgement is the Lord's.

=== 12
Bathsheba has become David's wife and borne him a son, and from the outside the matter seems closed. Then the Lord sends Nathan the prophet back to the palace, the same Nathan who brought David the promise of an everlasting house.

Nathan does not begin with an accusation. He tells the king a story about a rich man, a poor man and a single ewe lamb, the kind of case a king might be asked to judge. David listens as a judge, and his verdict comes quickly and with real anger. What Nathan says next is one of the most famous confrontations in the Bible. Psalm fifty-one carries a title linking it to this moment, when Nathan came to David after he had gone in to Bathsheba.

David makes no excuses and offers no defence. Nathan tells him that the Lord has taken away his sin, yet the words he speaks over David's house will shape the rest of the book. The chapter also records a birth, a son whom David names Solomon and whom the Lord, through Nathan, calls Jedidiah, which means loved by the Lord. Meanwhile the siege of Rabbah has gone on all this time, and the chapter ends with Joab sending for the king to come and finish it.

=== 13
The war with Ammon is over, and David is back in Jerusalem with the crown of Rabbah on his head. Now the story moves inside the palace, among the king's children, and the words Nathan spoke, that the sword would never depart from David's house, begin to come true.

David's sons were born to different wives, and each mother's household was its own world. Amnon is the eldest, the son of Ahinoam, and so first in line for the throne. Absalom is the third son, whose mother Maacah was a princess, the daughter of the king of Geshur, a small Aramean kingdom north-east of the Sea of Galilee. Absalom has a full sister named Tamar. We also meet a cousin, Jonadab, the son of David's brother, described as a very shrewd man.

This is one of the hardest chapters in the Bible to read, and the narrator tells it plainly, giving Tamar her own voice. Twice in this chapter David hears what has happened in his family, and both times the listener will notice what he feels and what he fails to do. By the end, one son is dead, and another has spent three years in exile with his grandfather, the king of Geshur.

=== 14
Absalom has been in Geshur for three years, and David's grief for Amnon has eased. The king longs to see his exiled son, but he will not act. It is Joab, as so often, who sees what is happening and takes matters into his own hands.

Joab's method recalls Nathan's in chapter twelve. He sends for a wise woman from Tekoa, a village in the hills south of Bethlehem, and coaches her in a story. She is to come before the king as a grieving widow with a case for him to judge, a family dispute that is not quite what it seems. David is a shrewd listener, and before long he recognises whose words she is speaking.

The second half of the chapter brings Absalom back into the light. The narrator pauses to describe him: his flawless good looks, the heavy hair he cuts once a year and weighs, his children. He returns to Jerusalem, but on his father's terms, and for two more years he lives in the city without being allowed into the king's presence. Absalom is not a patient man, and he finds his own way to get Joab's attention.

=== 15
Absalom has been received by his father with a kiss, and he is back at the heart of the court. Now he begins, quietly and patiently, to build something of his own. He takes a chariot and horses, and fifty men to run ahead of him, the outward show of a man who expects to be king.

Then he goes to work by the road to the city gate, where people came to have their disputes heard. To every man with a grievance he says the same thing: your case is good, but the king has no one to hear you. And he takes each one by the hand and kisses him. After four years, he asks permission to go to Hebron, the city where David was first made king, and there he raises the trumpet of rebellion. With him is Ahithophel, the most respected counsellor in Israel.

What follows is one of the most vividly told days in the whole Old Testament. David leaves Jerusalem on foot, crossing the Kidron Valley and climbing the Mount of Olives, and along the way he meets a series of people, each of whom reveals something about loyalty. Psalm three bears the title, a psalm of David when he fled from his son Absalom.

=== 16
David has crossed the summit of the Mount of Olives and is heading down the road toward the Jordan valley. Behind him Absalom is entering Jerusalem. The chapter moves between the two men.

On David's road there are two more encounters. The first is with Ziba, the servant whom David placed in charge of Mephibosheth's estate, who arrives with donkeys and provisions and a report about his master. It is Ziba's word alone, and the matter will come up again. The second encounter, near the village of Bahurim, is with Shimei, a man of Saul's clan, who walks along the hillside above the road shouting curses. Abishai, Joab's brother, is ready to deal with him at once.

In Jerusalem, David's friend Hushai presents himself to Absalom as a loyal servant, playing the part David gave him. Then Absalom turns to Ahithophel for counsel, and the advice he receives fulfils, in a very public way, one of the things Nathan foretold back in chapter twelve.

=== 17
Absalom holds Jerusalem, and David is somewhere in the wilderness east of the city, near the fords of the Jordan, with a weary band of loyal followers. Everything now depends on what Absalom does next, and he has two advisers in the room.

Ahithophel speaks first. His plan is swift and ruthless. Then Absalom calls for Hushai, the man David sent back to the city for exactly this moment. The contest of counsel between them is the turning point of the rebellion, and the narrator pauses to explain what lies behind it: the Lord had determined to defeat the good advice of Ahithophel.

The rest of the chapter moves fast. A secret chain of messengers, the sons of the priests Zadok and Abiathar, must get word to David, and their errand turns into a chase. David crosses the Jordan and makes his base at Mahanaim, the same town where Ish-bosheth once reigned. Absalom appoints a new commander, his cousin Amasa, and follows with his army. And at Mahanaim, David's need is met by three men from east of the Jordan: a son of Nahash the Ammonite, old Barzillai of Gilead, and Machir of Lo-debar, the man who once sheltered Mephibosheth.

=== 18
David is at Mahanaim, east of the Jordan, and his friends from beyond the river have fed his weary followers. Absalom and his army, under Amasa, have crossed the river and are camped in Gilead. A battle is coming, and both sides know it will decide the kingdom.

David divides his troops into three companies under three trusted commanders: Joab, his brother Abishai, and Ittai the Gittite, the Philistine soldier who swore on the road out of Jerusalem to stay with David in life or death. David wants to march out with them, but his men will not allow it. So the king stands at the city gate as the army goes out, and every soldier hears the one order he gives his commanders about Absalom.

The battle is fought in the forest of Ephraim, rough wooded country in Gilead, where the terrain itself takes a heavy toll. Then the chapter narrows to a single tree, and to Joab, who has always been willing to do what David would not. Its final scenes take place back at the gate of Mahanaim, where the king sits waiting for news, and two runners are on their way to bring it.

=== 19
The rebellion is over, but there is no celebration. David has withdrawn to the room above the gate of Mahanaim, weeping for Absalom, and his soldiers creep back into the town as though they had lost the battle. It is Joab, once again, who confronts the king with a hard truth.

Then the question becomes how David is to return to Jerusalem. The northern tribes, who followed Absalom, are now arguing among themselves about bringing the king back. Judah, David's own tribe, holds back. David reaches out to the elders of Judah through the priests Zadok and Abiathar, and makes a surprising offer to Amasa, Absalom's commander.

The journey back to the Jordan is marked by a series of meetings, several of them echoing the day David fled. Shimei, who cursed him, comes to beg forgiveness. Ziba hurries down to the river with his sons and servants, and Mephibosheth comes too, unwashed and untrimmed since the day the king left, with his own account of what happened. Old Barzillai of Gilead, who fed the king at Mahanaim, comes to see him across the river. By the end of the chapter, the old rivalry between Judah and the rest of Israel has flared up again, and the words are growing hot.

=== 20
The quarrel at the Jordan between the men of Judah and the men of Israel has been left unresolved, and someone is ready to exploit it. Sheba son of Bichri, a Benjamite from Saul's tribe, blows the ram's horn and raises a cry that rejects David's rule altogether. The words of that cry will be heard again, almost unchanged, when the kingdom finally splits in the days of David's grandson.

David is back in his palace in Jerusalem, and he deals first with the ten concubines Absalom took. Then he turns to Sheba. He has promised command of the army to Amasa, and he gives him three days to summon the men of Judah. Amasa is late, and David sends Abishai after Sheba instead, with Joab's men and the royal guard. Joab, demoted but not idle, goes with them, and when Amasa catches up with them at Gibeon, Joab settles the question of command in his own way.

The pursuit runs to the far north of the land, to the town of Abel-beth-maacah, near the sources of the Jordan. There the chapter's last voice is a wise woman on the city wall, speaking for her town. It closes with a list of David's officials, and Joab's name stands at the top once more.

=== 21
The main story of David's reign has reached its end, and the last four chapters of the book step aside from it, gathering stories, songs and the roll of David's warriors from across his reign.

It begins with a famine that lasts three years. When David seeks the Lord, the answer points back to Saul. The Gibeonites were a remnant of the Amorites who, in Joshua's day, tricked Israel into swearing a treaty that protected them. Saul, the text now reveals, tried to wipe them out, breaking that oath. Nothing of this was told in First Samuel. David asks the Gibeonites what will make amends, and their answer falls on Saul's descendants. David spares Mephibosheth, because of his oath to Jonathan. Rizpah, Saul's concubine, whose name came up long ago in the quarrel between Abner and Ish-bosheth, keeps a long and lonely vigil, and her devotion moves David to give Saul and Jonathan an honourable burial at last.

The chapter ends with brief accounts of four Philistine giants of Gath, all killed by David's men. In one of these battles David himself nearly dies, and his men resolve that he must never again go out with them to war.

=== 22
At the centre of the book's closing chapters stands a song. The heading places it on the day the Lord delivered David from all his enemies and from the hand of Saul, so it looks back over the whole of his life rather than to the troubled years just described. Almost the same poem appears in the book of Psalms as Psalm eighteen, and its presence in both places shows how highly it was treasured.

It is a long hymn of thanksgiving from a warrior king. It begins with a cluster of images for God drawn from a life spent in the wild hill country of Judah: rock, fortress, refuge, shield, stronghold. Then it remembers a time when death seemed to close in like flood waters, and describes God coming to the rescue in the language of storm and earthquake, thunder, lightning and dark cloud, as he once came down on Mount Sinai.

The second half turns to battle and victory, to God training the king's hands for war and setting him at the head of nations. It ends with the Lord's loving devotion to his anointed, to David and his descendants forever, a closing echo of the promise Nathan brought in chapter seven.

=== 23
After the long song of chapter twenty-two come David's last words, a much shorter poem. It is called an oracle, the word used for a prophet's message, and David speaks of the Spirit of the Lord speaking through him. Its subject is the just ruler, who is like sunrise on a cloudless morning, and the everlasting covenant God has made with David's house.

The rest of the chapter is a roll of honour: David's mighty men, the warriors who stood with him from his outlaw days onwards. First come the Three, the most renowned of all, with brief accounts of their exploits. One story takes us to the cave of Adullam, at a time when a Philistine garrison held David's home town of Bethlehem. Then come Abishai and Benaiah, each with his own feats, and after them the wider company known as the Thirty.

Several names on this list will be familiar. Joab's brother Asahel, who fell in chapter two, is here. So is an Eliam son of Ahithophel, the counsellor who joined Absalom; Bathsheba's father, too, was called Eliam. And the last name on the list is Uriah the Hittite.

=== 24
The book's final chapter returns to the theme of the opening story in chapter twenty-one: the Lord's anger against Israel, and a disaster that David must answer for. Again we are not told exactly when it happened. The chapter opens by saying that the Lord incited David to count the people. When the book of Chronicles tells the same story, it says that Satan incited him. Neither account explains exactly why the census was wrong, but even Joab, no stranger to hard choices, protests against it.

The census takes almost ten months, and it traces the borders of David's kingdom, from Dan in the far north to Beersheba in the south. When it is done, David's conscience strikes him. The prophet Gad, who has been with David since his fugitive days, brings him a hard choice between three judgements.

The chapter, and the book, end on a hill just north of the City of David, at the threshing floor of a Jebusite named Araunah. Chronicles later identifies this as the place where Solomon built the temple, so Second Samuel closes on the ground where the next great building of Israel's story will rise.
