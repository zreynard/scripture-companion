=== intro
Genesis closed with a coffin in Egypt. Joseph had died there, an honoured man in a foreign land, and before he died he made his brothers swear that when God came to rescue them they would carry his bones home to Canaan. Exodus opens in the same place, the green delta of the Nile, but a great deal of time has passed. The family of seventy has become a people, and the welcome they once enjoyed has turned to fear. A new king who does not know Joseph sees only a dangerous foreign population, and he sets them to forced labour, making bricks and building cities for Pharaoh.

The name Exodus comes from the Greek for a way out, or a departure, and the book tells how a nation of slaves was brought out of Egypt and made the people of God. It is the story Israel would tell more than any other. Every later generation looked back to it, in their songs, their festivals and their prayers, as the moment God showed who he was. Ancient tradition, Jewish and Christian, links the book with Moses, and much of it is presented as his words or the words given to him, though like the other books of the Torah it does not name its writer.

The central human figure is Moses himself. He is born under a death sentence, rescued from the Nile, raised in Pharaoh's household, and then driven into exile in the wilderness of Midian, where he spends long years as a shepherd. He is eighty when he stands before Pharaoh. Beside him are his elder brother Aaron, who speaks on his behalf and later becomes Israel's first high priest, and his sister Miriam. Opposite him stands Pharaoh, king of the most powerful nation of the age, who is never named. The book is not interested in his name. It is interested in the contest between the one who claims to own Israel and the Lord who says, let my people go.

When this happened is debated. Exodus says Israel lived in Egypt for four hundred and thirty years. Some scholars place the departure in the fifteenth century before Christ, others in the thirteenth, during the reign of the great builder kings of Egypt's New Kingdom, and the evidence can be read either way. The route out of Egypt and the exact location of Mount Sinai are also uncertain; several mountains in the Sinai peninsula and beyond have been proposed, and the book's own place names are hard to map with confidence.

The story moves in three great stages. The first is in Egypt. God calls Moses from a burning bush at Horeb, the mountain of God, and reveals his personal name, the Lord. Moses returns to Egypt, and through a series of ten plagues the Lord breaks Pharaoh's grip, until on the night of the Passover the firstborn of Egypt die and Israel goes free. Pharaoh's army pursues them to the sea, and the waters that open for Israel close over the chariots of Egypt.

The second stage is the journey through the wilderness to Sinai. Freedom brings hunger and thirst, and the people complain bitterly, but God provides water from the rock and bread from heaven, which they call manna. They fight their first battle, against the Amalekites, and Moses' father-in-law Jethro brings practical advice for governing a nation.

The third and longest stage takes place at the foot of Sinai. There, amid thunder and fire, God makes a covenant with Israel. He speaks the Ten Commandments and gives laws for their life together, and the people solemnly agree to keep them. Then God gives Moses detailed plans for a tabernacle, a portable tent where the Lord himself will live among his people. Before it can be built, the covenant is almost destroyed. While Moses is on the mountain, the people make a golden calf and worship it, and only Moses' pleading and God's mercy carry them through. The book ends with the tabernacle finished and the glory of the Lord filling it.

Many listeners find the long sections of law and building instructions hard going after the drama of the early chapters. They belong to the story. Exodus is about a God who rescues his people from slavery so that they can serve him, and who wants to dwell in their midst. The laws show what kind of people they are to be; the tabernacle shows how a holy God can live among them. The book moves from Pharaoh's building projects, where Israel laboured as slaves, to God's dwelling place, which they build as free people.

Exodus also looks back and forward. It picks up the promises made to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, and it explains the Passover, the covenant and the priesthood that shape the rest of the Old Testament. The prophets later spoke of a new exodus, and the writers of the New Testament described the death of Jesus at Passover in its language. The book of Leviticus follows directly, at the same mountain, as God speaks from the newly completed tent.

=== 1
Exodus begins by looking back. It names the sons of Jacob who came down to Egypt, the same seventy people listed near the end of Genesis, and then, in a single sentence, it lets a whole generation pass away. Joseph is gone, his brothers are gone, and their descendants have multiplied until the land is filled with them. The language deliberately recalls God's first blessing on humanity: they are fruitful and they increase.

Then a new king arises in Egypt who does not know Joseph. The years when an Israelite governed Egypt and Pharaoh welcomed his family are forgotten. To this king the Israelites are a threat, a large foreign community on his eastern border who might side with an invader. His solution is forced labour. The Israelites are made to build the store cities of Pithom and Rameses.

When oppression fails to reduce their numbers, Pharaoh turns to something worse. The chapter introduces two Hebrew midwives, Shiphrah and Puah, whose names are remembered while Pharaoh's is not, and it ends with a command to the whole of Egypt that every newborn Hebrew boy be thrown into the Nile.

=== 2
Pharaoh has ordered that every Hebrew baby boy be drowned in the Nile. Into that world a child is born to a couple from the tribe of Levi. His parents are not named here; they will be later. His mother hides him for three months, and when she can hide him no longer she makes a basket of papyrus, seals it with tar and pitch, and sets it among the reeds at the river's edge, while his older sister watches from a distance.

The river that was meant to be his grave becomes the place of his rescue, and the one who finds him is Pharaoh's own daughter. She gives him his name, Moses.

The chapter then moves quickly through the years. Moses grows up in the royal household, but he knows who his own people are, and one day his anger at their suffering leads him to kill an Egyptian. He flees east to the land of Midian, in the desert beyond Egypt, and finds a home with a Midianite priest called Reuel. Moses marries his daughter Zipporah and settles into life as a shepherd. Meanwhile, in Egypt, the king dies, the Israelites cry out under their slavery, and God hears them and remembers his covenant with Abraham, Isaac and Jacob.

=== 3
Many years have passed. Moses is now a shepherd in the wilderness, caring for the flocks of his father-in-law, who is called here by another name, Jethro, the priest of Midian. One day he leads the flock to the far side of the wilderness and comes to Horeb, called the mountain of God, the mountain later chapters call Sinai.

There Moses sees a bush on fire that does not burn up. When he turns aside to look, God speaks to him from the flames, calls him by name, and tells him to take off his sandals, because he is standing on holy ground. This is one of the great encounters of the Old Testament. The God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob has seen the misery of his people and heard their cry, and he has come down to rescue them.

The conversation that follows is honest and very human. God's plan has Moses at its centre, and Moses does not want the part. His first question is who he is to go to Pharaoh. His second is the name of the God who sends him, and the answer, I am who I am, has been pondered ever since. It is linked to the personal name of God, printed in many English Bibles as the Lord in capital letters.

=== 4
The conversation at the burning bush continues, and Moses is still full of objections. What if the Israelites do not believe him? God gives him signs to perform, beginning with the shepherd's staff in his hand. Then Moses protests that he has never been a good speaker, and finally he simply asks God to send someone else. God's patience gives way to anger, but he provides a helper: Moses' brother Aaron, who speaks well.

Moses returns to Jethro and sets out for Egypt with Zipporah and his sons, carrying what is now called the staff of God. On the way comes one of the strangest episodes in the Bible. The Lord meets Moses at a lodging place and is about to kill him, and Zipporah saves him by circumcising their son. The few lines are difficult, and interpreters have long discussed them, but they turn on the sign of the covenant made with Abraham, which Moses has neglected in his own family.

The chapter ends with a reunion at the mountain of God, as Aaron comes out to meet his brother, and then with the elders of Israel in Egypt, who hear the message, see the signs and bow down in worship.

=== 5
The elders of Israel have believed, and now Moses and Aaron go to the palace. Their request is carefully limited: let the people go three days into the wilderness to hold a feast to the Lord. Pharaoh's answer sets the tone for everything that follows. Who is the Lord, that I should obey him? I do not know the Lord.

In the world of ancient Egypt, Pharaoh was regarded as a divine king, the son of the gods, and he has no reason to listen to the God of a nation of slaves. He sees the request as a pretext for laziness, and he responds by making the work harder. Until now the brick makers have been given straw, which was mixed with the clay to strengthen the bricks. From now on they must gather their own straw and still meet the same quota.

The rest of the chapter traces the effects down the chain of command, from Pharaoh to the Egyptian taskmasters, to the Israelite foremen who are beaten when the quota fails, and finally to Moses and Aaron, who are blamed by their own people. The first attempt at deliverance has only made things worse, and Moses turns to the Lord with an anguished question: why did you ever send me?

=== 6
Moses has brought his complaint to God. His first visit to Pharaoh has made the slavery heavier, and the Israelite foremen blame him for it. God's answer begins with the words, now you will see what I will do to Pharaoh.

God repeats and deepens the promises given at the burning bush. He appeared to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob as God Almighty, but now he will make himself known by his name, the Lord, in a way they never saw. He will bring Israel out, redeem them with an outstretched arm, take them as his own people, and bring them into the land he swore to give their fathers.

But the Israelites are too crushed by their slavery to listen. When God sends Moses back to Pharaoh, Moses objects again that he is a poor speaker. At this point the story pauses for a family record. It begins with the tribes of Reuben and Simeon, then slows down for Levi, because this is the line of Moses and Aaron. It names their parents, Amram and Jochebed, and Aaron's wife and sons, including Nadab, Abihu, Eleazar and Ithamar, and his grandson Phinehas, all of whom will matter later. Then the narrative returns to the moment where it left off.

=== 7
The family record is complete, and the story resumes with God's answer to Moses' fear that Pharaoh will never listen to a poor speaker. God will make Moses like God to Pharaoh, with Aaron as his prophet, the one who speaks his words. The chapter pauses to record their ages. Moses is eighty and Aaron eighty-three when they stand before the king.

God also tells Moses what to expect. He will harden Pharaoh's heart, and Pharaoh will not listen, so that God can multiply his signs and the Egyptians will know that he is the Lord. The hardening of Pharaoh's heart runs through these chapters. Sometimes Pharaoh hardens his own heart, sometimes his heart is simply said to be hard, and sometimes the Lord hardens it, and the narrative holds these together without explaining how.

The contest begins with a sign. Aaron's staff becomes a serpent, and Pharaoh's magicians match it, though Aaron's staff swallows theirs. Then comes the first of the plagues. Early in the morning Moses meets Pharaoh at the edge of the Nile, the river that gave Egypt its life and was honoured in its religion, and the water is struck.

=== 8
The Nile has run red with blood for seven days. Now the plagues come one after another, and a pattern begins to form. Moses delivers the Lord's demand, let my people go, so that they may worship me. Pharaoh refuses, the plague strikes, and Pharaoh's resistance bends a little and then hardens again.

This chapter holds three plagues. First come frogs, swarming out of the rivers into the palace, the bedrooms and the ovens. The magicians can produce frogs too, but they cannot take them away, and for the first time Pharaoh asks Moses to pray for him and promises to let the people go. Next comes a plague of gnats, which arrives without warning, and here the magicians admit defeat. They tell Pharaoh that this is the finger of God.

The third plague in the chapter, swarms of flies, brings something new. The land of Goshen, where the Israelites live, is spared, and from now on the line between Egypt and Israel is drawn more clearly. Pharaoh begins to bargain. The Israelites may sacrifice, he says, but here in Egypt, and then perhaps in the wilderness, but not too far away.

=== 9
Pharaoh has gone back on his word again after the plague of flies. The pressure on Egypt now grows heavier, and the plagues begin to strike at the country's wealth and its people's bodies.

First a disease falls on Egypt's livestock in the fields, the horses, donkeys, camels, cattle and sheep, while the animals of Israel are untouched. Pharaoh sends officials to check, and finds it is so, yet he still refuses. Then Moses throws soot from a furnace into the air, and painful boils break out on people and animals alike. The magicians, who once copied Moses' signs, cannot even stand before him.

The seventh plague is introduced with a longer speech, in which the Lord tells Pharaoh that he could have wiped Egypt out already, but has raised him up to show his power and make his name known in all the earth. A hailstorm is coming, the worst Egypt has ever seen. For the first time a warning is given in time for the Egyptians to shelter, and some of Pharaoh's own officials take it seriously. The storm that follows, with thunder and lightning, wrecks the crops of the field, and Pharaoh at last confesses that he has sinned. Moses is not deceived.

=== 10
The hail has flattened Egypt's barley and flax, and Pharaoh has broken his promise again. The Lord sends Moses back, telling him that these signs are being done so that Israel will tell their children and grandchildren how the Lord dealt with Egypt.

This time the warning is of locusts, which will eat whatever the hail has left. Something new happens before the plague even arrives: Pharaoh's officials beg him to give in. Do you not yet realise, they ask, that Egypt lies in ruins? Pharaoh calls Moses back and tries to negotiate. Who exactly will be going? When Moses answers that everyone will go, young and old, with their flocks and herds, Pharaoh offers to let only the men go, and drives Moses and Aaron out.

The locusts come on an east wind and cover the land until it is black. Then comes the ninth plague, a darkness so thick it can be felt, lasting three days in a land where the sun was worshipped among the greatest of the gods, while the Israelites have light where they live. Pharaoh makes one final offer, which Moses refuses, and the chapter ends with a threat of death and Moses' grim reply that Pharaoh will never see his face again.

=== 11
Pharaoh has just told Moses never to come into his presence again. Before Moses leaves, though, he has one more message to deliver, and this short chapter records it.

It begins with what the Lord had already told Moses privately. One more plague is coming, and after it Pharaoh will not merely let Israel go; he will drive them out. The Israelites are to ask their Egyptian neighbours for silver and gold, and the Egyptians, the narrator notes, now regard the people favourably and hold Moses himself in high honour.

Then Moses speaks his final warning to Pharaoh. About midnight the Lord will go through Egypt, and every firstborn son will die, from the heir on Pharaoh's throne to the son of the slave girl at the hand mill, along with the firstborn of the livestock. Israel will be untouched. The chapter's last lines look back over the whole contest so far, and Moses leaves the palace, as the text says, hot with anger.

=== 12
The final plague has been announced, and the Israelites are about to leave Egypt. Before the story tells what happens at midnight, it stops to give instructions, because this night is to be remembered for ever.

God tells Moses and Aaron that this month will be the first month of Israel's year. Their calendar will begin with their liberation. Each household is to choose a lamb, keep it for four days, and slaughter it at twilight, painting its blood on the doorframe of the house. They are to eat it roasted, with unleavened bread and bitter herbs, dressed for travel, with sandals on and staff in hand. This is the Passover, named because the Lord will pass over the houses marked with blood. With it comes the seven-day Feast of Unleavened Bread, which will recall a people leaving in such haste that their bread had no time to rise.

Then the night comes. The chapter tells of a cry across the whole land of Egypt, of Pharaoh summoning Moses and Aaron in the dark, and of the Israelites setting out from Rameses. They are a vast company, with many others who are not Israelites going with them. The narrator marks the moment: four hundred and thirty years to the very day since Israel came to live in Egypt.

=== 13
The Israelites are out of Egypt, camped at Succoth after their first day's march from Rameses. Before the story follows them further, it records more instructions that grow out of the night of the Passover.

Because the Lord struck down the firstborn of Egypt and spared those of Israel, every firstborn male in Israel now belongs to him. Firstborn animals are to be offered, and firstborn sons are to be redeemed, bought back. The Feast of Unleavened Bread is to be kept every year in the month of Abib, in the spring, when they are settled in the land. In both cases, when the children of later generations ask what this means, they are to be told what the Lord did with a mighty hand.

Then the journey begins. The quickest road to Canaan ran along the Mediterranean coast, through the land of the Philistines, but God does not want his people facing war so soon. Instead he leads them south and east toward the wilderness and the Red Sea. Moses carries with him the bones of Joseph, keeping the oath made at the end of Genesis. And from now on, the Lord goes before them in a pillar of cloud by day and a pillar of fire by night.

=== 14
The Israelites have reached the edge of the wilderness, guided by the pillar of cloud and fire. Now God gives a strange order. They are to turn back and camp by the sea, at a place called Pi-hahiroth, between Migdol and the sea. These places can no longer be located with any certainty, and nor can the exact body of water. The Hebrew name, traditionally the Red Sea, can also be translated the Sea of Reeds.

The purpose of the order is to draw Pharaoh out. He will think the Israelites are lost and trapped by the wilderness. And in Egypt the loss of a whole workforce has begun to sink in. Pharaoh and his officials ask themselves what they have done, and the king gathers his chariots, the most advanced military force of the age, and goes in pursuit.

When the Israelites see the Egyptian army coming, they are terrified, and they turn on Moses with bitter sarcasm: were there no graves in Egypt? With the sea in front of them and the chariots behind, Moses tells them to stand firm and watch. The Lord will fight for you. What happens during the night that follows became the defining act of rescue in Israel's memory.

=== 15
The Israelites stand on the far shore, and the army of Egypt lies dead behind them. Their first response is a song. It is one of the oldest and most celebrated poems in the Bible, and the first great hymn of praise in Scripture. Moses and the people sing of the horse and rider thrown into the sea, of the Lord as a warrior, of waters piled up like a wall at the breath of his nostrils.

As the song goes on it looks ahead. The nations of Philistia, Edom, Moab and Canaan will hear and tremble, and the Lord will bring his people in and plant them on the mountain of his inheritance, the place he has chosen to dwell. Then Miriam, Aaron's sister, called here a prophetess, leads the women with tambourines and dancing, singing back the opening words.

The rest of the chapter starts the long journey through the wilderness, and the mood changes quickly. Three days into the desert of Shur, in the dry country east of Egypt, they find no water, and when they do find some, at a place called Marah, it is bitter. The first complaint is heard, the Lord provides, and then the people reach the welcome shade of Elim, with its twelve springs and seventy palm trees.

=== 16
The Israelites have left the oasis of Elim and come into the Desert of Sin, between Elim and Mount Sinai. It is the middle of the second month since they left Egypt, and the food they carried out is running low. The whole community grumbles against Moses and Aaron. In their memory Egypt has become a place of pots full of meat and bread to eat their fill.

God's answer is to provide. Quail cover the camp in the evening, and in the morning, when the dew lifts, the ground is covered with fine white flakes. The Israelites ask one another, what is it? In Hebrew that question sounds like the name they give it: manna. It will feed them through the whole of their time in the wilderness.

The manna comes with instructions, and they test the people. Each family is to gather only what it needs for the day; anything kept overnight spoils. On the sixth day they are to gather a double portion, because on the seventh no manna will fall. This is the first time Israel is told to keep the Sabbath as a day of rest, and not everyone listens. Before the chapter is over, a jar of manna is set aside to be kept for future generations.

=== 17
The Israelites move on from the Desert of Sin and camp at Rephidim, somewhere on the way to Sinai. Once again there is no water, and this time the complaints turn dangerous. The people quarrel with Moses, and he tells the Lord they are almost ready to stone him.

God sends Moses ahead with some of the elders to the rock at Horeb, carrying the same staff that struck the Nile. When Moses strikes the rock, water comes out. He names the place Massah and Meribah, meaning testing and quarrelling, because the people asked, is the Lord among us or not? Israel's later psalms, and the New Testament after them, would recall those names as a warning.

Next, Israel fights its first battle. The Amalekites, a desert people descended from Esau, attack the Israelites at Rephidim. Here we meet Joshua for the first time, chosen by Moses to lead the fighting men. Moses climbs a hill with the staff of God, together with Aaron and a man named Hur, and the outcome of the battle below follows the raising and lowering of Moses' hands. The conflict with Amalek will run on for generations.

=== 18
The story now turns to a visit at the mountain of God, where Moses first saw the burning bush and where the Israelites are camped. Word has reached Midian of everything that has happened, and Jethro, Moses' father-in-law, comes out to meet him. With him are Moses' wife Zipporah and their two sons, Gershom and Eliezer. We learn here that Moses had sent them back to Jethro at some point during the struggle with Egypt.

It is a warm meeting. Moses bows to his father-in-law, kisses him, and tells him all that the Lord has done for Israel. Jethro, a priest of Midian and not an Israelite, responds with praise and a confession: now I know that the Lord is greater than all other gods. He offers sacrifices, and Aaron and the elders of Israel share a meal with him in the presence of God.

The next day Jethro watches Moses at work, sitting from morning until evening while the people bring him their disputes, and he sees at once that it cannot go on. His advice is practical and wise, and Moses takes it. The chapter shows a nation beginning to be organised, just before it receives the law that will govern it.

=== 19
Three months after leaving Egypt, the Israelites arrive in the Wilderness of Sinai and camp in front of the mountain. They will be here for almost a year. Everything from this chapter to the end of Exodus, all of Leviticus and the first part of Numbers takes place in this one location.

God had promised Moses at the burning bush that the people would worship him on this mountain, and now it has happened. Moses climbs up to meet God, and comes down with a message that sums up what God intends. You have seen what I did to Egypt, and how I carried you on eagles' wings and brought you to myself. If you obey me and keep my covenant, you will be my treasured possession, a kingdom of priests and a holy nation. The people answer together, we will do everything the Lord has said.

The rest of the chapter is preparation for the moment when God himself will come down on the mountain. The people must wash their clothes and consecrate themselves for three days, and a boundary is set around the base that no one may cross. On the morning of the third day the mountain is wrapped in smoke and fire, thunder and lightning break out, the ground shakes, and the sound of a ram's horn grows louder and louder.

=== 20
The mountain is covered in smoke and fire, and the people stand trembling at its foot. Now God speaks to them directly. These are the words known as the Ten Commandments, though Exodus here simply calls them all these words.

They begin not with a command but with a reminder of who is speaking: I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery. Everything that follows rests on that rescue. The first commands concern Israel's relationship with God: no other gods, no idols, no misuse of his name, and the keeping of the Sabbath. The rest concern life among their neighbours: honour for parents, and protection for life, marriage, property, truth, and even the desires of the heart.

When the voice falls silent, the people are terrified. They beg Moses to speak to them himself, for fear that if God speaks again they will die. So they stand at a distance while Moses approaches the thick darkness where God is. Then come the first of the laws given to Moses there, about how Israel is to worship: with no images of gold or silver, and on a simple altar of earth or uncut stone.

=== 21
Moses has gone into the thick darkness on the mountain, and God continues to speak to him. What follows, running through to the end of chapter twenty-three, is a collection of laws that later in the book is called the Book of the Covenant. If the Ten Commandments are the foundation, these are the case laws that apply them to the ordinary life of an ancient farming community.

The laws open, perhaps surprisingly, with the treatment of servants, and with limits on how long a Hebrew may be held in service. For a people just freed from slavery, that is where justice begins. Then come laws dealing with violence: murder and manslaughter, with places of refuge for those who kill by accident, injuries in a fight, and the principle of an eye for an eye, which in its setting set a limit on revenge and required that a penalty fit the harm done.

The chapter closes with the problems of a world of livestock and open land: an ox that gores, a pit left uncovered. Similar collections of law survive from elsewhere in the ancient Near East, the code of the Babylonian king Hammurabi being the best known, and Israel's laws share their form while differing at many points, especially in the value they set on human life.

=== 22
The laws given to Moses on the mountain continue. This chapter begins with property, and with the principle of restitution. A thief must repay what he has taken, often several times over. There are rulings about a burglar caught at night, livestock that strays into a neighbour's field, a fire that spreads, and goods or animals left in someone else's care and then lost.

Then the subjects change more quickly, and the tone shifts. The laws move from compensation to holiness, and from the courtroom to the heart of God. Sorcery, bestiality and sacrifice to other gods are forbidden. Then come some of the most striking words in the collection. Israel must not oppress the foreigner, because they themselves were foreigners in Egypt. They must not mistreat the widow or the orphan, because God hears their cry. They must not charge interest to the poor, and a cloak taken as security must be returned by sunset, because it may be the only covering a poor man has at night.

Last come the offerings that belong to God, including the firstborn, and with a reminder of the reason behind all of it: you are to be my holy people.

=== 23
This is the last part of the Book of the Covenant. It begins with justice in the courts: no false reports, no siding with the crowd, no bribes, and fairness for rich and poor alike. Even an enemy's stray animal must be returned. Once again comes the reminder that Israel knows what it is to be a foreigner.

The laws then turn to the rhythms of time. Every seventh year the fields are to rest, so the poor and the wild animals can eat what grows. Every seventh day people and animals rest. Three times a year the men of Israel are to come before the Lord for the great festivals: Unleavened Bread in the spring, the Feast of Harvest when the first grain is gathered, and the Feast of Ingathering at the end of the agricultural year. The second is also called the Feast of Weeks, later known as Pentecost, and the third the Feast of Tabernacles.

Finally the Lord looks ahead to Canaan. God promises to send his angel before Israel to guard them and bring them into the land, and he will drive out its inhabitants little by little. Israel must make no covenant with those nations or their gods, because their gods would become a snare.

=== 24
The Book of the Covenant is finished, and now the covenant is formally sealed. Moses comes down from the mountain and tells the people all the words and laws of the Lord, and they answer with one voice that they will do it all.

The ceremony that follows is one of the most important in the Old Testament. Moses writes down God's words, builds an altar at the foot of the mountain and sets up twelve pillars, one for each tribe. Young men offer sacrifices. Moses reads the Book of the Covenant aloud, the people promise again to obey, and he splashes blood on the altar and on the people, calling it the blood of the covenant. Jesus would use the same phrase centuries later, at the Last Supper.

Then Moses climbs the mountain with Aaron, Aaron's sons Nadab and Abihu, and seventy elders of Israel. There they see the God of Israel, with something like a pavement of sapphire under his feet, and they eat and drink in his presence. Finally God calls Moses higher still, to receive tablets of stone. Taking Joshua as his attendant, he leaves Aaron and Hur in charge below and disappears into the cloud. He will be gone for forty days and forty nights.

=== 25
Moses is on the summit of Sinai, inside the cloud, where he will stay for forty days. The covenant has been sealed, and now God tells him what comes next. The next seven chapters are God's instructions for a sanctuary, and the reason is given at the start: they are to make a sanctuary for me, so that I may dwell among them.

The materials are to come as a free offering from anyone whose heart moves them: gold, silver and bronze, fine yarns dyed blue, purple and scarlet, linen, goat hair, skins, acacia wood, oil, spices and precious stones. Many of these riches were carried out of Egypt. Everything is to be made according to the pattern God shows Moses on the mountain.

The instructions begin at the centre and work outward. First comes the ark, a chest of acacia wood covered in gold, which will hold the tablets of the covenant. Its lid, called the mercy seat, has two golden cherubim facing one another with outstretched wings, and God says he will meet Moses there. Then come the golden table for the Bread of the Presence and the lampstand, hammered from a single piece of gold, with seven lamps on branches shaped like an almond tree in blossom.

=== 26
The instructions for the sanctuary continue. Having described the ark, the table and the lampstand, God now describes the structure that will house them: the tabernacle itself, a tent designed to be taken down, carried and set up again wherever Israel travels.

Its innermost layer is a set of ten linen curtains in blue, purple and scarlet, with cherubim woven into them. Over these go curtains of goat hair, then coverings of ram skins dyed red and of fine leather, to keep out the weather. The tent stands on a framework of upright frames made of acacia wood and overlaid with gold, set in silver bases and held together with crossbars.

The most important detail comes near the end. Inside the tent a veil, embroidered with cherubim, divides the space into two rooms. Behind it, in the Most Holy Place, stands the ark with its mercy seat. In front of it, in the Holy Place, are the table and the lampstand. A further curtain closes the entrance. Many cubits of measurement are given; a cubit was about the length of a forearm, roughly half a metre.

=== 27
The tent and its furniture have been described. Now the instructions move outside, into the open air.

In front of the tent is to stand the great altar for burnt offerings, a hollow box of acacia wood covered in bronze, with a horn at each corner. It is about two and a half metres square, and every sacrifice in Israel's worship will be offered here. Around both the altar and the tent there is to be a courtyard, enclosed by linen curtains hung from posts, with an embroidered gate on the east side, toward the sunrise. Inside the tent, gold is the main metal, while out here in the courtyard bronze is used, and the materials grow plainer as one moves away from the presence of God.

The chapter ends with a brief command that leads into the next subject. The Israelites are to bring pure olive oil to keep the lamps burning, and Aaron and his sons are to tend them from evening until morning before the Lord. For the first time in these instructions the priests are mentioned, and they will be the subject of the next two chapters.

=== 28
God has described the tabernacle, its furniture and its courtyard. Now he turns to the people who will serve there. Aaron and his four sons, Nadab, Abihu, Eleazar and Ithamar, are to be set apart as priests, and this chapter describes their clothing, made by skilled craftsmen, for glory and for beauty.

Aaron, as the high priest, is to wear a set of garments unlike anyone else's. Over a blue robe goes the ephod, a richly woven garment rather like an apron or vest, with two onyx stones on its shoulders, each engraved with the names of six of the tribes of Israel. On his chest he wears the breastpiece of judgment, set with twelve precious stones, each engraved with the name of a tribe. Inside it are the Urim and Thummim, objects used to seek God's will, whose exact form is not known. So whenever Aaron enters the sanctuary he carries the names of Israel on his shoulders and over his heart.

Golden bells hang from the hem of his robe. On his turban he wears a gold plate engraved with the words: holy to the Lord. Aaron's sons are given simpler garments of their own.

=== 29
The priestly garments have been described. Now God gives instructions for the ceremony that will make Aaron and his sons priests. It is to last seven days, and it will be carried out, almost exactly as described here, in Leviticus chapter eight.

The ordination begins with washing at the entrance to the tent. Aaron is dressed in his garments and anointed with oil poured on his head, and his sons are dressed too. Then three animals are sacrificed. A bull is offered as a sin offering, to deal with the priests' own sin; one ram is burned whole as a burnt offering; and the second ram, called the ram of ordination, provides blood that is put on the right ear, the right thumb and the big toe of the right foot of each new priest. Parts of this ram, with bread, are placed in the priests' hands and then burned, and they eat the rest at the tent's entrance.

The chapter then describes the daily offerings to be made on the altar, a lamb each morning and another at twilight, for all generations. And it ends with God's purpose for the whole tabernacle. There I will meet with Israel. I will dwell among them and be their God, the God who brought them out of Egypt so that he might dwell among them.

=== 30
The great instructions for the tabernacle are nearly complete, and this chapter adds a series of final items. The first is a small golden altar for incense, which is to stand in the Holy Place, just in front of the veil that hides the ark. Aaron is to burn fragrant incense on it every morning and every evening, so that its smoke rises continually before the Lord.

Then comes a ruling for whenever a census is taken. Every man counted must pay half a shekel of silver, rich and poor alike, as a ransom for his life, and the money is to be used for the service of the tent. A bronze basin is to stand between the tent and the altar, so that the priests can wash their hands and feet before they serve.

The chapter closes with recipes for two sacred blends. One is the anointing oil, made with myrrh, cinnamon, fragrant cane, cassia and olive oil, to be used only for anointing the tabernacle, its furnishings and the priests. The other is the incense, made of spices and frankincense. Neither may be copied for ordinary use. They are holy.

=== 31
Forty days of instructions on the mountain are drawing to a close. God has described what is to be made; now he names who will make it. Bezalel, from the tribe of Judah, the grandson of Hur, is said to be filled with the Spirit of God, and here that gift brings skill in every kind of craft: metalwork, gem cutting, woodwork and design. Oholiab, from the tribe of Dan, is appointed to help him, along with other skilled craftsmen.

Then God gives Moses a final word about the Sabbath. Israel must keep it as a sign between God and his people for ever, and the penalty for breaking it is severe. Even the building of God's dwelling must stop on the seventh day.

Finally, when God has finished speaking with Moses on Mount Sinai, he gives him the two tablets of the covenant, tablets of stone written by the finger of God. Moses is about to carry them down the mountain. But while he has been receiving them, something has been happening in the camp below.

=== 32
While Moses has been on the mountain receiving the plans for God's dwelling, the people in the camp below have grown restless. Weeks have gone by with no sign of him. They gather around Aaron, whom Moses left in charge, and demand gods to go before them. As for this Moses who brought us out of Egypt, they say, we do not know what has happened to him.

Aaron gives way. He collects their gold earrings and makes a calf, and the people declare that these are the gods who brought them out of Egypt. Aaron builds an altar and proclaims a feast to the Lord, and the next day becomes a riot of eating, drinking and revelry. The bull or calf was a common religious image in the ancient world, a symbol of strength and fertility. Whatever Aaron intended, the people have broken the commandments they heard spoken from the mountain only weeks before.

On the summit, God tells Moses what has happened, and says he will destroy the people and make a great nation from Moses instead. What follows is one of the great intercessions of the Bible, as Moses pleads for Israel. Then he goes down the mountain, carrying the tablets, and comes within sight of the camp.

=== 33
The calf has been destroyed and the guilty punished, but the covenant is badly damaged. God tells Moses to lead the people on to the promised land, and he will send an angel ahead of them to drive out its inhabitants. But he himself will not go with them. They are a stiff-necked people, and if he went among them he might destroy them on the way. When the people hear this, they mourn and take off their jewellery.

The chapter then describes something Moses used to do. He would pitch a tent outside the camp, called the Tent of Meeting. This is not yet the tabernacle, which has still to be built, but a simpler tent where the pillar of cloud would come down, and where the Lord would speak with Moses face to face, as a man speaks with his friend. Joshua, Moses' young assistant, would stay there.

What follows is among the most intimate conversations in the Old Testament. Moses refuses to go on without the presence of God. If your presence does not go with us, he says, do not send us up from here. And then he makes a bolder request still: please show me your glory.

=== 34
God has agreed to go with his people, and he has promised Moses a glimpse of his glory. Now Moses is told to cut two new stone tablets, like the ones he broke, and to climb Sinai again alone at first light.

What happens on the mountain is one of the high points of the Old Testament. The Lord descends in the cloud, passes in front of Moses and proclaims his own name: the Lord, the Lord God, compassionate and gracious, slow to anger, abounding in loving devotion and faithfulness, forgiving iniquity, yet by no means leaving the guilty unpunished. These words, spoken just after the golden calf, became Israel's most treasured description of God, and they echo through the psalms and the prophets.

Moses bows to the ground and asks again that God would go with them and forgive. God renews the covenant, repeating some of its central commands, especially against making treaties with the peoples of Canaan or worshipping their gods. Moses stays on the mountain another forty days and nights. When he comes down with the new tablets, he does not know that his face is shining from speaking with the Lord.

=== 35
Moses has come down from the mountain with the new tablets, his face shining, and the covenant has been renewed. Now, at last, the work can begin. The final six chapters of Exodus describe the building of the tabernacle, and to a large extent they repeat, almost word for word, the instructions given earlier. The repetition is deliberate. The point is that everything God commanded is now done, exactly as he commanded it.

Moses gathers the whole community and begins with the Sabbath. Even this holy work must stop on the seventh day. Then he calls for the offering. Only a few chapters ago the people stripped off their gold to make a calf. Now men and women come with willing hearts, bringing brooches, earrings, rings and necklaces, fine yarn and linen, skins, wood, oil and spices. The leaders bring the precious stones for the high priest's garments, and the skilled women spin yarn and goat hair with their own hands.

Finally Moses presents the master craftsmen to the people. Bezalel of the tribe of Judah and Oholiab of the tribe of Dan, filled with skill by God, will lead the work, and they are given the ability to teach others.

=== 36
The offerings have been brought, and the craftsmen are ready. Moses hands over to Bezalel, Oholiab and the other skilled workers everything the people have given. But the gifts keep coming, morning after morning, until the craftsmen come to Moses with an unusual problem: the people are bringing far more than is needed. Moses sends word through the camp, and the people are told to stop giving.

Then the building begins, and it begins, as the instructions did not, with the tent itself. The narrator describes the craftsmen making the ten linen curtains with their cherubim, joined by loops and golden clasps, then the curtains of goat hair for the tent over it, and the coverings of skins. After that come the gold-covered frames of acacia wood, standing on silver bases and held together with crossbars, then the embroidered veil and the curtain for the entrance.

Listeners who have heard chapter twenty-six will recognise the details. The language has simply changed from you shall make to he made.

=== 37
The structure of the tabernacle has been made. Now Bezalel turns to the furniture for its two rooms, and the narrator names him at the start of the chapter.

First comes the ark of acacia wood, overlaid with gold, with its poles for carrying and its golden lid, the mercy seat, with two cherubim of hammered gold facing one another, their wings spread over it. Then he makes the table for the Bread of the Presence, with its plates, dishes, bowls and pitchers of pure gold, and the lampstand, hammered from a single talent of gold, with its seven lamps and almond-blossom cups.

Last in the chapter is the small golden altar of incense, which is to stand before the veil, together with the sacred anointing oil and the fragrant incense. These were the last items described in the instructions on the mountain, and with them the furnishings of the inner tent are complete.

=== 38
The golden furnishings of the tent are finished. Now the work moves outside, to the bronze altar for burnt offerings and the bronze basin for washing. The basin, we are told, was made from the bronze mirrors of the women who served at the entrance to the Tent of Meeting, a detail the instructions did not include. Then comes the courtyard, with its linen curtains, its posts banded with silver and its embroidered gate.

The second half of the chapter is an inventory, an account of the materials, kept by the Levites under the direction of Ithamar, Aaron's son. Bezalel and Oholiab are named once more as the master craftsmen. The quantities are large. About a tonne of gold was given, several tonnes of silver and bronze, and the silver came from the half shekel paid by every man counted in a census, a total of six hundred and three thousand, five hundred and fifty men. The silver was cast into bases for the frames and posts of the sanctuary, and the bronze into the altar, the courtyard bases and the tent pegs.

=== 39
The tabernacle and its furnishings have been made. The last task is the clothing for the priests, and this chapter follows the craftsmen as they weave and stitch the garments described back in chapter twenty-eight. There is the ephod, with gold beaten into thin sheets and cut into threads to weave through the coloured yarn, and the two onyx stones on its shoulders, and the breastpiece with its twelve stones engraved with the names of the tribes. There is the blue robe with its bells and pomegranates, the tunics, turbans and sashes, and the gold plate engraved with the words: holy to the Lord.

Listen for the refrain that runs through this chapter: just as the Lord had commanded Moses. It is repeated again and again, like a seal pressed onto each finished piece.

Then the whole work is brought to Moses, item by item, from the tent and its clasps to the priests' garments. He inspects everything and sees that it has all been done exactly as the Lord commanded. And Moses blesses them. As at the end of the creation account in Genesis, the work is complete, it is examined, and a blessing is spoken.

=== 40
All the pieces of the tabernacle have been made and inspected. Now God tells Moses to put it all together. The date he gives is significant: the first day of the first month, at the beginning of a new year, almost exactly a year after Israel left Egypt.

The chapter describes Moses setting up the tent with his own hands. He lays the bases and raises the frames, spreads the coverings, places the tablets in the ark and the ark behind the veil, and arranges the table, the lampstand and the golden altar in the Holy Place. In the courtyard he sets up the altar of burnt offering and the basin, and hangs the curtain at the gate. Once more the refrain sounds at each step: just as the Lord had commanded him. And at last, the simple sentence: so Moses finished the work.

What happens next is the climax of the whole book. The story that began with Israel enslaved in Egypt, building cities for Pharaoh, ends in a very different place. The cloud that led them out of Egypt and settled on Sinai now comes down upon the tent they have built.
