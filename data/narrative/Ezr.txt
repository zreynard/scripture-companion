=== intro
The book of Ezra opens with a royal proclamation. Cyrus, king of Persia, has made himself master of Babylon, and in the first year of his reign there he announces that the God of heaven has charged him to build a house in Jerusalem. Any of that God's people who wish to go home and build it are free to go. For the Jews living in exile in Babylonia, it was the news their prophets had told them to wait for.

To feel the weight of that moment, it helps to remember how they came to be in Babylon at all. After the reign of Solomon the kingdom of Israel split in two. The northern kingdom fell to the Assyrians late in the eighth century before Christ, and its people were deported and replaced with settlers from other lands, who made their homes around the city of Samaria. The southern kingdom of Judah, with Jerusalem as its capital and a king of David's line on its throne, lasted more than a century longer. Then the armies of Babylon came. Nebuchadnezzar carried off the young king Jehoiachin and thousands of his people, and some years later, in five hundred and eighty-six before Christ, his army burned Jerusalem, broke down its walls and destroyed the temple Solomon had built. The survivors were marched away to Babylonia, where they settled beside its rivers and canals. The prophet Jeremiah had told them the exile would last seventy years, and that afterwards God would bring them back.

The empire that took them did not last. Cyrus the Great, a Persian prince from the highlands of what is now southwestern Iran, united the Medes and the Persians and swept across the ancient world. In five hundred and thirty-nine before Christ Babylon opened its gates to him almost without a fight. Cyrus preferred to win the goodwill of conquered peoples, and he allowed communities that had been uprooted to return and restore their sanctuaries. Under him and his successors Persia became the largest empire the world had yet seen, reaching from the Aegean Sea to the borders of India. It was divided into great provinces, and Judah became a small district within the province the documents in this book call the region west of the Euphrates. Its business was done in Aramaic, the common language of the empire, and several of the official letters quoted in Ezra are preserved in that language.

The book falls into two parts, separated by a long silence. The first six chapters follow the earliest returns from exile, led by Sheshbazzar, by Zerubbabel, a descendant of King David, and by Jeshua the high priest. The returning exiles rebuild the altar and lay the foundation of a new temple, but the peoples of the land oppose them, and the work stands still for about sixteen years. Then two prophets, Haggai and Zechariah, whose messages survive in the books that bear their names, rouse the people to build again, and the temple is finished in the reign of Darius, roughly seventy years after the first one was destroyed.

Between chapters six and seven nearly sixty years pass without a word. The story of Esther belongs in that gap, in the reign of King Xerxes, among the Jews who stayed behind in Persia. When the book resumes, Ezra himself arrives: a priest descended from Aaron and a scribe devoted to the Law of Moses, sent from Babylon by King Artaxerxes with royal authority and generous gifts. The final chapters tell of his journey and of the crisis that meets him in Jerusalem, and much of this part is written in Ezra's own voice, as a personal memoir.

In the Hebrew Bible, Ezra and Nehemiah were counted as a single book, and they tell one continuous story. Jewish tradition credited Ezra with writing it. Whoever gave the book its final shape drew on lists of families, official correspondence and the first-person records of Ezra and Nehemiah. The work is closely tied to the books of Chronicles: the last words of Second Chronicles are the first words of Ezra, so that one picks up where the other leaves off.

This is a story of small things. There is no king in Jerusalem now, no army and no independence. The returning exiles are a modest, often frightened community living in the ruins of a famous city, on the edge of a vast empire whose kings hold their future in their hands. Their questions are about identity and faithfulness: how to worship the God of their fathers again, how to live by his Law, and how to remain a distinct people among the nations. Running through the book is a quiet refrain, that the good hand of the Lord was upon them, even through the decisions of foreign kings.

The story continues in the book of Nehemiah, which begins about thirteen years after Ezra's arrival, when the walls of Jerusalem are still in ruins. Together these books, with the prophets Haggai, Zechariah and Malachi, bring the history of the Old Testament to its close.

=== 1
The book begins with a date: the first year of Cyrus king of Persia, meaning his first year as ruler of Babylon. Nearly fifty years have passed since Jerusalem was burned, and the writer sees the hand of God behind the politics: the Lord stirs the spirit of Cyrus, so that the word spoken through Jeremiah, the promise of a return after seventy years, might be fulfilled.

The chapter gives the text of the king's proclamation. The God of heaven has given Cyrus the kingdoms of the earth and appointed him to build a house in Jerusalem, and any of God's people may go up and build it, helped on their way by their neighbours.

Many exiles have settled into life in Babylonia and will stay. Those who prepare to leave are described simply as everyone whose spirit God had stirred. Then Cyrus brings out the gold and silver vessels Nebuchadnezzar had taken from the temple, the same vessels the book of Daniel describes being used at Belshazzar's feast on the night Babylon fell. They are counted out to Sheshbazzar, the prince of Judah, a leader about whom little else is known.

=== 2
Before the story goes further, the book pauses to record who went home. This long list is a register of the returning community, family by family and town by town.

It opens with the leaders, among them Zerubbabel and Jeshua, who will guide the work in the chapters to come. A Nehemiah and a Mordecai appear among them, but these are not the famous men of the books of Nehemiah and Esther, who lived generations later. Then come the lay families, grouped by ancestor or by the towns of Judah and Benjamin they came from, followed by priests, Levites, singers, gatekeepers and temple servants.

A list like this mattered a great deal. It established who belonged to Israel, who could serve at the altar, and which families had a claim to which towns. A few families could not prove their descent, and some priests were barred from serving until the question could be settled.

The total comes to more than forty-two thousand people, with their servants and animals. The same list appears again in the book of Nehemiah.

=== 3
The returning exiles have settled in their towns. Now, in the seventh month, the season of Israel's great autumn festivals, they gather as one in Jerusalem.

Two men lead them. Jeshua son of Jozadak is the high priest; his grandfather Seraiah had been the chief priest in Jerusalem when the city fell. Zerubbabel son of Shealtiel is a grandson of Jehoiachin, the king of Judah taken captive to Babylon, and so an heir of David's royal house. Before there are walls or a temple, they rebuild the altar on its old foundation and begin the daily sacrifices again, though they are afraid of the peoples around them.

Cedar from Lebanon is brought down the coast to Joppa, as it had been for Solomon's temple centuries before. In the second year the foundation is laid, and the priests and Levites lead the people in a song of praise. But not every voice is glad. Some of the older men remember the first temple, and the chapter ends with a sound in which joy and grief can no longer be told apart.

=== 4
The foundation has been laid. Now the book turns to the opposition that will dog the returning exiles for generations.

It begins with an offer. People from the surrounding region come to Zerubbabel and the family heads, asking to help build. They say they have worshipped Israel's God ever since the Assyrian king Esar-haddon settled them in the land. They are descended from the peoples brought in after the northern kingdom fell, whose worship, as the book of Kings describes it, mixed the Lord with other gods. Zerubbabel and Jeshua refuse, and the refusal turns to hostility. The peoples of the land set out to frighten the builders and hire advisers to work against them at the Persian court.

The middle of the chapter can be confusing, because it leaps forward in time. To show how long this hostility lasted, the writer gathers later episodes from the reigns of Xerxes and Artaxerxes, when the quarrel was over the city walls rather than the temple, and the book switches from Hebrew into Aramaic, the language of the letters it quotes. Only in the final sentence does the story return to the temple, and to a long, discouraging silence on the building site.

=== 5
The work on the temple has stood still for about sixteen years. A new king, Darius, now rules Persia, having seized the throne after a period of upheaval and rebellion across the empire.

In his second year, God sends two prophets. Haggai and Zechariah, a member of the priestly family of Iddo, begin to speak to the people of Judah and Jerusalem. Haggai asks how the people can live in finished houses while the house of the Lord lies in ruins, and Zechariah's visions speak of the Lord returning to Jerusalem. Stirred by their words, Zerubbabel and Jeshua start building again, with the prophets beside them.

It is not long before the authorities take notice. Tattenai, the governor of the whole region west of the Euphrates, comes to inspect the work and demands to know who gave permission. The builders are allowed to carry on while he writes to the king. His letter, quoted in full, preserves the elders' reply, in which they tell a foreign official who they are, why the first temple fell, and what Cyrus had decreed.

=== 6
Tattenai's letter has gone to Darius, asking whether Cyrus really authorised the rebuilding. Now the king orders a search of the royal archives.

The record is found not in Babylon itself but at Ecbatana, the old capital of the Medes, where the Persian kings kept a summer residence. It is a memorandum of Cyrus's decree, ordering that the costs be met from the royal treasury. Darius confirms it and goes further. The very officials who questioned the work are now told to fund it from the taxes of their region and supply the animals for sacrifice, and anyone who interferes faces a fearsome penalty.

So the work goes ahead, strengthened by the preaching of Haggai and Zechariah, and the temple is completed late in the winter of the sixth year of Darius. The returned exiles dedicate it with sacrifices for all twelve tribes, and in the spring they keep the Passover with joy. Here the Aramaic section of the book comes to an end, and the story falls silent for nearly sixty years.

=== 7
Nearly sixty years have passed since the temple was dedicated. Darius and his son Xerxes have come and gone, and it is in the reign of Xerxes that the story of Esther takes place. Now Artaxerxes sits on the throne of Persia, most likely the first king of that name, and the year is his seventh, around the middle of the fifth century before Christ.

Here at last we meet the man the book is named after. Ezra is introduced with a priestly pedigree running back to Aaron, the brother of Moses. He is a scribe, skilled in the Law of Moses, and he has set his heart to study it, to keep it and to teach it in Israel.

Ezra sets out from Babylon with a company of exiles, and the journey takes four months. He carries a remarkable letter from the king, quoted in Aramaic, giving him funds for the temple and authority to appoint judges and teach the Law throughout the region west of the Euphrates. At the end of the chapter the voice changes. Ezra begins to speak for himself, and his own account continues from here.

=== 8
Ezra is now telling his own story. He begins with a register of the family heads who travelled with him from Babylon, a company of about fifteen hundred men, with women and children besides. Among them is Hattush, a descendant of King David.

The travellers gather at a canal called Ahava, somewhere in Babylonia, and camp there for three days. Ezra reviews the company and finds that no Levites have volunteered, so he sends messengers to a place called Casiphia, where a community of Levites and temple servants lives, to bring men to serve in the house of God.

The journey ahead is long and dangerous, and the caravan will carry a fortune in silver and gold. Ezra has told the king that the hand of God protects those who seek him, and he is ashamed now to ask for soldiers, so instead he calls a fast. Before setting out he entrusts the treasure to chosen priests and Levites, weighed and counted, to be weighed again on arrival in Jerusalem. The chapter follows them all the way to the temple.

=== 9
Ezra has arrived, the treasure has been delivered, and the king's orders have been handed to the officials of the region. Some time later, the leaders of the community come to Ezra with a troubling report.

Many of the returned exiles, including priests, Levites and leading officials, have married women from the surrounding peoples. The leaders' concern is with the practices of those peoples, which they liken to the abominations of the Canaanites of old. The Law of Moses had warned Israel against such marriages because they would draw the people into the worship of other gods, and Israel's history, from the wilderness to King Solomon, had shown how real that danger was. To Ezra it seems that the community saved from exile is repeating the very unfaithfulness that sent their fathers into it.

His response is the public grief of the ancient world. He tears his clothes, pulls hair from his head and beard, and sits stunned until the evening sacrifice. Then he kneels and prays. The prayer that fills the rest of the chapter is a confession, and Ezra, though he has done nothing wrong himself, prays throughout as one of the guilty.

=== 10
Ezra's prayer is the last word of his memoir; the final chapter tells his story from outside. He is lying face down before the house of God, and a great crowd of men, women and children has gathered, weeping with him.

A man named Shecaniah, from one of the families listed among the returned exiles, steps forward with a proposal: the people should make a covenant to send away the foreign wives and their children, and Ezra should lead it. A summons goes out to every town in Judah. Within three days the men of Judah and Benjamin are sitting in the open square before the temple in the ninth month, at the start of winter, trembling in the heavy rain.

This is one of the hardest passages in the Old Testament, and the book does not soften it. The people agree, and a commission sits for three months to examine each case. The book ends with the names of those found to have married foreign women, beginning with the families of the priests. There is no closing word of comment, and the book of Nehemiah will show that the issue was far from over.
