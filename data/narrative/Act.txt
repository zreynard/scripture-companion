=== intro
The book of Acts is the second volume of Luke's work, and it begins where his gospel ends. Once again it is addressed to Theophilus. Once again the setting is Jerusalem, with a small group of Jesus' followers waiting, as he told them to, for power from on high. From that room the story runs outward for some thirty years, from the early thirties of the first century to the early sixties, and from the temple courts of Jerusalem to a rented house in Rome.

The traditional title, the Acts of the Apostles, was added later, and it promises more than the book sets out to give. Most of the Twelve are hardly mentioned after the opening chapters. Luke follows two men above all, Peter in the first half of the book and Paul in the second, and behind both of them the Holy Spirit, who drives the story on at every turn. Luke himself appears at intervals. In several passages, beginning in chapter sixteen, the narrative changes from they to we, and the writer is evidently travelling with Paul. Those passages include the long voyage to Rome at the end of the book.

Jesus' last words to his disciples, near the start of chapter one, give the book its map. You will be my witnesses in Jerusalem, and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth. The first seven chapters stay in Jerusalem. The coming of the Spirit at Pentecost, the first preaching, the life of the first community and the growing opposition of the temple authorities lead to the death of Stephen, the first to die for his faith in Jesus. In chapters eight to twelve the believers are scattered by persecution, and the message moves out into Judea and Samaria and beyond. Philip preaches to Samaritans and to an Ethiopian official. Saul of Tarsus, a fierce persecutor, is stopped on the road to Damascus. Peter is sent to the house of a Roman centurion in Caesarea, and Gentiles receive the Spirit. In Antioch, the great city of Syria, a mixed congregation of Jews and Gentiles grows up, and there the disciples are first called Christians.

From chapter thirteen the story belongs to Saul, now known by his Roman name, Paul. The church at Antioch sends him out with Barnabas, and Luke follows him on three great journeys. The first takes them to Cyprus and to the towns of southern Asia Minor, in what is now Turkey. Then, in chapter fifteen, a council in Jerusalem settles whether Gentile believers must be circumcised and keep the law of Moses. On the second journey Paul and Silas cross the sea from Asia Minor into Macedonia, in northern Greece, and plant churches at Philippi, Thessalonica and Berea before Paul moves on to Athens and spends eighteen months at Corinth. The third journey centres on Ephesus, where Paul stays more than two years.

The world of these journeys is the Roman Empire at the height of its peace. Roman roads and shipping lanes tie it together, and Greek is spoken in every port. In almost every city there is a Jewish community with its synagogue, and often Gentiles who worship Israel's God without becoming Jews. Paul usually begins there. Luke names Roman officials with care, proconsuls, city magistrates and governors, and his titles have often been confirmed by inscriptions. One of them, naming the proconsul Gallio, allows Paul's time in Corinth to be dated to around the year fifty or fifty-one, a fixed point for the whole chronology.

Acts never mentions that Paul wrote letters, but many of them belong inside its story, and following Acts makes them easier to place. The letters to the Thessalonians were written from Corinth, not long after Paul had been driven out of Thessalonica. The first letter to the Corinthians was written from Ephesus, the second from Macedonia, and Romans from Corinth, as Paul prepared to carry a gift of money to Jerusalem. Galatians may belong close to the council of chapter fifteen, though its date is debated. Ephesians, Philippians, Colossians and Philemon were written in prison, traditionally in Rome, though some think of Caesarea or Ephesus.

The last quarter of the book slows down. Paul goes up to Jerusalem knowing that trouble awaits him, and he is seized in the temple courts and rescued from the mob by Roman soldiers. Two years of custody follow at Caesarea, the Roman capital of Judea, where he defends himself before the governors Felix and Festus and before King Agrippa. Appealing to Caesar, he is sent to Rome by sea, and Luke's account of the voyage and the shipwreck off Malta is one of the most vivid descriptions of ancient seafaring to survive.

Acts ends with Paul in Rome under house arrest for two years, receiving visitors and preaching openly. Luke does not say how his trial ended. Early Christian tradition holds that both Peter and Paul died in Rome in the sixties, in the reign of Nero, but that lies beyond the book.

In the New Testament, Acts stands between the gospels and the letters, and it joins them. It tells how a movement that began among the Jewish followers of a crucified teacher from Galilee came, within a single generation, to be found in the great cities of the empire, among Jews and Gentiles alike.

=== 1
Luke begins his second volume as he began the first, with a word to Theophilus. He reminds him that the gospel told what Jesus began to do and to teach, and he fills in what happened after the resurrection. For forty days Jesus appeared to the apostles and spoke with them about the kingdom of God. He told them not to leave Jerusalem, but to wait for the Holy Spirit the Father had promised.

The apostles still have one question on their minds: will he now restore the kingdom to Israel? Jesus' answer turns them outward, and it sketches the course of the whole book, from Jerusalem to Judea and Samaria and to the ends of the earth. Then, on the Mount of Olives, he is taken up from their sight.

The rest of the chapter takes place in the days of waiting. The eleven apostles are named, and with them in the upper room are the women, Mary the mother of Jesus, and his brothers, who during his ministry had not believed in him. About a hundred and twenty believers are gathered when Peter stands up to speak about the place left empty by Judas.

=== 2
Ten days after Jesus was taken up, the believers are together in Jerusalem on the day of Pentecost. This was the Feast of Weeks, the harvest festival that fell fifty days after Passover, and one of the occasions when Jews came on pilgrimage to the city from far and wide.

That matters for what happens next. When the Spirit comes, with a sound like a rushing wind and what look like tongues of fire, the believers begin to speak in other languages, and the crowd that gathers includes Jews from all over the known world. Luke's list sweeps from Parthia, beyond the eastern edge of the Roman Empire, through Mesopotamia and Asia Minor, to Egypt, Libya, Crete, Arabia and Rome itself. Each one hears the wonders of God in his own language.

Then Peter stands up with the other apostles and gives the first sermon of the church. A few weeks earlier he had denied knowing Jesus in a high priest's courtyard. Now he speaks to the crowds of Jerusalem about the man they crucified, drawing on the prophet Joel and on the Psalms of David. The chapter ends with a portrait of the community that forms in the days that follow.

=== 3
The believers are still meeting daily in the temple courts, as the end of chapter two described. Now Luke tells one story from those days. Peter and John are going up to the temple for the afternoon prayer, at the ninth hour, about three o'clock. At the gate called Beautiful, probably one of the gates leading into the inner courts, sits a man who has been lame from birth, carried there every day to beg from the worshippers.

Peter and John appear together often in these early chapters. They had been partners in the fishing business on the Sea of Galilee, and they were among the three closest to Jesus. Now they are leaders in Jerusalem.

What happens at the gate draws a large crowd to Solomon's Colonnade, a long covered walkway along the eastern side of the temple's outer court. Peter seizes the moment to speak again. This second sermon is aimed squarely at the people of Jerusalem, who stood before Pilate and asked for a murderer to be released in Jesus' place. Peter calls them to repentance, and he grounds his appeal in Moses, in the prophets from Samuel onwards, and in the promise made to Abraham.

=== 4
Peter is still speaking in Solomon's Colonnade when the temple authorities arrive: priests, the captain of the temple guard, and members of the Sadducees, the party of the high priestly families. They are disturbed above all that the apostles are proclaiming resurrection from the dead through Jesus. Peter and John are held overnight, and yet the number of believing men grows to about five thousand.

The next morning they stand before the council, the Sanhedrin, and Luke names the men facing them: Annas, Caiaphas and others of the high priest's family. These are the same leaders who handed Jesus over to Pilate only weeks before. The council members notice that Peter and John are ordinary men without formal training in the law, and they recognise them as men who had been with Jesus.

After their release, the two return to the believers, who pray together in the words of the second Psalm about rulers raging against the Lord's anointed. The chapter closes with a picture of a community sharing its possessions, and with a man who will be important later: Joseph, a Levite from the island of Cyprus, whom the apostles call Barnabas, meaning son of encouragement.

=== 5
Barnabas has just sold a field and laid the money at the apostles' feet. The chapter opens with a couple who do the same, or appear to. Ananias and his wife Sapphira sell a property and keep back part of the money while pretending to give it all. What follows is a sobering moment in the life of the young community, and Luke tells it without softening.

Then the story returns to the wider city. The apostles' healing work draws the sick from the towns around Jerusalem, and the high priest and his circle, all Sadducees, are filled with jealousy. They arrest the apostles and put them in the public jail, but the next morning the prisoners are found teaching in the temple courts again.

Brought before the Sanhedrin once more, the apostles refuse to be silent. The council is ready to have them killed when a respected Pharisee named Gamaliel, one of the most honoured teachers of the law in his day, rises to counsel caution. He reminds them of other movements that rose and collapsed when their leaders died. We will later learn that Saul of Tarsus had studied under this same Gamaliel. The apostles are flogged and released, and they go out rejoicing.

=== 6
The believers in Jerusalem are growing fast, and growth brings strain. The community now includes two groups of Jews. The Hebraic Jews were local, at home in Aramaic and the ways of the land. The Grecian Jews had come from the cities of the dispersion, spoke Greek as their first language, and many had settled in Jerusalem, some perhaps to end their days near the temple. When the Grecian widows are overlooked in the daily distribution of food, the complaint reaches the apostles.

The answer is to appoint seven men to oversee the work. All seven have Greek names, and one, Nicolas, is a convert from Antioch. Two of them will matter greatly in the chapters ahead. One is Philip, not the apostle of that name but a different man. The other is Stephen, full of faith and of the Holy Spirit.

Stephen's preaching soon brings him into conflict with members of the Synagogue of the Freedmen, probably descendants of Jews once enslaved and later freed, with links to Cyrene, Alexandria, Asia and Cilicia, the province that included Saul's home city of Tarsus. They bring him before the Sanhedrin with charges that echo those once brought against Jesus: that he speaks against the temple and the law.

=== 7
Stephen stands before the Sanhedrin, accused of speaking against the temple and the law of Moses. The high priest asks him whether the charges are true. What follows is the longest speech in Acts.

Stephen does not answer directly. Instead he retells the story of Israel, from God's call to Abraham in Mesopotamia, through Joseph in Egypt and Moses in the wilderness, to the tabernacle and the temple built by Solomon. Listeners who know Genesis and Exodus will recognise every step, but Stephen chooses his emphases with care. God appears to Abraham far from the promised land, and he is with Joseph in Egypt and with Moses at a burning bush in the desert. And again and again Israel turns against the deliverers God sends, first Joseph, then Moses. The speech builds toward the temple, and toward the claim that the Most High does not dwell in houses made by human hands.

At the end Stephen turns on his judges directly, and the hearing becomes a riot. He is dragged outside the city and stoned. Luke adds a quiet detail: the witnesses lay their cloaks at the feet of a young man named Saul.

=== 8
Stephen is dead, and the young man named Saul approved of his killing. We will hear his story fully in the next chapter. For now Luke shows him as the leading persecutor of the church, going from house to house and dragging believers off to prison. Persecution breaks out against the believers in Jerusalem, and all but the apostles are scattered through Judea and Samaria. Those who scatter take the message with them, and the second stage of Jesus' charge, Judea and Samaria, begins to be fulfilled.

Philip, another of the seven chosen in chapter six, goes north to a city in Samaria. Jews and Samaritans had long kept apart, yet the Samaritans welcome his message. Among those who believe is a man named Simon, who has amazed the city with his sorcery, and whose name later gave the word simony to the buying of spiritual office. Peter and John come from Jerusalem to see what is happening.

Then Philip is sent south to the desert road that runs from Jerusalem toward Gaza. There he meets a court official from the kingdom Luke calls Ethiopia, the ancient land of Cush on the upper Nile, in what is now Sudan. He is treasurer to the queen, known by the title Candace, and he is riding home in his chariot, reading the prophet Isaiah aloud.

=== 9
Saul, the young man who looked after the cloaks at Stephen's stoning, was born in Tarsus, a prosperous city in Cilicia on the south-eastern coast of Asia Minor, and educated in Jerusalem as a Pharisee. Not content with harrying believers in Jerusalem, he now seeks letters from the high priest to arrest followers of the Way, as the movement was then called, in the synagogues of Damascus, an ancient city in Syria some hundred and fifty miles to the north-east.

What happens near Damascus is told three times in Acts, here and twice more in Paul's own defence speeches. A disciple of the city named Ananias, not the man of chapter five, is sent to the house where Saul is staying on Straight Street. Saul soon begins preaching in the synagogues, until a plot against his life ends in an escape over the city wall in a basket. In his letter to the Galatians Paul adds that he spent time in Arabia and returned to Damascus, and that three years passed before he went up to Jerusalem. Barnabas, the generous Levite from Cyprus, is the one who vouches for him there, before threats send him home to Tarsus.

The chapter ends with Peter travelling in the coastal plain, at Lydda and at Joppa, the old seaport of Jerusalem. He lodges with a tanner named Simon, a trade many Jews avoided because it meant handling dead animals.

=== 10
Peter is staying in Joppa, in the house of Simon the tanner by the sea. About thirty miles up the coast lies Caesarea, a splendid port built by Herod the Great and now the seat of the Roman governors of Judea. This chapter moves between the two towns.

In Caesarea lives Cornelius, a centurion of the unit known as the Italian Regiment. He is a Gentile, but he is one of those Luke calls God-fearing, a man who worships Israel's God, prays regularly and gives generously, without having become a Jew. An angel tells him to send for Peter.

The next day, at noon, Peter goes up on the flat roof of the tanner's house to pray. He is hungry, and he falls into a trance in which he sees a great sheet let down from heaven, full of animals the law of Moses forbade Jews to eat. To understand what follows, it helps to know how strictly many Jews kept apart from Gentiles. A devout Jew would not normally enter a Gentile's house or share his table. When Cornelius' messengers arrive at the gate, Peter has to decide whether to go with them, and what he finds waiting in Caesarea will change the church for good.

=== 11
News travels fast. By the time Peter returns to Jerusalem, the believers there have heard that Gentiles in Caesarea have received the word of God, and some of the Jewish believers are troubled that Peter entered a Gentile house and ate with uncircumcised men. Peter tells the story again from the beginning, and the matter is settled, at least for now.

Then Luke turns north, to a development that will shape the rest of the book. Some of those scattered after Stephen's death have reached Antioch, on the Orontes river in Syria, one of the largest cities of the Roman world. There, men from Cyprus and Cyrene begin speaking about Jesus not only to Jews but to Greeks, and many believe. It is the first church we hear of where large numbers of Gentiles and Jews belong together.

The Jerusalem church sends Barnabas to see what is happening, and Barnabas goes on to Tarsus to find Saul, who has been there for some years. The two spend a full year teaching in Antioch, and it is there that the disciples are first called Christians. The chapter ends with a prophet named Agabus predicting a famine, which came in the reign of the emperor Claudius, and with Barnabas and Saul carrying relief to the believers in Judea.

=== 12
While Barnabas and Saul are carrying famine relief to Judea, Luke turns back to Jerusalem, where the believers face a new kind of danger. The king named here is Herod Agrippa the First, a grandson of Herod the Great. He had grown up partly in Rome among the imperial family, and from the year forty-one the emperor Claudius allowed him to rule Judea as king. He was careful to win the favour of his Jewish subjects.

Herod has James, the brother of John, put to death with the sword. He is the first of the Twelve to die for his faith. Then, at Passover time, Herod arrests Peter and holds him under heavy guard.

Two new names appear in this chapter. Mary, in whose house the believers are praying, is the mother of John, also called Mark, who will soon travel with Barnabas and Saul and is traditionally the author of the second gospel. And James, to whom Peter sends word, is the brother of Jesus, now a leader of the church in Jerusalem.

The chapter ends in Caesarea with Herod's sudden death, an event the Jewish historian Josephus also describes, and which he places in the year forty-four. Then Barnabas and Saul return to Antioch, bringing Mark with them.

=== 13
Barnabas and Saul are back in Antioch, and the church there has grown into a community with prophets and teachers of its own. Luke names them, and the list shows how varied it has become: Barnabas from Cyprus, Simeon called Niger, Lucius from Cyrene in North Africa, Manaen, who had been brought up with Herod Antipas, and Saul. Now, during worship, the Spirit calls Barnabas and Saul to a new work, and the church sends them out. This is the beginning of what is usually called Paul's first missionary journey, probably in the late forties of the first century.

They sail from Seleucia, the port of Antioch, to Cyprus, Barnabas' home island, with John Mark as their helper. At Paphos, the seat of the Roman governor, they meet the proconsul Sergius Paulus, and from this point Luke calls Saul by his Roman name, Paul. Soon it is no longer Barnabas and Saul but Paul and his companions.

From Cyprus they cross to the mainland of Asia Minor, where Mark leaves them and goes home, and they climb inland to a different Antioch, Pisidian Antioch, a Roman colony on the high plateau. There, in the synagogue, Luke gives us Paul's first recorded sermon, and its reception sets a pattern that will be repeated in city after city.

=== 14
Driven out of Pisidian Antioch, Paul and Barnabas travel east along the Roman road to Iconium, and then to Lystra and Derbe, smaller towns in the district of Lycaonia. All these places lay within the Roman province of Galatia, and many think these are the churches to which Paul later wrote his letter to the Galatians, though some place those readers further north.

At Iconium the familiar pattern recurs: preaching in the synagogue, many believers among Jews and Greeks, and growing opposition, until the apostles learn of a plot to stone them and move on. Lystra is different. It is a country town, where the people speak their own Lycaonian language, and there is no synagogue mentioned. When a man lame from birth is healed, the crowd decides that the gods have come down in human form. An old tale of the region told of Zeus and Hermes visiting in disguise, and the priest of Zeus prepares a sacrifice. Paul's short speech here is the first in Acts addressed to people with no background in Israel's Scriptures.

The welcome does not last. Before the chapter ends, the missionaries retrace their steps, appoint elders in each new church, and sail back to Antioch in Syria, where the journey began.

=== 15
Paul and Barnabas are back in Antioch, and they have reported how God opened the door of faith to the Gentiles. But not everyone welcomes the news. Some men come down from Judea and teach the Gentile believers that unless they are circumcised according to the law of Moses, they cannot be saved. The question is enormous. Must a Gentile become a Jew in order to belong to Israel's Messiah?

The church at Antioch sends Paul and Barnabas up to Jerusalem to put the matter to the apostles and elders, and this chapter records the meeting usually called the Council of Jerusalem, probably around the year forty-nine. Peter speaks, recalling the house of Cornelius; it is his last appearance in Acts. Then James, the brother of Jesus, gives the judgement, drawing on the prophet Amos. Paul's letter to the Galatians wrestles with the same question, and some think it was written shortly before this meeting.

The decision is sent to Antioch in a letter, carried by two leaders of the Jerusalem church. One of them is Silas, a prophet, called Silvanus in Paul's letters, and like Paul a Roman citizen. He will soon become Paul's companion. The chapter ends with a sharp disagreement between Paul and Barnabas over John Mark, who had left them on the first journey. They part, and Paul sets out with Silas.

=== 16
Paul and Silas have set out from Antioch on what is usually called the second missionary journey. They travel overland, through Paul's home region of Cilicia, to the churches founded on the first journey. At Lystra they are joined by a young disciple named Timothy, the son of a Jewish mother who has become a believer and a Greek father. He will become one of Paul's closest companions, and two letters in the New Testament will bear his name.

Then the route becomes uncertain. Twice the Spirit closes a door, first to the province of Asia and then to Bithynia in the north, and the travellers come down to Troas, a port on the Aegean coast near the site of ancient Troy. There Paul has a vision of a man of Macedonia appealing for help. At this point the narrative quietly changes from they to we. The writer has joined the party.

They cross the sea into Macedonia, in northern Greece, and come to Philippi, a Roman colony settled with retired soldiers, proud of its Roman status. There seem to be few Jews and no synagogue, only a place of prayer by the river outside the gate. Among the women gathered there is Lydia, a trader in costly purple cloth from Thyatira in Asia Minor. Years later Paul will write to the church that begins in her house, in the letter to the Philippians.

=== 17
Released from prison and asked to leave Philippi, Paul and Silas travel west along the Via Egnatia, the great Roman road across Macedonia, to Thessalonica, the capital of the province and a busy port. Timothy is with them, though Luke the narrator seems to have stayed behind in Philippi, for the we passages stop here.

In Thessalonica Paul preaches in the synagogue for three Sabbaths, and both Jews and God-fearing Greeks believe. The opposition that follows is fierce. A mob attacks the house of a believer named Jason, and the charge laid before the city officials is political: that these men proclaim another king besides Caesar. Paul is sent away by night, first to Berea, where the hearers examine the Scriptures daily, and then, as trouble follows him, south to Athens.

Athens was no longer a great power, but it was still the most famous centre of learning in the Greek world, full of temples and statues, and home to rival schools of philosophy. Among them were the Epicureans, who taught that the gods were remote and the aim of life was a quiet happiness, and the Stoics, who saw a divine reason running through all things. Paul debates in the marketplace and is brought to the Areopagus, the council that took its name from the Hill of Ares, to explain himself.

=== 18
Paul leaves Athens for Corinth, some fifty miles to the west. Corinth was a very different city: a Roman colony refounded about a century earlier, capital of the province of Achaia, and a thriving commercial centre on the narrow isthmus between two seas, with a port on each side. It was rich, cosmopolitan, and notorious even in its own day for its loose living.

There Paul meets Aquila and his wife Priscilla, Jews who had been expelled from Rome when the emperor Claudius ordered the Jews to leave the city. They share his trade of tentmaking, and they become lifelong friends and fellow workers. Soon Silas and Timothy arrive from Macedonia with news of the believers there, and it was from Corinth, at about this time, that Paul wrote his two letters to the Thessalonians.

Paul stays in Corinth eighteen months. Near the end of that time he is brought before the proconsul Gallio, the brother of the philosopher Seneca, whose term of office is dated by an inscription to the early fifties. Then Paul sails east with Priscilla and Aquila, leaves them at Ephesus, and travels on to Jerusalem and back to Antioch. Before long he sets out again through Galatia and Phrygia.

Meanwhile a new figure arrives in Ephesus: Apollos, a learned and eloquent Jew from Alexandria in Egypt, who knows the way of the Lord but only the baptism of John. Priscilla and Aquila take him aside, and he goes on to Corinth.

=== 19
Paul's third missionary journey has brought him overland through the interior of Asia Minor to Ephesus, as he promised when he passed through briefly in chapter eighteen. Ephesus was the leading city of the province of Asia, a great port near the western coast of what is now Turkey. Its pride was the temple of the goddess Artemis, one of the seven wonders of the ancient world, and the city was also known for its magicians and their spells.

Paul stays here longer than anywhere else on his travels, some three years in all. He begins with a small group of disciples who know only John's baptism, then preaches for three months in the synagogue, and then, when opposition grows, teaches daily for two years in a lecture hall belonging to a man named Tyrannus. Luke says that from Ephesus the message reached the whole province, and it was probably in these years that churches were founded in nearby towns such as Colossae. It was also from Ephesus that Paul wrote the letter we call First Corinthians.

Near the end of his stay Paul resolves to go to Jerusalem by way of Macedonia and Greece, and then to see Rome. Before he leaves, a dispute about the trade in silver shrines of Artemis fills the great theatre of Ephesus with an angry crowd.

=== 20
The riot in Ephesus is over, and Paul leaves for Macedonia, as he had planned. Luke covers the next year or so in a few sentences, but Paul's letters help to fill them in. It was in Macedonia that he wrote Second Corinthians, after an anxious time waiting for news from Corinth. Then he spends three months in Greece, almost certainly at Corinth, and it was during that winter that he wrote his letter to the Romans, telling them he was about to carry a gift for the poor believers in Jerusalem.

A plot against his life makes him change his route, and he goes back north through Macedonia with a group of companions from the churches he has founded. They may well be the delegates who carry that gift. At Philippi the narrative returns to we: the writer is with Paul again, and he will stay with him to the end of the book.

At Troas, over a long night of teaching, a young man named Eutychus falls from a window. Then the ship works its way down the coast of Asia Minor, island by island, to Miletus. Paul is hurrying to reach Jerusalem by Pentecost, so he does not stop at Ephesus. Instead he sends for the elders of the Ephesian church, and the chapter closes with his farewell to them, the only speech in Acts that Paul addresses to church leaders.

=== 21
The farewell at Miletus is over, and Paul's ship sails on. Luke tracks the voyage port by port, to Rhodes and Patara, then across the open sea south of Cyprus to Tyre on the Phoenician coast, and on to Ptolemais and Caesarea. At each stop there are believers, and again and again they warn Paul through the Spirit of what awaits him in Jerusalem. At Caesarea the party stays with Philip the evangelist, the one who preached in Samaria back in chapter eight, now settled there with four daughters who prophesy. The prophet Agabus, who foretold the famine years before, comes down from Judea with a vivid warning.

In Jerusalem, James and the elders welcome Paul, but they are worried. Rumours have spread among the many Jewish believers, all zealous for the law, that Paul teaches Jews living among the Gentiles to abandon Moses. They suggest a public gesture of loyalty in the temple.

It is there that Paul is recognised by Jews from Asia, who accuse him of bringing a Gentile into the inner courts. Stone notices, some of which have been found, warned that any Gentile who passed beyond the barrier would have himself to blame for his death. The city erupts, and Roman soldiers rush down from the fortress overlooking the temple to rescue Paul from the crowd.

=== 22
Paul has been rescued from the mob in the temple courts and carried up the steps toward the Roman barracks, in the Antonia fortress that overlooked the temple from the north-west. The commander has mistaken him for an Egyptian rebel, and is surprised to hear him speak Greek. Now, standing on the steps, Paul asks to address the crowd, and when he speaks to them in Hebrew, probably meaning Aramaic, the everyday language of Judea, they fall silent.

This is the second of three accounts in Acts of what happened on the road to Damascus, and this time Paul tells it himself, shaped for the people in front of him. He stresses everything that makes him one of them: born in Tarsus but brought up in Jerusalem, trained under Gamaliel, as zealous for God as they are, once a persecutor of the very people he now belongs to. The crowd listens until he reaches one particular word.

The commander, unable to make sense of the uproar, orders Paul to be flogged and questioned. It was a brutal method of interrogation, but the law did not allow it to be used on a Roman citizen who had not been tried, and Paul is about to say so. The chapter ends with Paul being brought before the Sanhedrin, the same council that once tried Jesus and Stephen.

=== 23
Paul stands before the Sanhedrin, with the Roman commander looking on. Presiding is the high priest Ananias, not to be confused with the Ananias of Damascus, a man remembered in Jewish sources for his greed and violence. The council itself is divided between Sadducees and Pharisees, and Paul, a Pharisee by training, knows exactly where the fault line lies: the Pharisees believed in resurrection, angels and spirits, and the Sadducees did not.

The hearing collapses into uproar, and Paul is taken back to the barracks. That night the Lord stands by him and tells him that he must testify in Rome as well. The rest of the chapter reads almost like an adventure story. More than forty men swear not to eat or drink until they have killed Paul. His sister's son, the only member of his family we ever meet, hears of the plot.

The commander, whose name we now learn is Claudius Lysias, sends Paul by night under heavy guard to Caesarea, by way of Antipatris, with a letter to the governor. This is Antonius Felix, a former slave of the imperial household who had risen to govern Judea, and whose harsh rule the Roman historian Tacitus later recalled with contempt. Paul is held in the palace built at Caesarea by Herod the Great.

=== 24
Five days after Paul's arrival in Caesarea, his accusers come down from Jerusalem: the high priest Ananias, some elders, and a professional advocate named Tertullus, who will present their case in the proper Roman manner. Hearings before a governor followed a recognised form, and this chapter gives a glimpse of one. Tertullus opens with flattery of Felix, then lays out three charges: that Paul is a troublemaker among the Jews all over the world, a ringleader of the sect of the Nazarenes, and a man who tried to profane the temple.

Paul answers each point. In passing he mentions why he came to Jerusalem at all, to bring gifts for the poor of his nation. It is the only time Acts refers to the collection he had gathered from the Gentile churches, a project that fills several pages of his letters.

Felix knows more about the Way than he lets on, and he adjourns the case. His wife Drusilla is Jewish, a daughter of the Herod Agrippa whose death Luke described in chapter twelve. Felix sends for Paul often, partly out of curiosity and partly, Luke says, in hope of a bribe. Two years pass with Paul in custody, until a new governor, Porcius Festus, arrives, probably around the year fifty-nine or sixty.

=== 25
Porcius Festus has just taken office as governor of Judea, and he inherits Paul's case, unresolved after two years. He would govern only briefly before dying in office, but the Jewish historian Josephus remembered him as a more conscientious ruler than Felix. Within days of his arrival he goes up to Jerusalem, where the chief priests press him to bring Paul there for trial, planning to have him killed on the way.

Festus hears the case at Caesarea instead, but when he asks whether Paul is willing to be tried in Jerusalem, Paul invokes his right as a Roman citizen to have his case heard by the emperor himself. At this time the emperor is Nero, still in the early years of his reign.

Before Paul can be sent, there are visitors. King Agrippa, Herod Agrippa the Second, is the son of the Herod who died in chapter twelve and the last of the Herodian kings. He ruled territories to the north and east of Galilee, and Rome had given him oversight of the temple, including the power to appoint the high priest. With him is his sister Bernice. Festus, who must send a report to Rome and has little idea what to write, is glad to have Agrippa hear Paul for himself.
