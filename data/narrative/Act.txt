=== intro
The book of Acts is the second volume of Luke's work, and it begins where his gospel ends. Once again it is addressed to Theophilus. Once again the setting is Jerusalem, with a small group of Jesus' followers waiting, as he told them to, for power from on high. From that room the story runs outward for some thirty years, from the early thirties of the first century to the early sixties, and from the temple courts of Jerusalem to a rented house in Rome.

The traditional title, the Acts of the Apostles, was added later, and it promises more than the book sets out to give. Most of the Twelve are hardly mentioned after the opening chapters. Luke follows two men above all, Peter in the first half of the book and Paul in the second, and behind both of them the Holy Spirit, who drives the story on at every turn. Luke himself appears at intervals. In several passages, beginning in chapter sixteen, the narrative changes from they to we, and the writer is evidently travelling with Paul. Those passages include the long voyage to Rome at the end of the book.

Jesus' last words to his disciples, near the start of chapter one, give the book its map. You will be my witnesses in Jerusalem, and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth. The first seven chapters stay in Jerusalem. The coming of the Spirit at Pentecost, the first preaching, the life of the first community and the growing opposition of the temple authorities lead to the death of Stephen, the first to die for his faith in Jesus. In chapters eight to twelve the believers are scattered by persecution, and the message moves out into Judea and Samaria and beyond. Philip preaches to Samaritans and to an Ethiopian official. Saul of Tarsus, a fierce persecutor, is stopped on the road to Damascus. Peter is sent to the house of a Roman centurion in Caesarea, and Gentiles receive the Spirit. In Antioch, the great city of Syria, a mixed congregation of Jews and Gentiles grows up, and there the disciples are first called Christians.

From chapter thirteen the story belongs to Saul, now known by his Roman name, Paul. The church at Antioch sends him out with Barnabas, and Luke follows him on three great journeys. The first takes them to Cyprus and to the towns of southern Asia Minor, in what is now Turkey. Then, in chapter fifteen, a council in Jerusalem settles whether Gentile believers must be circumcised and keep the law of Moses. On the second journey Paul and Silas cross the sea from Asia Minor into Macedonia, in northern Greece, and plant churches at Philippi, Thessalonica and Berea before Paul moves on to Athens and spends eighteen months at Corinth. The third journey centres on Ephesus, where Paul stays more than two years.

The world of these journeys is the Roman Empire at the height of its peace. Roman roads and shipping lanes tie it together, and Greek is spoken in every port. In almost every city there is a Jewish community with its synagogue, and often Gentiles who worship Israel's God without becoming Jews. Paul usually begins there. Luke names Roman officials with care, proconsuls, city magistrates and governors, and his titles have often been confirmed by inscriptions. One of them, naming the proconsul Gallio, allows Paul's time in Corinth to be dated to around the year fifty or fifty-one, a fixed point for the whole chronology.

Acts never mentions that Paul wrote letters, but many of them belong inside its story, and following Acts makes them easier to place. The letters to the Thessalonians were written from Corinth, not long after Paul had been driven out of Thessalonica. The first letter to the Corinthians was written from Ephesus, the second from Macedonia, and Romans from Corinth, as Paul prepared to carry a gift of money to Jerusalem. Galatians may belong close to the council of chapter fifteen, though its date is debated. Ephesians, Philippians, Colossians and Philemon were written in prison, traditionally in Rome, though some think of Caesarea or Ephesus.

The last quarter of the book slows down. Paul goes up to Jerusalem knowing that trouble awaits him, and he is seized in the temple courts and rescued from the mob by Roman soldiers. Two years of custody follow at Caesarea, the Roman capital of Judea, where he defends himself before the governors Felix and Festus and before King Agrippa. Appealing to Caesar, he is sent to Rome by sea, and Luke's account of the voyage and the shipwreck off Malta is one of the most vivid descriptions of ancient seafaring to survive.

Acts ends with Paul in Rome under house arrest for two years, receiving visitors and preaching openly. Luke does not say how his trial ended. Early Christian tradition holds that both Peter and Paul died in Rome in the sixties, in the reign of Nero, but that lies beyond the book.

In the New Testament, Acts stands between the gospels and the letters, and it joins them. It tells how a movement that began among the Jewish followers of a crucified teacher from Galilee came, within a single generation, to be found in the great cities of the empire, among Jews and Gentiles alike.

=== 1
Luke begins his second volume as he began the first, with a word to Theophilus. He reminds him that the gospel told what Jesus began to do and to teach, and he fills in what happened after the resurrection. For forty days Jesus appeared to the apostles and spoke with them about the kingdom of God. He told them not to leave Jerusalem, but to wait for the Holy Spirit the Father had promised.

The apostles still have one question on their minds: will he now restore the kingdom to Israel? Jesus' answer turns them outward, and it sketches the course of the whole book, from Jerusalem to Judea and Samaria and to the ends of the earth. Then, on the Mount of Olives, he is taken up from their sight.

The rest of the chapter takes place in the days of waiting. The eleven apostles are named, and with them in the upper room are the women, Mary the mother of Jesus, and his brothers, who during his ministry had not believed in him. About a hundred and twenty believers are gathered when Peter stands up to speak about the place left empty by Judas.

=== 2
Ten days after Jesus was taken up, the believers are together in Jerusalem on the day of Pentecost. This was the Feast of Weeks, the harvest festival that fell fifty days after Passover, and one of the occasions when Jews came on pilgrimage to the city from far and wide.

That matters for what happens next. When the Spirit comes, with a sound like a rushing wind and what look like tongues of fire, the believers begin to speak in other languages, and the crowd that gathers includes Jews from all over the known world. Luke's list sweeps from Parthia, beyond the eastern edge of the Roman Empire, through Mesopotamia and Asia Minor, to Egypt, Libya, Crete, Arabia and Rome itself. Each one hears the wonders of God in his own language.

Then Peter stands up with the other apostles and gives the first sermon of the church. A few weeks earlier he had denied knowing Jesus in a high priest's courtyard. Now he speaks to the crowds of Jerusalem about the man they crucified, drawing on the prophet Joel and on the Psalms of David. The chapter ends with a portrait of the community that forms in the days that follow.

=== 3
The believers are still meeting daily in the temple courts, as the end of chapter two described. Now Luke tells one story from those days. Peter and John are going up to the temple for the afternoon prayer, at the ninth hour, about three o'clock. At the gate called Beautiful, probably one of the gates leading into the inner courts, sits a man who has been lame from birth, carried there every day to beg from the worshippers.

Peter and John appear together often in these early chapters. They had been partners in the fishing business on the Sea of Galilee, and they were among the three closest to Jesus. Now they are leaders in Jerusalem.

What happens at the gate draws a large crowd to Solomon's Colonnade, a long covered walkway along the eastern side of the temple's outer court. Peter seizes the moment to speak again. This second sermon is aimed squarely at the people of Jerusalem, who stood before Pilate and asked for a murderer to be released in Jesus' place. Peter calls them to repentance, and he grounds his appeal in Moses, in the prophets from Samuel onwards, and in the promise made to Abraham.

=== 4
Peter is still speaking in Solomon's Colonnade when the temple authorities arrive: priests, the captain of the temple guard, and members of the Sadducees, the party of the high priestly families. They are disturbed above all that the apostles are proclaiming resurrection from the dead through Jesus. Peter and John are held overnight, and yet the number of believing men grows to about five thousand.

The next morning they stand before the council, the Sanhedrin, and Luke names the men facing them: Annas, Caiaphas and others of the high priest's family. These are the same leaders who handed Jesus over to Pilate only weeks before. The council members notice that Peter and John are ordinary men without formal training in the law, and they recognise them as men who had been with Jesus.

After their release, the two return to the believers, who pray together in the words of the second Psalm about rulers raging against the Lord's anointed. The chapter closes with a picture of a community sharing its possessions, and with a man who will be important later: Joseph, a Levite from the island of Cyprus, whom the apostles call Barnabas, meaning son of encouragement.

=== 5
Barnabas has just sold a field and laid the money at the apostles' feet. The chapter opens with a couple who do the same, or appear to. Ananias and his wife Sapphira sell a property and keep back part of the money while pretending to give it all. What follows is a sobering moment in the life of the young community, and Luke tells it without softening.

Then the story returns to the wider city. The apostles' healing work draws the sick from the towns around Jerusalem, and the high priest and his circle, all Sadducees, are filled with jealousy. They arrest the apostles and put them in the public jail, but the next morning the prisoners are found teaching in the temple courts again.

Brought before the Sanhedrin once more, the apostles refuse to be silent. The council is ready to have them killed when a respected Pharisee named Gamaliel, one of the most honoured teachers of the law in his day, rises to counsel caution. He reminds them of other movements that rose and collapsed when their leaders died. We will later learn that Saul of Tarsus had studied under this same Gamaliel. The apostles are flogged and released, and they go out rejoicing.
