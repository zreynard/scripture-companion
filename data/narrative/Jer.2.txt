=== 26
The book now turns from collected messages to stories about Jeremiah himself, and it steps back in time to do so. This scene is set at the beginning of Jehoiakim's reign, just after Josiah's death, and it tells what happened when Jeremiah preached the Temple Sermon of chapter seven: this house will become like Shiloh.

Priests, prophets and people seize him and shout that he must die. The royal officials come up from the palace and sit in judgement at the New Gate of the temple, and the scene becomes a trial. Jeremiah retracts nothing, and warns his judges that if they kill him they will bring innocent blood on the city.

Some of the elders recall a precedent. A century earlier, Micah of Moresheth had said that Zion would be ploughed like a field, and King Hezekiah did not kill him, but sought the Lord. Then, to show how close Jeremiah came, the chapter tells of another prophet, Uriah, who spoke the same message and did not survive. Jeremiah is protected by Ahikam son of Shaphan. Shaphan was the royal scribe who had read the newly found book of the law to King Josiah, and his family will appear again.

=== 27
Some years have passed. Jehoiakim is dead, his son Jeconiah is in Babylon, and Zedekiah reigns as Nebuchadnezzar's appointee. Envoys from the kings of Edom, Moab, Ammon, Tyre and Sidon have come to Jerusalem. The next chapter places this around Zedekiah's fourth year, when these small kingdoms were exploring a joint rebellion against Babylon.

Into that meeting walks Jeremiah wearing a yoke, the bars and straps laid across the neck of an ox. He sends a message back with the envoys. The Lord made the earth and gives it to whom he pleases, and he has given these lands to his servant Nebuchadnezzar. Any nation that bows its neck to Babylon will remain in its land. Any nation that resists will be destroyed.

He says the same to Zedekiah, and then to the priests and people, who are hearing from other prophets that the temple treasures taken to Babylon will soon come back. Jeremiah answers that even the great bronze pillars and the bronze Sea still standing in the temple court will follow them.

=== 28
That same year, in the temple, Jeremiah is publicly contradicted. The challenger is Hananiah son of Azzur, a prophet from Gibeon, and he speaks just as Jeremiah does, in the name of the Lord of Hosts. The yoke of Babylon is broken, he says. Within two years the temple treasures will be returned, and King Jeconiah and the exiles will come home.

Jeremiah's first reply is remarkable. Amen, he says. May the Lord do so. He would like nothing better. But he reminds Hananiah that the prophets of old spoke of war and disaster, and that a prophet who promises peace will be known as truly sent only when his words come true.

Then Hananiah takes the yoke from Jeremiah's neck and breaks it in front of everyone, and Jeremiah simply walks away. Only later does the word of the Lord come to him, and he returns with a message for Hananiah. The chapter's ending is told in a single plain sentence.

=== 29
While false prophets contend with Jeremiah in Jerusalem, the same struggle is going on among the exiles in Babylon. Jeremiah sends them a letter, carried by two envoys whom Zedekiah is sending to Nebuchadnezzar, one of them a son of Shaphan.

The exiles are being told by their own prophets that their stay will be short. Jeremiah's letter says the opposite. Build houses and live in them. Plant gardens. Marry, and see your children married. Seek the welfare of the city where you have been sent and pray to the Lord for it, for in its welfare you will find your own. Only when Babylon's seventy years are complete will the Lord bring them home. Here is one of the most quoted promises in the book, of plans for a future and a hope, spoken to people most of whom would not live to see the return.

The letter also names false prophets among the exiles, and the chapter ends with an angry reply from one of them, Shemaiah, who writes back to Jerusalem demanding that the priests put Jeremiah in the stocks.

=== 30
After so many chapters of warning, the tone changes almost completely. Jeremiah is told to write in a book all the words the Lord has spoken to him, because the days are coming when the Lord will restore his people, Israel and Judah together, to the land given to their fathers. These four chapters are often called the Book of Consolation.

This chapter and the next are poetry, and undated. The sorrow is not forgotten. The poem opens with a cry of terror, a time of distress for Jacob unlike any other. Judah's wound is incurable, her allies gone, her punishment deserved. Then the turn comes, from the Lord alone. I will restore your health and heal your wounds, he says, because they have called you an outcast.

The promises are concrete: the city rebuilt on its ruins, the palace in its proper place, songs of thanksgiving, children as in former days, and a ruler who comes from among the people themselves. Running through it is the covenant promise first spoken at Sinai: you will be my people, and I will be your God.

=== 31
The promises now reach the northern tribes, Ephraim and Samaria, lost to Assyria long before Jeremiah was born. I have loved you with an everlasting love, the Lord says. There will be vineyards on the hills of Samaria again, and watchmen on the hills of Ephraim calling the people up to Zion. The blind and the lame, mothers with child, will come back from the north in a great company.

One brief passage is full of sorrow. A voice is heard in Ramah, a town of Benjamin: Rachel, the mother of Joseph and Benjamin, weeping for her children because they are no more. The Lord tells her to dry her tears, for they will return. Matthew's Gospel would recall these words at the slaughter of the children of Bethlehem.

Then comes the passage for which this chapter is best known. The Lord promises a new covenant, unlike the one made at the exodus, which Israel broke. This time the law will be written on their hearts, all will know him, and he will remember their sins no more. Jesus spoke of the new covenant in his blood at the Last Supper, and the letter to the Hebrews quotes this passage at length.

=== 32
Now comes a dated narrative, set at the darkest point of all: the tenth year of Zedekiah, the eighteenth of Nebuchadnezzar. The Babylonian army is besieging Jerusalem, and Jeremiah is confined in the courtyard of the guard at the palace, imprisoned by the king for saying that the city will fall.

His cousin Hanamel arrives from Anathoth and asks him to buy the family field there. Under the law, when land had to be sold, the nearest kinsman had the right to buy it and keep it in the family. This land lies behind enemy lines, and its value must have seemed close to nothing.

Here we meet by name, for the first time, Baruch son of Neriah, Jeremiah's scribe. The purchase is done with legal care: silver weighed, the deed signed, sealed and witnessed, and two copies stored in a clay jar to last a long time. Then Jeremiah prays, and his prayer is honest about how strange it all seems. The Lord's answer is that nothing is too hard for him. The city will fall, and yet fields will be bought in this land again.

=== 33
Jeremiah is still shut up in the courtyard of the guard when the word of the Lord comes to him a second time. Call to me, the Lord says, and I will answer you and show you great and hidden things.

The present is not softened. Houses in Jerusalem, royal buildings among them, are being pulled down to shore up the walls against the siege ramps, and the gaps will be filled with the dead. Yet the Lord promises healing for this very city. In the empty streets, the sounds silenced in so many earlier chapters will be heard again: the voices of bride and bridegroom, and worshippers bringing thank offerings and singing that the Lord is good, his loving devotion endures forever. Shepherds will count their flocks again in the towns of Judah.

The righteous Branch of chapter twenty-three returns, and this time Jerusalem itself bears the name The Lord Our Righteousness. The last verses bind the covenant with David and with the Levitical priests to the fixed order of day and night. People are saying the Lord has rejected both Israel and Judah. He would sooner break his covenant with the sun and moon.

=== 34
We remain in the final siege. Nebuchadnezzar's army, swelled with troops from the kingdoms under his control, is fighting against Jerusalem, and only two other fortified towns of Judah still hold out, Lachish and Azekah, in the lowlands to the south-west. Archaeologists at Lachish found letters written on broken pottery by Judean officers from about this time, and one reports that the signals from Azekah can no longer be seen.

Jeremiah brings Zedekiah a personal word. The city will burn, and the king will meet the king of Babylon face to face. Yet he will not die by the sword.

The rest of the chapter records a broken promise. Zedekiah and the people had made a solemn covenant in the temple to free their Hebrew slaves, as the law of Moses required after six years of service. They did it, and then took the slaves back when the Babylonian army withdrew for a time and the danger seemed past. The covenant had been sealed by walking between the halves of a slaughtered calf. The fate of the calf, the Lord says, will now be theirs.

=== 35
This chapter steps back to the days of Jehoiakim, near the end of his reign, when Babylonian forces and Aramean raiders were already in the land. Among those who have taken refuge inside Jerusalem is an unusual clan, the Rechabites.

They trace their customs to Jonadab son of Rechab, who appears in the second book of Kings as an ally of King Jehu, some two and a half centuries earlier. He commanded his descendants never to drink wine, build houses, sow fields or plant vineyards, but to live in tents. They have kept those rules ever since, and only the invasion has driven them into the city.

Jeremiah brings the family into a room in the temple, sets bowls of wine and cups in front of them, and invites them to drink. They refuse, politely and firmly. The test is not a trap. It is a lesson staged for Jerusalem. A family has kept the command of a human ancestor for generations, while Judah has not listened to the Lord, who has spoken again and again. The family receives a promise of its own.

=== 36
We go back to the fourth year of Jehoiakim, the year of Carchemish. Jeremiah is told to write on a scroll everything the Lord has said to him since the days of Josiah. Perhaps, hearing it all together, the people will turn.

Jeremiah dictates and Baruch writes. Jeremiah is barred from the temple, so Baruch reads the scroll aloud there on a fast day the following year, from the room of Gemariah son of Shaphan. Gemariah's son reports it to the royal officials, and they summon Baruch to read it to them too. Among them is Elnathan son of Achbor, the man who once fetched the prophet Uriah back from Egypt to his death.

The officials are afraid. They tell Baruch and Jeremiah to hide, and take the scroll to the king. It is winter, and Jehoiakim is sitting in his winter apartment with a fire burning in a brazier in front of him. What he does next is one of the most vivid scenes in the book, and some of his own officials plead with him to stop. Then Jeremiah dictates a second scroll, with more added, and it is likely that the book we have grew from it.

=== 37
The story jumps forward again, to Zedekiah's reign and the siege. The chapter opens with a reminder of who Zedekiah is: placed on the throne by Nebuchadnezzar, and like those before him, not listening to the word of the Lord. Yet he keeps sending to Jeremiah.

This scene comes earlier in the siege than the courtyard chapters we have just heard, and Jeremiah is still free to come and go. An Egyptian army has marched out to help Judah, and the Babylonians have lifted the siege to meet it. Hope revives in Jerusalem. Jeremiah says it will not last. Even if the Babylonians were reduced to wounded men lying in their tents, they would rise up and burn the city.

During the lull, Jeremiah sets out for the land of Benjamin to see to family property, and at the city gate he is arrested as a deserter. The officials beat him and shut him in a dungeon cell in the house of Jonathan the scribe for many days. Then the king sends for him in secret and asks a question he will ask more than once: is there any word from the Lord?

=== 38
In the courtyard of the guard, Jeremiah is still speaking to those around him, and his message has not changed. Whoever stays in the city will die. Whoever goes out to the Chaldeans will live. To a group of royal officials, among them Pashhur son of Malchijah, this is treason, weakening the hands of the soldiers, and they demand his death.

Zedekiah's reply reveals the kind of king he is. He is in your hands, he says, for the king can do nothing against you. So Jeremiah is lowered by ropes into a cistern, empty of water in the long siege, and sinks into the mud at the bottom.

Help comes from an unexpected quarter. Ebed-melech, a Cushite from the kingdom on the upper Nile beyond Egypt, is an official in the palace, and he goes to the king to plead for the prophet. The rescue is told with tender detail. Then Zedekiah meets Jeremiah secretly at an entrance to the temple. It is their last recorded conversation, and it shows a king afraid of almost everyone: the Babylonians, his own officials, and the Judeans who have already deserted.

=== 39
The siege comes to its end, told briefly and with dates. Nebuchadnezzar's army arrived in the ninth year of Zedekiah, in the tenth month. In the eleventh year, on the ninth day of the fourth month, a year and a half later, the wall was broken through. The second book of Kings records the same events, and chapter fifty-two will tell them again.

Babylonian officers take their seats in the Middle Gate, a sign that the city now belongs to them. Zedekiah and his soldiers slip out by night through the gate between the two walls by the king's garden and run east toward the Jordan valley. They are caught on the plains of Jericho and brought north to Nebuchadnezzar's headquarters at Riblah, in Syria. What happens to Zedekiah there is what Jeremiah had warned him of.

The palace and houses are burned and the walls pulled down. Nebuzaradan, the captain of the guard, takes the people into exile and leaves only the poorest to work the land. By Nebuchadnezzar's own order, Jeremiah is treated with care and entrusted to Gedaliah, grandson of Shaphan. Last of all comes an earlier promise to Ebed-melech, the man who pulled him out of the mud.

=== 40
Jerusalem has fallen, and the book follows the remnant left in Judah. First it fills in a detail. In the confusion after the city's capture, Jeremiah was caught up among the captives and taken in chains to Ramah, the assembly point north of Jerusalem for those being sent to Babylon. There Nebuzaradan sets him free, and even speaks of the disaster as the judgement of Jeremiah's God. He offers a choice: come to Babylon under his protection, or stay. Jeremiah stays.

Nebuchadnezzar has made Gedaliah son of Ahikam governor of what is left of Judah, with his seat at Mizpah, a few miles north of the ruined city. Army officers who had scattered into the countryside come to him, and Judeans who had fled to Moab, Ammon and Edom begin to return. Gedaliah's advice is Jeremiah's: serve the king of Babylon and it will go well with you. That summer brings a rich harvest of grapes and fruit.

But one of the officers, Johanan son of Kareah, warns Gedaliah that the king of the Ammonites has sent another, Ishmael son of Nethaniah, to kill him. Gedaliah refuses to believe it.

=== 41
The warning proves true. In the seventh month, Ishmael son of Nethaniah, a man of the royal family, comes to Mizpah with ten men, and they share a meal with the governor. Behind him stands the king of Ammon, and Gedaliah, who governs Judah under Babylon, is not of David's line.

What follows is told without comment, and its violence spreads beyond Gedaliah: to the Judeans and Babylonian soldiers with him, and then to a group of pilgrims from Shechem, Shiloh and Samaria, coming in mourning, with offerings for the house of the Lord, unaware of what has happened. The cistern they end in was dug centuries earlier by King Asa.

Johanan and the other officers pursue Ishmael and overtake him at the great pool of Gibeon. The captives are freed, though Ishmael escapes to Ammon. Now the survivors are afraid. The man Babylon appointed has been murdered, and they expect reprisals. They stop near Bethlehem, already meaning to go down to Egypt. Jews have long kept a fast in the seventh month in memory of Gedaliah.

=== 42
The survivors are camped near Bethlehem, on the road south. Before going further, the officers, with Johanan son of Kareah at their head, come to Jeremiah with every appearance of humility. Pray to the Lord your God for us, they say, that he will show us the way to go. Whatever the answer, pleasant or unpleasant, we will obey.

Jeremiah agrees, and then he waits. For ten days there is no answer. It is a small detail, but it says something about how the word came to him. He did not produce it on demand.

When the answer comes, it is clear. If they stay in the land, the Lord will build them up and not tear them down, plant them and not uproot them, the language of Jeremiah's first call. They need not fear the king of Babylon. But if they go to Egypt, looking for a land with no war, no trumpet and no hunger, the sword and famine they are fleeing will follow them there. Before he finishes, Jeremiah senses that their minds are already made up.

=== 43
The answer comes at once. Johanan, Azariah and the men with them call Jeremiah a liar. The Lord has not sent you, they say, and they blame Baruch for turning him against them so that they will be handed to the Babylonians.

So the whole remnant sets off for Egypt: soldiers, women and children, the king's daughters, those who had come back from neighbouring lands, and Jeremiah and Baruch with them, against their will. They come to Tahpanhes, a frontier town on the eastern edge of the Nile Delta, where Pharaoh had a residence.

Jeremiah's ministry continues on foreign soil. As his fellow Judeans watch, he buries large stones in the brick pavement at the entrance to the royal building. Nebuchadnezzar, he says, will one day set his throne on these very stones. Egypt, where they hoped to escape Babylon, will feel its power too, and the obelisks of the temple of the sun, at the city the Greeks called Heliopolis, will be broken. A Babylonian inscription records that Nebuchadnezzar did campaign against Egypt years later.

=== 44
Some time has passed. Judeans are now living in several parts of Egypt: at Migdol and Tahpanhes in the Delta, at Memphis, the ancient capital near modern Cairo, and in Pathros, the region of Upper Egypt to the south. Jeremiah addresses them all. It is the last of his messages that the book records.

He reminds them of what they have seen with their own eyes, the ruins of Jerusalem and the towns of Judah, and why it happened. Now in Egypt they are doing the same things again.

What makes the chapter unforgettable is the reply. A great crowd, men and women together, tell Jeremiah plainly that they will go on making offerings to the Queen of Heaven, as their fathers and kings did. Back then, they say, there was plenty of food and no disaster. It was when they stopped, in the days of Josiah's reforms, that everything went wrong. It is a rival reading of the same history. Jeremiah answers with a sign: Pharaoh Hophra will be handed over to his enemies, as Zedekiah was. Egyptian and Greek sources tell us that Hophra did lose his throne and his life in an uprising some years later.

=== 45
This short chapter is a postscript, and it takes us back some twenty years, to the fourth year of Jehoiakim, when Baruch first wrote Jeremiah's words on a scroll. It belongs beside chapter thirty-six, but it has been placed here, where by now Baruch's own life has played out against everything that scroll foretold.

Baruch, like Jeremiah, has been complaining to the Lord. Woe is me, he says, the Lord has added sorrow to my pain. I am worn out with groaning and find no rest. Writing those words of judgement had cost him something. He came from a family of some standing, and he had hoped for a different future.

The Lord's answer is gentle and unflinching at once. He is tearing down what he built and uprooting what he planted, across the whole land. Is this a time to seek great things for yourself? Do not seek them. But Baruch receives a promise of his own, the one given to Ebed-melech: wherever he goes, his life will be spared.

=== 46
A new heading opens the last major section of the book, the word of the Lord concerning the nations. At his call Jeremiah was appointed a prophet to the nations, and these chapters show what that meant. They run from Egypt in the west to Babylon in the east.

The first oracle concerns Egypt and a single battle. In the fourth year of Jehoiakim, Pharaoh Neco's army was defeated by Nebuchadnezzar at Carchemish, on the upper Euphrates. This was the same Neco who had killed Josiah at Megiddo four years earlier. The poem reads almost like a war report: orders shouted, horses harnessed, armour polished, and then the sudden rout, warriors stumbling over one another by the river in the north. Egypt rising like the Nile in flood is a proud image, and the poem turns it to scorn.

A second oracle looks ahead to Nebuchadnezzar coming against Egypt itself, against Memphis and the god Amon of Thebes, though a later restoration for Egypt is promised too. Then, in the middle of the oracles against the nations, come words of comfort for Jacob, repeated from the Book of Consolation: do not be afraid, for I am with you.

=== 47
From Egypt the oracles move up the coast to the Philistines, Israel's old rivals on the southern coastal plain. The heading dates it before Pharaoh struck down Gaza. The Philistines are called the remnant from Caphtor, the island of Crete, from which their ancestors were remembered to have come.

The poem is short and intense. The danger is pictured as waters rising out of the north and flooding the land, and then as the noise of an army: hammering hooves, rumbling chariots, rattling wheels. Fathers are so terrified that they do not turn back for their children. Gaza shaves its head in mourning and Ashkelon falls silent.

The poem ends with a cry to the sword of the Lord to go back into its sheath and rest, and the answer: how can it rest, when the Lord has given it its orders? Babylonian records tell us that Nebuchadnezzar sacked Ashkelon about a year after his victory at Carchemish.

=== 48
The next oracle, one of the longest in the book, concerns Moab, Judah's neighbour across the Dead Sea. The Moabites lived on the high tableland to the east, and Genesis traced their ancestry to Lot. Relations between the two peoples had been tangled for centuries, and Ruth, the great-grandmother of David, was a Moabite.

The poem moves from town to town across Moab: Nebo, Heshbon, Horonaim, Dibon, Aroer by the Arnon gorge, and many more. Several of these places appear on a stone inscription set up by a Moabite king named Mesha, some two and a half centuries earlier, and found at Dibon in the nineteenth century. Moab's god Chemosh, named on that stone too, will go into exile with his priests.

One image is especially memorable. Moab has been left undisturbed like wine settled on its dregs, never poured from jar to jar, so it has kept its old taste. Now it will be poured out. Some passages closely resemble Isaiah's oracle on Moab, and the grief in them is striking: the Lord's heart laments for Moab like a flute. Its final line, unexpectedly, is a promise that Moab's fortunes will one day be restored.

=== 49
This chapter gathers five shorter oracles, moving around the map of Judah's neighbours.

First comes Ammon, north of Moab, with its capital at Rabbah, the site of modern Amman. Its god Milcom has taken over land belonging to the tribe of Gad. Then Edom, south of the Dead Sea, the people descended from Esau, proud of the wisdom of Teman and of its strongholds in the rocks, high as an eagle's nest. Several lines here are shared with the book of Obadiah, which also concerns Edom.

The third oracle turns north to Damascus, the old Aramean capital, with Hamath and Arpad, cities further north in Syria. The fourth concerns Kedar and the kingdoms of Hazor, desert tribes of Arabia living in tents among their camels, and here the attacker is named as Nebuchadnezzar. This Hazor is not the old city in Galilee but somewhere in the Arabian desert.

The last oracle is dated to the beginning of Zedekiah's reign, and it reaches furthest of all, to Elam, east of Babylon in what is now south-western Iran, famous for its archers. Like Moab's, several of these oracles end with a promise of restoration.

=== 50
The oracles end where all of Jeremiah's prophecy has been heading: with Babylon. These two chapters are by far the longest of the oracles, and they turn the message of the book around. Babylon, the Lord's instrument of judgement, will now be judged.

The opening announces it as if it had already happened. Babylon is captured. Bel is put to shame and Marduk is shattered, two names for the great god of Babylon. A nation from the north will come against her. The language of the early chapters, where the enemy from the north threatened Jerusalem, is now aimed at Babylon, and lines from chapter six that described the army coming against the Daughter of Zion return almost word for word against the Daughter of Babylon.

Woven through the poem is the return of God's people. Israel and Judah will come together, weeping, asking the way to Zion. The Lord calls them a scattered flock: Assyria devoured them first, then Nebuchadnezzar gnawed their bones. Babylon, the hammer of the whole earth, is broken, and the exiles are told to flee, like the goats that lead the flock out of the fold.

=== 51
The oracle against Babylon continues, and now the attackers are named: the kings of the Medes, from the highlands to the east, with peoples from the northern mountains, Ararat, Minni and Ashkenaz. Babylon is a gold cup in the Lord's hand that made the whole earth drunk. The book of Revelation would later draw on several of these images when it described the fall of another Babylon.

Babylon did fall, to Cyrus of Persia and the Medes, in five hundred and thirty-nine before Christ, about seventy years after its rise at Carchemish. In fact it was taken with little fighting, and the great city faded over centuries rather than vanishing in a day. The poetry speaks in the language of total judgement, as the prophets often did.

A dated story follows, from the fourth year of Zedekiah, the year of the yoke and of Hananiah. Zedekiah travels to Babylon, and with him goes his quartermaster Seraiah son of Neriah, Baruch's brother. Jeremiah gives him a scroll of these words against Babylon, with instructions for what to do once it has been read. Then a short line marks the end of Jeremiah's own words.

=== 52
Jeremiah's words have ended, but the book has one more chapter. It is a historical appendix, and it matches almost word for word the closing chapters of the second book of Kings. It is placed here so that the reader finishes with the plain record of how the prophecies came true.

It opens with Zedekiah, twenty-one years old when he became king, reigning eleven years and doing evil in the sight of the Lord. Then the siege, the breach, the flight and the judgement at Riblah, told more fully than in chapter thirty-nine. A month after the wall fell, Nebuzaradan burns the house of the Lord. The bronze pillars and the great bronze Sea that Solomon made are broken up and carried to Babylon, as Jeremiah had told the prophets who promised their return. Three deportations are listed by number, smaller figures than in Kings, which may count only certain groups.

The last scene is in Babylon, about twenty-five years after the fall. Nebuchadnezzar has died, and his son Evil-merodach releases King Jehoiachin from prison and gives him a place at the royal table. Clay tablets found in Babylon list rations for Jehoiachin, king of Judah, and his sons. The book ends there, with David's heir alive in exile, and the seventy years not yet complete.
