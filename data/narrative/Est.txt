=== intro
The book of Esther takes us back to Susa, the royal city of Persia where Nehemiah served as cupbearer, but to an earlier generation. Its events fall in the reign of King Xerxes, in the early decades of the fifth century before Christ, after the temple in Jerusalem had been rebuilt and before Ezra made his journey there. In the book of Ezra, they belong in the long silence between chapters six and seven.

This is a story about the Jews who did not go home. When Cyrus allowed the exiles to return to Judah, many stayed where they were. Their families had lived in the east for two or three generations by then, and they had built houses, run businesses and found their place in a foreign land, much as the prophet Jeremiah had told the first exiles to do. By the time of Esther they were scattered across the provinces of the Persian Empire, a people with their own customs and their own God, living as a minority under a foreign king. Their safety depended on the goodwill of that king and the men around him.

The king is Xerxes, the son of Darius, who ruled Persia for about twenty years from four hundred and eighty-six before Christ. Older English Bibles call him Ahasuerus, a form of his name in Hebrew. Greek historians remember him as the king who led a vast army into Greece and was turned back, at Salamis and elsewhere, and the gap of several years between the opening chapters of Esther falls across those campaigns, though the book itself says nothing about them. His empire stretched, as the book puts it, from India to Cush, the land south of Egypt, and was divided into a hundred and twenty-seven provinces.

The world of the story is the Persian court in all its splendour. There are banquets that last for days, gold couches and marble pillars, a harem guarded by eunuchs, advisers who must be consulted, and a king whose word, once written and sealed with his ring, cannot be revoked. Couriers carry the king's letters to every province, written in every script and language. The writer knows this world well and describes it with a sharp eye, and sometimes with a touch of irony.

Into that world come four central figures. Xerxes himself is powerful, impulsive and easily steered. Esther is a young Jewish woman, an orphan with the Hebrew name Hadassah, who is taken into the royal harem and becomes queen. Mordecai is her older cousin, who raised her as his own daughter and serves at the king's gate. And Haman is the king's chief minister, proud and vengeful. The writer quietly notes their ancestry. Mordecai is a Benjamite descended from a man named Kish, the name of King Saul's father, and Haman is an Agagite, which recalls Agag, king of the Amalekites, the enemy Saul failed to destroy long before. The book never explains the link, but attentive readers have always heard in it the echo of an ancient feud.

The plot turns on reversals. Feasts mark its stages, from the king's great banquets at the beginning to the Jewish celebrations at the end. A plan to destroy a whole people is set in motion, the date chosen by casting lots. Then, through a series of events that look like coincidences, a sleepless night, a forgotten record, a well-timed invitation, everything that has been planned against the Jews comes back upon the one who planned it.

Esther is famous for one thing it does not do. God is never mentioned by name anywhere in the book. There are fasts, but no prayers are recorded, and no prophet speaks. Yet hardly any reader comes away thinking that God is absent. Mordecai's conviction that help will come from somewhere, and his suggestion that Esther has come to her position for such a time as this, invite the listener to see a hidden hand behind events. Later Greek versions added prayers and mentions of God, and these survive as the Additions to Esther among the books of the Apocrypha; the Hebrew text, which our Bible follows, leaves them out.

The author is unknown, and the date of writing is uncertain. The book explains the origin of the Jewish feast of Purim, which takes its name from the lots Haman cast, and it is still read aloud in synagogues every year at that festival, in late winter. The decree at its centre was sent to every province of the empire, Judah among them, and so the story concerns not only the Jews of Susa but the small community in Jerusalem as well. Some years after Xerxes' death, his son Artaxerxes will send Ezra to Jerusalem, and the story of the return will resume.

=== 1
The book opens in the third year of King Xerxes, in the citadel of Susa, with a display of royal wealth on a breathtaking scale. For six months the king shows off his riches to the military leaders, nobles and governors of his empire. Then comes a seven-day feast in the palace garden for everyone in the citadel, great and small, with linen hangings, marble pillars, gold and silver couches, and wine served without limit.

The detail is lavish, and it is meant to be. This is the world in which the whole story will be played out: a court of enormous splendour, where the king's pleasure is law and his advisers are always close at hand.

Queen Vashti is holding her own banquet for the women. On the seventh day, when the king is merry with wine, he sends seven of his attendants to bring her before his guests, wearing her crown, so that they can admire her beauty. She refuses. The chapter follows what happens when a king's command is publicly ignored, and how his counsellors turn a domestic quarrel into a matter of state.

=== 2
Some time has passed, and the king's anger has cooled. Vashti has been removed, and now there is no queen. The royal attendants propose a solution: a search across the empire for beautiful young women, to be brought to the harem in Susa, so that the king can choose a new queen from among them.

At this point we meet the story's Jewish family. Mordecai son of Jair is a Benjamite living in the citadel of Susa. His family had been carried into exile from Jerusalem with King Jeconiah, more than a century earlier. He has brought up his young cousin Hadassah, also called Esther, as his own daughter, because her parents have died.

Esther is one of the young women taken into the palace. She wins the favour of Hegai, the official in charge of the harem, and on Mordecai's instructions she tells no one that she is Jewish. Each woman is given a full year of beauty treatments before her night with the king. Esther's turn comes in the king's seventh year, four years after Vashti's fall. The chapter ends with an incident at the king's gate that seems minor at the time but will matter a great deal later.

=== 3
Esther is queen, her Jewish identity still a secret, and Mordecai has saved the king's life without reward. Now a new figure rises to power.

King Xerxes promotes Haman son of Hammedatha, the Agagite, above all his other officials, and commands that everyone at the king's gate bow down to him. Mordecai refuses. The text does not say exactly why, but when pressed he tells the other officials that he is a Jew, and the long-remembered feud between Israel and the Amalekites, the people of Agag, stands in the background.

Haman's response is out of all proportion. Rather than punish one man, he resolves to destroy Mordecai's entire people throughout the empire. In the first month of the king's twelfth year, the lot, called pur, is cast before him to choose a day, and it falls nearly a year away, in the twelfth month, Adar.

Then Haman goes to the king. He does not name the people. He describes them as scattered, different and disobedient, and offers a huge sum of silver for the king's treasury. The chapter ends with two pictures side by side: the king and Haman sitting down to drink, and the city of Susa in confusion.

=== 4
The king's edict has gone out by courier to every province: on the thirteenth day of Adar, all the Jews, young and old, women and children, are to be killed and their goods plundered. Mordecai learns of it and goes out into the middle of the city in sackcloth and ashes, wailing aloud, as far as the king's gate. Across the empire, Jewish communities mourn and fast.

Esther, inside the palace, knows nothing of the decree. The chapter is built from a series of messages carried back and forth between her and Mordecai by Hathach, one of the king's attendants. Mordecai sends her a copy of the edict and urges her to go to the king and plead for her people.

Esther's reply explains the danger. Anyone who enters the king's inner court without being summoned is put to death, unless the king holds out his gold sceptre, and she has not been called for a month. Mordecai's answer to that has become one of the best-known passages in the Old Testament. The chapter ends with Esther giving instructions to Mordecai, and with her decision made.

=== 5
Three days of fasting have passed. Esther puts on her royal robes and goes to stand in the inner court of the palace, facing the hall where the king sits on his throne. She has not been summoned. Everything depends on what the king does when he sees her.

From here the story moves like a carefully staged drama. The king's question, what is your request, even up to half the kingdom, is a formula of royal generosity, not a literal offer, and Esther does not rush to answer it. Instead she invites the king and Haman to a banquet she has prepared, and at the banquet she invites them to another one, the following day. Why she waits is not explained, and the delay builds the suspense.

The second half of the chapter follows Haman home. He has been honoured by the queen and is full of joy, until he passes Mordecai at the king's gate, who will neither rise nor tremble before him. At home he boasts to his friends and his wife Zeresh of his wealth and honours, and admits that none of it satisfies him. Their advice pleases him, and that night a gallows goes up beside his house.

=== 6
The gallows stands ready beside Haman's house, and he means to ask the king in the morning for permission to hang Mordecai on it. But that night, in the palace, the king cannot sleep.

This is the turning point of the book, and it hangs on the smallest of details. To pass the hours, the king has the royal chronicles read aloud to him, and the reader comes to the record of the plot Mordecai uncovered back in chapter two. The king asks what reward Mordecai received for saving his life. The answer is nothing.

At that very moment Haman arrives in the outer court to speak to the king about Mordecai. The writer lets the irony unfold through a single question, as the king asks Haman what should be done for the man the king delights to honour, and Haman assumes he is talking about himself.

By the end of the chapter even Haman's wife and advisers can see which way things are going. And before Haman has time to recover, the king's attendants arrive to hurry him to Esther's second banquet.

=== 7
Haman has spent the morning leading Mordecai through the streets in the king's own robe, and he has gone home in grief, only to be hurried away to the queen's banquet. The king and Haman sit down once more to drink wine with Esther.

For the third time the king asks her what she wants, and for the first time she answers. She asks for her own life and the lives of her people, and she chooses her words carefully. She tells the king that she and her people have been sold for destruction, using the language of Haman's own bargain, and that she would have kept silent if they had merely been sold as slaves.

Until this moment the king has not known that his queen is Jewish, and he seems not to have grasped what Haman's edict really meant. When he demands to know who has dared to do such a thing, Esther names the enemy sitting at the table. The rest of the chapter moves swiftly, and it brings the first great reversal of the story to its end, on the gallows Haman built for Mordecai.

=== 8
Haman is dead, and on the same day the king gives his estate to Esther. Mordecai is brought before the king, now that Esther has revealed how they are related, and he receives the royal signet ring that was taken back from Haman.

But the danger to the Jews has not passed. Haman's edict still stands, and under Persian law a decree written in the king's name and sealed with his ring cannot be revoked. Esther goes before the king a second time, falling at his feet in tears, and pleads for the scheme against her people to be undone.

The solution is a second decree. Some two months after Haman's letters went out, the royal scribes are summoned again, and Mordecai writes in the king's name to every province, in every language, and to the Jews in their own. The new edict allows the Jews, on the very day appointed for their destruction, to gather and defend themselves against anyone who attacks them. The couriers ride out on the king's swiftest horses, and the mood in Susa and across the empire is transformed.

=== 9
Nearly nine months have passed since Mordecai's decree went out, and the thirteenth day of Adar has arrived, the day Haman chose by lot for the destruction of the Jews. Two royal edicts now stand side by side, and this is the day both take effect.

The writer describes it in a single phrase: the plan was overturned. Across the provinces the Jews gather to defend themselves against those who hate them, and the officials of the empire, fearing Mordecai's growing power, take their side. In Susa, Haman's ten sons are among the dead, and at Esther's request the Jews there are given a second day. Three times the narrator notes that the Jews did not touch the plunder, though the decree allowed it. Long before, King Saul had been condemned for keeping the spoil of the Amalekites, and the contrast may be deliberate.

The rest of the chapter turns to the festival that came out of it. Mordecai and Esther send letters to all the Jews of the empire, establishing the fourteenth and fifteenth days of Adar as days of feasting, joy and gifts to the poor, to be kept every year. They are called Purim, after the pur, the lot that Haman cast.

=== 10
The book closes with a brief epilogue of only three verses. After the turmoil of the previous chapters, it returns to the language of royal chronicles.

King Xerxes is still on his throne, imposing tribute across his empire to its farthest shores. The narrator notes that the full record of Mordecai's achievements can be found in the official chronicles of the kings of Media and Persia.

The last word belongs to Mordecai. The Jewish exile who once sat in sackcloth at the king's gate is now second only to the king, honoured by his own people, and remembered as one who sought their good and spoke peace to them all.
