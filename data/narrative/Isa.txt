=== intro
Isaiah lived in Jerusalem in the second half of the eighth century before Christ, and for something like forty years he spoke for God in the streets, at the temple, and in the presence of kings. He was the son of a man named Amoz, he was married to a woman the book calls the prophetess, and he gave his sons names that were themselves messages. He seems to have had ready access to the royal court, and he spoke to kings face to face. His book is one of the longest in the Bible, and one of the richest in its poetry.

To understand his world, we need to go back to the books of Kings. Two centuries earlier, after the death of Solomon, the people of Israel had split in two. In the north stood the kingdom of Israel, often called Ephraim after its leading tribe, with its capital at Samaria. In the south stood the smaller kingdom of Judah, ruled from Jerusalem by the descendants of David. Isaiah belongs to Judah, and the four kings named in his opening line, Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah, are the kings of Jerusalem in his lifetime. His story runs alongside the closing chapters of Second Kings, and he was a contemporary of Amos and Hosea in the north and of Micah in Judah.

Uzziah's long reign had brought prosperity, walls and towers, trade, silver and horses. But a shadow was rising in the east. From about seven hundred and forty-five before Christ, the Assyrian empire, centred on the Tigris in what is now northern Iraq, began to expand westward with a ruthless efficiency the region had never seen. Its kings deported whole populations and turned conquered lands into provinces. Every small nation between Mesopotamia and Egypt, Israel and Judah among them, now had to decide whether to resist, to submit, or to look to Egypt for help. That decision, and where a nation places its trust, sits at the heart of Isaiah's preaching.

The great events of his lifetime appear in the book. Under King Ahaz, the kings of Israel and of Damascus marched on Jerusalem to force Judah into an alliance against Assyria, and Isaiah met Ahaz with a sign. Ahaz turned to Assyria instead, and Judah became its vassal. In seven hundred and twenty-two before Christ, Samaria fell, and the northern kingdom was carried away. Under Hezekiah, Judah rebelled, and in seven hundred and one the Assyrian king Sennacherib swept through the land, capturing its fortified cities and surrounding Jerusalem. Isaiah stood with Hezekiah in those days, and the account of the siege forms the hinge of the book.

The book moves in great blocks rather than in strict order of time. The first twelve chapters address Judah and Jerusalem, with Isaiah's call and the crisis under Ahaz at their centre, and with promises of a child and a king from David's line. Then comes a long series of oracles against the nations, Babylon, Assyria, Philistia, Moab, Damascus, Egypt, Tyre and others, followed by visions of judgement and a great feast on a scale that takes in the whole earth. Further warnings follow from the years when Hezekiah's court was seeking an alliance with Egypt. Chapters thirty-six to thirty-nine then tell the story of Sennacherib's siege, Hezekiah's illness and the visit of envoys from Babylon, closing with a prediction that Judah's treasures and some of the king's descendants will one day be carried to Babylon.

At chapter forty the horizon changes. The voice now speaks comfort to a people whose city lies in ruins and who are living in exile in Babylon, a situation that came about more than a century after Isaiah, when Nebuchadnezzar destroyed Jerusalem in five hundred and eighty-six before Christ. The Persian king Cyrus, who would conquer Babylon and let the exiles go home, is named. Here, too, are the songs of the servant of the Lord, who suffers for others and is vindicated. The final chapters turn to a community that has returned but is still waiting, and the book ends with new heavens and a new earth. Many scholars since the eighteenth century have held that these later chapters come from prophets who lived during and after the exile, while others, following long Jewish and Christian tradition, read the whole book as the work of Isaiah himself.

However it came together, the book holds together around one vision of God. Isaiah's favourite title for him is the Holy One of Israel, a name that runs from the first chapter to the last. Its poetry moves between the courtroom and the lament, the taunt song and the lullaby, and its scenes range from Jerusalem's waterworks to the courts of heaven.

Few books mattered more to the first Christians. The New Testament quotes Isaiah more often than any other prophet, and Jesus himself read from it in the synagogue at Nazareth. It also survives in one of the most remarkable manuscripts ever found: among the Dead Sea Scrolls is a complete copy of Isaiah, written in the second century before Christ. We begin, though, in Jerusalem, with the kingdom of Judah still standing, and a prophet summoning heaven and earth to listen.

=== 1
The book opens with its own title page: the vision of Isaiah son of Amoz concerning Judah and Jerusalem, across the reigns of four kings. Then, without further introduction, the prophet calls heaven and earth to listen, as if they were witnesses in a court of law. The Lord has raised children, and they have rebelled. Even an ox knows its owner.

The nation Isaiah describes has already been beaten almost senseless. The land lies desolate, the cities are burned, and Jerusalem is left like a lonely shelter in a field. It is not certain when this oracle was first spoken; many think it reflects the Assyrian devastation of Judah late in Isaiah's life, set here at the front as an overture to the whole book.

What troubles God is not a lack of religion. The temple courts are crowded with sacrifices, festivals and prayers, offered by hands covered with blood, while the widow and the orphan go undefended. Yet the chapter also holds out an invitation to come and reason together, and a promise that the faithful city, now fallen so far, will be purged and called faithful again.

=== 2
A new heading marks a new message, and it begins far in the future. In the last days, the mountain of the Lord's house in Jerusalem will be raised above all others, and the nations will stream to it to be taught. Swords will be beaten into ploughshares. The same oracle appears almost word for word in the book of Micah, Isaiah's contemporary, and no one can say for certain which prophet first spoke it.

Then the vision snaps back to the present, and the contrast is deliberate. The land of Jacob is full of silver and gold, horses and chariots, fortune tellers and idols. This sounds like the prosperous Judah of Uzziah's long reign, confident in its wealth and its defences.

Against all of it Isaiah announces the Day of the Lord, a day when everything lofty will be brought low: the cedars of Lebanon, the oaks of Bashan, every high tower and fortified wall, every great trading ship of Tarshish. A refrain runs through the poem. The Lord alone will be exalted in that day.

=== 3
The poem about the Day of the Lord runs straight on, and it now comes down to earth, in the streets of Jerusalem. The Lord is about to take away everything a society leans on: its bread and water, its soldiers and judges, its prophets and elders, and even its fortune tellers. In their place will come inexperienced boys and chaos, until a man will beg his brother to take charge of a heap of rubble just because he owns a cloak.

At the centre of the chapter stands a scene from a courtroom. The Lord rises to bring a charge against the elders and leaders of his people. They have devoured the vineyard, and the plunder of the poor is in their houses.

Then Isaiah turns to the wealthy women of Jerusalem, the daughters of Zion, walking proudly through the city. What follows is one of the most detailed lists of clothing and jewellery anywhere in the Old Testament, anklets, crescents, perfume bottles, nose rings and festive robes, and then, item by item, its reversal, as the finery of a prosperous city gives way to sackcloth and the gates of Zion sit on the ground in mourning.

=== 4
This short chapter begins where the last one ended, in a city emptied of its men by war. So few are left that seven women take hold of one man and beg simply to bear his name, to be spared the disgrace of being unmarried and childless.

Then, quite suddenly, the darkness lifts. Isaiah looks beyond the judgement to the day when the Branch of the Lord will be beautiful and glorious, and the survivors of Jerusalem will be called holy. The word branch, a new shoot springing up from what has been cut down, will become an important image in the prophets.

The city will first be washed and cleansed by a spirit of judgement and burning. Then the Lord will spread over Mount Zion a cloud by day and fire by night, the signs of his presence that once led Israel through the wilderness. They become a canopy and a shelter from heat and storm.

=== 5
Isaiah now steps forward as a singer. He offers a song about his beloved friend's vineyard on a fertile hill, and his listeners would have recognised the form of a love song. The friend clears the stones, plants the choicest vines, builds a watchtower and cuts a winepress, and then waits for good grapes. The vineyard produces only sour fruit.

The people of Jerusalem are invited to judge between the owner and his vineyard, and only then is the trap sprung: the vineyard is the house of Israel, and the men of Judah are the vines. The punch line rests on a play on words in Hebrew. God looked for justice, mishpat, and found bloodshed, mishpach; for righteousness, tsedaqah, and heard a cry, tseaqah.

A series of woes follows, against those who buy up house after house and field after field, against drinkers and mockers, and against those who call evil good. Near the end, a refrain appears that will return several times in the chapters ahead: his anger is not turned away, his hand is still upraised. The chapter closes with God whistling for a nation from the ends of the earth. It is not named, but its tireless army sounds very like Assyria.

=== 6
Now Isaiah tells how his ministry began. It was the year King Uzziah died, around seven hundred and forty before Christ. Uzziah had reigned for more than fifty years and brought Judah wealth and strength, but Second Chronicles records that in his pride he entered the temple to burn incense, was struck with leprosy, and lived out his days barred from the house of the Lord. In the year of that king's death, Isaiah sees in the temple the true King, high and lifted up.

Seraphim, fiery winged beings, call to one another, holy, holy, holy is the Lord of Hosts. The building shakes and fills with smoke, and Isaiah is overwhelmed by his own uncleanness. A burning coal from the altar touches his lips, and when the Lord asks whom he shall send, Isaiah offers himself.

The commission he receives is a hard one: to speak to a people who will hear without understanding, until the land lies desolate and only a stump remains. Jesus quoted these words to explain why he taught in parables, and John's Gospel says that Isaiah saw his glory.

=== 7
The scene moves forward several years, into the reign of Uzziah's grandson Ahaz, around seven hundred and thirty-four before Christ. Two northern neighbours have formed an alliance against the growing power of Assyria: Rezin, king of Aram, with its capital at Damascus, and Pekah son of Remaliah, king of Israel, here called Ephraim. They want Judah to join them, and when Ahaz refuses, they march on Jerusalem to put a king of their own choosing on David's throne. Jerusalem's king and people shake like trees in the wind.

God sends Isaiah to meet Ahaz by the aqueduct of the upper pool, perhaps where the king was inspecting the city's water supply against a siege. Isaiah brings his son, whose name, Shear-jashub, means a remnant will return. His message is to stay calm. These two kings are only smouldering stubs of firewood.

Ahaz is offered any sign he cares to ask for, and refuses in pious language. Second Kings reveals that he had already decided to send silver and gold to the king of Assyria and ask for his help. Isaiah answers with a sign of God's choosing: a child to be called Immanuel, God with us. Matthew's Gospel would see its fulfilment in the birth of Jesus. For Ahaz, the warning is plain. The power he is courting, the king of Assyria, will come like a hired razor against Judah itself.

=== 8
The crisis with Aram and Israel continues, and Isaiah writes a strange name on a large tablet before trusted witnesses, one of them Uriah the priest, probably the same Uriah who later builds an altar for Ahaz on a design brought back from Damascus. Then Isaiah's wife, the prophetess, gives birth to a son, and he receives the same name: Maher-shalal-hash-baz, which means something like swift to the plunder, quick to the spoil. Before the child can say father or mother, Assyria will carry off the wealth of Damascus and Samaria. Within a few years both had fallen.

But the flood will not stop at their borders. Because Judah has rejected the gentle waters of Shiloah, the stream that fed Jerusalem, it will face the mighty river of the Euphrates, the king of Assyria, rising up to its neck.

In the second half of the chapter Isaiah turns inward. The Lord warns him not to fear what everyone else fears, and to regard the Lord himself as holy. To some he will be a sanctuary, to others a stone to stumble over, words later taken up by Paul and Peter. Isaiah seals his teaching among his disciples and waits, while the people turn to mediums and drift into darkness.

=== 9
The darkness at the end of the last chapter gives way to light, and it falls on a particular place. Zebulun and Naphtali, around the Sea of Galilee, were the northern territories first stripped away by the Assyrian king Tiglath-pileser and turned into provinces in the time of Ahaz. Now Isaiah promises that the very region humbled first will be honoured first. Matthew's Gospel quotes these lines when Jesus begins his ministry in Galilee.

The cause of the joy is a birth. A child is born, a son is given, and he will reign on David's throne with justice and righteousness forever. His titles, Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father and Prince of Peace, go far beyond anything said of an ordinary king.

Then the tone changes completely. The rest of the chapter is a poem against the northern kingdom, Ephraim and Samaria, which has been struck and still boasts that it will rebuild with finer stone. Its stanzas end with the refrain first heard in chapter five: despite all this, his anger is not turned away, his hand is still upraised. The kingdom turns on itself, Manasseh against Ephraim, in the coups and civil strife of its final years.

=== 10
The poem with the refrain reaches its last stanza, aimed at those who write unjust laws to rob widows and orphans. Then Isaiah turns his attention to the empire itself.

Assyria is the rod of God's anger, sent against a godless nation. But the Assyrian king has no idea that he is a tool in anyone's hand. He boasts of the cities he has taken, Carchemish and Calno, Hamath and Arpad, Damascus and Samaria, real cities in Syria and Israel that had fallen to Assyria in quick succession, and he asks why Jerusalem should be any different. Isaiah's reply is withering. Does an axe boast over the one who swings it?

The judgement on Assyria will come in its turn, and a remnant of Jacob will return to the Mighty God, a deliberate echo of the name of Isaiah's elder son. The chapter ends with a vivid sketch of an army advancing on Jerusalem from the north, village by village, through Michmash and Geba, Gibeah and Anathoth, until it halts at Nob, within sight of the city, and shakes its fist at Mount Zion. There the Lord fells it like a forest.

=== 11
At the end of the last chapter the forest of Assyria was cut down. Now Isaiah uses a gentler image from the same woodland. From the stump of Jesse, the father of David, a shoot will spring up. The royal line, reduced to a stump, will grow again.

On this new king the Spirit of the Lord will rest, bringing wisdom, understanding, counsel and might. He will judge the poor with justice and strike the wicked with the word of his mouth. His reign is pictured in one of the best known passages in the prophets: the wolf living with the lamb, a little child leading them, and the earth as full of the knowledge of the Lord as the sea is full of water.

The Root of Jesse will stand as a banner to the nations, words Paul quotes in his letter to the Romans. The Lord will then reach out a second time to gather his scattered people, from Assyria and Egypt, from Cush and Elam and Shinar and the islands of the sea. The old rivalry between Ephraim and Judah will end, and a highway will lead home, as there was when Israel came up out of Egypt.

=== 12
The first great section of the book closes with a song. Isaiah has spoken of judgement on Judah and Israel, of the threat of Assyria, and of a king from David's line. Now he gives the people the words they will sing on the day of restoration.

The song is short and joyful. God's anger has turned away, and he has brought comfort. One line, the Lord is my strength and my song, and he has become my salvation, comes almost word for word from the song Moses and Israel sang beside the sea after the exodus. The promise of a second exodus at the end of the last chapter receives its answering hymn.

The people will draw water with joy from the springs of salvation, and they will make his name known among the nations. The final word goes to the citizens of Zion: great among you is the Holy One of Israel.

=== 13
With this chapter the book enters a long new section. For the next eleven chapters Isaiah turns from Judah to the nations around it, and each message is introduced as a burden, a weighty pronouncement laid on the prophet to deliver. The series is addressed to foreign peoples, but it would have been heard in Jerusalem, by a small kingdom tempted to fear its neighbours or to trust them.

It is striking that the first nation named is Babylon. In Isaiah's day, Babylon was an ancient and proud city, but it lay under Assyrian control and rebelled against it more than once. Only later would it become the great empire that destroyed Jerusalem. Interpreters differ over which fall of Babylon this oracle first had in view, but it names the Medes as the attackers, and the Medes would one day help to bring both Assyria and Babylon down.

The poem begins with the sound of an army gathering on the mountains, mustered by the Lord of Hosts himself. The Day of the Lord is described in the language of a shaken universe, with stars and sun and moon going dark. It ends with Babylon, the jewel of the kingdoms, left to jackals and wild goats.

=== 14
The burden against Babylon continues, and it begins with a promise to Israel. The Lord will have compassion on Jacob and settle his people in their own land again, and on the day they are given rest, they will raise a taunt song over the fallen king of Babylon.

The song is one of the most vivid poems in the Bible. The whole earth breaks into singing, and even the cedars of Lebanon rejoice that no woodcutter comes against them any more. Down in Sheol, the realm of the dead, the departed kings rise from their thrones to greet the newcomer: you too have become as weak as we are. The proud ruler who said he would climb above the stars of God is addressed as day star, son of the dawn. An old Latin translation rendered that title as Lucifer, and later Christian tradition often read the passage as a picture of Satan's fall, but here the taunt is aimed at a human king.

Two shorter oracles follow. The first promises that Assyria will be broken on the Lord's own mountains. The second is dated to the year King Ahaz died, and warns the Philistines not to celebrate too soon because some rod that struck them has been broken. Which ruler that rod represents is uncertain.

=== 15
The next burden is against Moab, Judah's neighbour on the high plateau east of the Dead Sea. The Moabites traced their descent from Lot, Abraham's nephew, and their relations with Israel had swung for centuries between kinship, rivalry and open war. Moab had at times been subject to Israel, and had broken away.

The poem is a lament. Disaster has fallen suddenly, and town after town is named as the cry passes across the land: Ar and Kir, destroyed in a night, then Dibon, Nebo and Medeba, Heshbon and Elealeh, until the refugees are streaming south toward Zoar with their possessions, across the Brook of the Willows. Heads are shaved and beards cut off, the customary signs of mourning.

The enemy who brings this ruin is never named, and the occasion cannot be fixed with certainty; Moab suffered at the hands of Assyria more than once. What stands out is the voice of the prophet himself. This is a foreign people and an old enemy, yet he says that his heart cries out for Moab.

=== 16
The lament for Moab continues, and now the refugees appear at the fords of the Arnon, the river gorge that marked Moab's northern border, like birds pushed out of the nest. In their distress they turn toward Jerusalem. Send tribute lambs to the ruler of the land, the poem begins. Centuries earlier, Moab had paid its tribute to the kings of Israel in lambs and rams.

The Moabite fugitives plead for shelter, and the prophet looks ahead to a throne established in the tent of David, held by a ruler who seeks justice. But Moab's famous pride stands in the way.

Much of the chapter mourns the loss of Moab's vineyards. The vines of Sibmah had once spread as far as Jazer and the desert, and now no one treads the grapes or shouts in the harvest. Again the prophet's own grief breaks through: he weeps with Jazer and his heart sighs like a harp. A short note closes the chapter. This was the word spoken earlier about Moab, and now it is given a timetable: within three years.

=== 17
The burden against Damascus takes us back to the days of chapter seven, when Aram and the northern kingdom of Israel stood together against Assyria. In this oracle they fall together. Damascus will become a heap of ruins, and the glory of Jacob will waste away. Damascus fell to Assyria around seven hundred and thirty-two before Christ, and Samaria about ten years later.

Isaiah uses two pictures from the farming year. Israel will be like a field after the reapers have passed, or like an olive tree beaten for its fruit, with only a few olives left at the top. Those few will turn at last from the altars and Asherah poles they have made, and look to the Holy One of Israel.

The last verses change their subject. Isaiah hears the roaring of many nations, like the noise of crashing seas, but at the rebuke of God they scatter like chaff. In the evening there is terror, and by morning they are gone. Many readers have heard in these lines an anticipation of what would happen to the Assyrian army outside Jerusalem, described later in the book.

=== 18
This chapter is addressed to Cush, the land along the upper Nile, south of Egypt, in what is now Sudan. In Isaiah's day kings from Cush had taken control of Egypt itself, and they were the one power in the region able to stand up to Assyria. The chapter opens with their envoys travelling by water in papyrus boats, very probably seeking allies in Jerusalem.

Isaiah describes the Cushites with evident respect, a tall people with smooth skin, feared far and wide, whose land is divided by rivers. But he sends the messengers home with a different message. God is not anxious, and he needs no alliance. He looks on quietly from his dwelling place, like shimmering heat or a cloud of dew, waiting for the right moment. Then, like a vine dresser before harvest, he will cut away the shoots, and the carcasses will be left for the birds.

The chapter ends with a reversal. The same people who sent ambassadors seeking help will one day bring gifts to Mount Zion, the place of the name of the Lord.

=== 19
The burden against Egypt follows naturally after the message to Cush. Egypt was the great power to the south, the old house of bondage, and in Isaiah's day the ally that Judah's politicians kept hoping would save them from Assyria. For much of this period it was also a divided land, split among rival rulers, until the kings of Cush united it under their control.

The first half of the chapter pictures Egypt falling apart. The Lord rides on a swift cloud into the land, and its idols tremble. Egyptian fights against Egyptian, and a harsh master rules over them. The Nile itself fails, and with it the fishermen, the flax workers and the weavers of fine linen. The famous wise men of Pharaoh's court, the princes of Zoan and Memphis, have no idea what to do.

Then the chapter takes a turn no one in Jerusalem could have expected. In a series of sayings, each beginning in that day, Egypt comes to know the Lord, with an altar in the middle of the land and a saviour sent in answer to its cries. At the very end a highway runs from Egypt to Assyria, the two great enemies worship together, and Israel stands beside them as a blessing in the midst of the earth.

=== 20
For once, an oracle comes with a precise historical setting. Sargon, king of Assyria, sent his chief commander against Ashdod, one of the Philistine cities on the coast, which had led a revolt against him in the hope of Egyptian support. Assyrian records confirm the campaign, and date the fall of Ashdod to around seven hundred and eleven before Christ. This is the only place in the Bible where Sargon is named.

Isaiah's role in these events is a strange and costly one. For three years before Ashdod fell, the Lord had told him to take off his sackcloth and sandals and walk about naked and barefoot, probably stripped to a loincloth, in the dress of a prisoner of war.

Now the meaning of the sign is given. That is how the king of Assyria will lead away the captives of Egypt and Cush, young and old, to their shame. Judah, like Ashdod, was being urged to lean on those southern powers. The chapter ends with the people of the coastland looking at the fate of the allies they had trusted and asking how they can possibly escape.

=== 21
This chapter contains three short oracles, each with a cryptic title. The first is the burden against the Desert by the Sea, and it soon becomes clear that the subject is Babylon, set among the marshes and waters of southern Mesopotamia.

The prophet describes the vision in intensely personal terms. Pain grips him like the pains of labour, and the twilight he had longed for has turned to horror. Elam and Media are summoned to the attack while the princes of Babylon are still at their feast. A lookout is posted on the watchtower, and at last the riders come with the news: fallen, fallen is Babylon. Babylon fell more than once, and it is not certain which fall this oracle first had in view. The cry itself is echoed in the book of Revelation.

The other two oracles are brief. Dumah was an oasis in northern Arabia, and its name also means silence. Someone calls from Seir, the land of Edom, to ask the watchman how much of the night is left, and receives only an enigmatic reply. The last oracle concerns the tribes and oases of Arabia, Dedan, Tema and Kedar, whose caravans and refugees are caught up in the wars of the empires.

=== 22
After Babylon, Edom and Arabia, the next burden is addressed to a place called the Valley of Vision, and it soon becomes clear that this is Jerusalem itself. The holy city takes its place among the nations under judgement.

The scene is a city in uproar. Crowds have gone up onto the rooftops in a mood of revelry, while its leaders have fled and been captured without a fight. The occasion is debated, but many think of the Assyrian invasion late in Isaiah's career. The city's preparations are described in detail: weapons drawn from the armoury called the House of the Forest, breaches in the walls repaired with stone from demolished houses, water stored in a new reservoir between the walls. They fit what Kings and Chronicles say of Hezekiah's work on Jerusalem's water supply. Yet they never looked to the One who made it all. Instead, they say, let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die, words Paul later quotes.

The chapter ends with a message to two royal officials. Shebna, the steward of the palace, is carving himself a grand tomb in the rock, and he will be thrown out of office. Eliakim son of Hilkiah will receive his robe and the key of the house of David. Both men will reappear in the story of the Assyrian siege.

=== 23
The last of the oracles against the nations is aimed at Tyre, the great Phoenician port on the coast north of Israel, together with its older neighbour Sidon. Tyre was built partly on an island, and its merchants dominated trade across the Mediterranean. Its ships carried grain from the Nile and sailed as far as Tarshish, a distant port in the far west.

The chapter opens with those ships on their way home, hearing the news when they reach Cyprus: Tyre is laid waste, without house or harbour. Who planned this against the crowning city, whose merchants are princes? The answer comes back plainly. The Lord of Hosts planned it, to humble the pride of all its glory. An aside points to the land of the Chaldeans, already ruined by Assyria, as a warning of what an empire can do.

Tyre was attacked by Assyria and later by Babylon, and the oracle cannot be tied to one siege with certainty. Its ending is unexpected. Tyre will be forgotten for seventy years, and then, like an old harlot singing in the streets to be remembered, she will return to her trade. But this time her profits will be set apart for the Lord.

=== 24
The oracles against individual nations are over. Now the vision widens to take in the whole earth. These next four chapters are often read together as a unit, and unlike the burdens before them, they name almost no particular nation or event.

The Lord lays the earth waste, and the judgement falls on everyone alike, priest and people, master and servant, lender and borrower. The earth is defiled by its inhabitants, who have broken the everlasting covenant. The joy of wine and music falls silent, and an unnamed city of chaos lies broken, its houses shut.

Even so, from the ends of the earth come voices singing glory to the Righteous One. The prophet cannot join them yet. Terror, pit and snare await the dweller of the earth, three words that sound alike in Hebrew, and the earth reels like a drunkard. The chapter closes with the Lord reigning in glory on Mount Zion, before his elders, as the elders of Israel once saw his glory on Mount Sinai.

=== 25
The ruin of the earth in the last chapter gives way to praise. The prophet speaks as one who has watched God's long-laid plans come true: a fortified city, again unnamed, has become a heap of rubble, and the Lord has proved a refuge for the poor and a shade from the heat.

Then comes one of the great visions of the Old Testament. On this mountain, Mount Zion, the Lord of Hosts will prepare a feast for all peoples, with the richest food and the finest aged wine. The shroud that covers the nations will be removed, and death itself will be swallowed up forever. The Lord God will wipe away the tears from every face. Paul quotes the promise about death being swallowed up when he writes of the resurrection, and the book of Revelation echoes the wiping away of tears.

The response of the people is simple: this is our God, we have waited for him, and he has saved us. The chapter ends with a brief word against Moab, which here seems to stand for every proud enemy of God's people, brought down into the dust.

=== 26
The vision of the whole earth continues, but now it becomes a song, one that will be sung in the land of Judah on the day of salvation. It begins with a strong city whose walls are salvation itself, and gates opened for a righteous nation to enter. It is the counterpart to the shattered city of chaos earlier in this section.

The song moves between confidence and longing. The Lord keeps in perfect peace the one whose mind is fixed on him. The people wait for him through the night and seek him at dawn. They confess that other lords have ruled over them, but they honour his name alone.

Then the mood darkens, and they admit their failure. Like a woman in labour, they have writhed in pain and given birth only to wind. Against that confession comes one of the clearest hopes of resurrection anywhere in the Old Testament: your dead will live, their bodies will rise. The chapter closes with the people told to hide in their rooms for a little while, until the Lord's wrath has passed by.

=== 27
The section that began with the ruin of the earth now draws to a close. It opens with the Lord taking up his sword against Leviathan, the coiling serpent, the dragon of the sea. The same monster appears in older Canaanite poetry from the city of Ugarit, and in the Old Testament it stands for the forces of chaos and every power set against God.

Then comes a second song of the vineyard. Back in chapter five, the Lord's vineyard produced only sour grapes and was left to thorns and briers. Now the Lord himself is its keeper, watering it and guarding it night and day, and he is not angry. Israel will take root, blossom and fill the world with fruit.

The rest of the chapter weighs Israel's suffering. Her punishment has been real, exile and a fierce east wind, but it has been measured, and its purpose is to remove her guilt and the altars of her idols. The chapter ends with the sound of a great ram's horn, calling home the exiles from Assyria and Egypt to worship on the holy mountain in Jerusalem.

=== 28
After the wide vision of the last four chapters, Isaiah comes back to the politics of his own day. A new series of messages begins here, many of them opening with the word woe, and most belong to the years when Judah was tempted to look to Egypt for protection against Assyria.

The first woe is aimed at Samaria, the capital of the northern kingdom, set on its hill above a fertile valley like a garland on a drunkard's head. It will be swallowed like an early fig. This places the oracle before Samaria's fall in seven hundred and twenty-two before Christ.

Then Isaiah turns on Jerusalem's own leaders, and they turn on him. Priests and prophets reel with wine and mock his teaching as baby talk, a matter of rules upon rules and line upon line, words that sound in Hebrew like a nursery chant. Very well, says the Lord, he will speak to them through people with foreign tongues. The rulers boast that they have made a covenant with death, probably their secret alliance, and the Lord answers by laying a tested cornerstone in Zion, a passage quoted by Paul and Peter. A closing parable about a farmer shows that God's judgements, too, are measured and purposeful.

=== 29
The next woe is addressed to Ariel, the city where David camped, which is clearly Jerusalem. The name probably plays on a word for an altar hearth, the place where the fire burns. Jerusalem's festivals go on year after year, but the Lord himself will lay siege to the city and bring it so low that its voice will whisper from the dust.

Then, suddenly, the scene reverses. The nations besieging Zion will vanish like a dream at waking, like a hungry man who dreams of food and wakes up still hungry. The Assyrian siege that Isaiah's book will describe later is never far from these chapters.

The deeper problem is the blindness of the people. Their prophets are asleep, and the vision is like a sealed scroll that no one can read. They honour the Lord with their lips while their hearts are far from him, words Jesus quoted against the religious leaders of his own day. The clay presumes to instruct the potter. But this chapter too ends in hope, with the deaf hearing the words of the scroll, the blind seeing, and the house of Jacob no longer ashamed.

=== 30
Here the reason for so many of Isaiah's warnings comes into the open. Judah's leaders, probably in the reign of Hezekiah, have sent envoys to Egypt to seek an alliance against Assyria, without asking the Lord. Their princes have reached Zoan and Hanes, cities of the Nile delta and the region south of it.

A short oracle pictures the cost. Through the dry southern desert, a land of lions and snakes, donkeys and camels carry Judah's treasures to a nation that cannot help them. Isaiah gives Egypt a mocking name, Rahab Who Sits Still. Rahab was a sea monster of ancient poetry, a different word from the name of the woman of Jericho, and here the great dragon of the south does nothing.

Isaiah is told to write his message down as a witness for the future, because the people want only pleasant visions. Their strength was meant to lie in repentance, rest and quiet trust, and they refused it. Yet the Lord still longs to be gracious. The second half of the chapter promises a Teacher no longer hidden, a voice saying this is the way, and at the end, the Assyrian king consigned to Topheth, the place of burning in the valley outside Jerusalem.

=== 31
The theme of the last chapter returns in a short and forceful oracle. Woe to those who go down to Egypt for help, who count horses and chariots and never consult the Holy One of Israel. Isaiah's argument fits in a single line: the Egyptians are men and not God, their horses flesh and not spirit. When the Lord stretches out his hand, helper and helped will fall together.

Then the Lord himself steps into the picture with two images. He is like a lion growling over its prey, unafraid of the shepherds shouting at it, and he will come down to fight on Mount Zion. He is also like birds hovering over their nest, shielding Jerusalem. The verb used for his passing over the city recalls the night of the Passover in Egypt.

The chapter ends with a call to Israel to return and throw away their idols of silver and gold, and with a prediction that will be remembered when the story of the siege is told a few chapters later. Assyria will fall, but not by any human sword.

=== 32
After so many woes, the chapter opens with a promise. A king will reign in righteousness, and his princes will rule with justice. Each will be like a shelter from the wind, streams of water in a dry land, the shadow of a great rock in a weary country. Eyes that were closed will see, and the fool will no longer be called noble.

Some of the language here sounds like the wisdom tradition of Proverbs, with its contrast between the scoundrel who plots against the poor and the noble man who makes noble plans.

Then the prophet addresses the comfortable women of Jerusalem, as he did in chapter three. In little more than a year the harvest will fail, and they will beat their breasts for the pleasant fields. The palace will be forsaken and the hill abandoned to wild donkeys, until the Spirit is poured out from on high. From that turning point the chapter ends in a picture of justice dwelling in the wilderness, and of righteousness bringing peace and quiet confidence forever.

=== 33
The last of this series of woes is aimed at an unnamed destroyer who has never yet been destroyed, a traitor who has never been betrayed. The obvious candidate in Isaiah's day is Assyria. Second Kings records that Hezekiah paid Sennacherib a heavy tribute, and the Assyrian army came against Jerusalem anyway, which may lie behind the complaint that the treaty has been broken.

The chapter moves like a liturgy, with voices taking turns. The people pray, be gracious to us, we wait for you. A lament describes the land in mourning, its highways empty and its envoys of peace weeping bitterly. Then the Lord answers: now I will arise.

The sinners in Zion tremble and ask who can live with a consuming fire. The answer is the one who walks in righteousness and refuses bribes. Such a person will see the King in his beauty. The old terrors, the foreign officials counting towers and weighing tribute, the invaders with their unintelligible speech, will be gone, and Jerusalem will be a tent whose pegs are never pulled up, beside broad rivers where no warship sails.

=== 34
Two contrasting chapters now close the first half of the book. This one summons the nations to hear a sentence of judgement against all their armies, pictured on a cosmic scale. The skies are rolled up like a scroll and the stars fall like withered leaves, imagery the book of Revelation takes up.

Then the sword of the Lord comes down on one nation in particular: Edom. The Edomites, descendants of Esau, lived in the rugged country south-east of the Dead Sea, with Bozrah as one of their chief cities. They were Israel's kin, and among its oldest and most bitter rivals. When Jerusalem finally fell to Babylon, later Scripture remembers Edom gloating and joining in the plunder.

The poem describes Edom's punishment in the language of sacrifice, and then of a land turned back to wilderness. Its streams become pitch, its soil sulphur, and its smoke rises forever. Its towers are overgrown with thorns and thistles, and the owls, hyenas and jackals take possession of it, each with its mate, according to the scroll of the Lord.

=== 35
Edom's land became a wilderness in the last chapter. Now the wilderness itself bursts into flower. The dry land rejoices, the desert blossoms, and the glory of Lebanon, Carmel and Sharon, the most fertile places in the land, is given to it.

This is a short chapter, and it reads almost like a preview of what is to come in the second half of the book. The weak and the fearful are told to be strong, because their God is coming to save them. The eyes of the blind will be opened, the ears of the deaf unstopped, and the lame will leap like a deer. Streams break out in the desert, and jackals' haunts turn to reeds and papyrus.

Through this restored land runs a highway called the Way of Holiness. No lion will prowl on it, and no fool will wander onto it. The redeemed of the Lord will travel it home and enter Zion with singing. The chapter ends with joy overtaking them and sorrow and sighing fleeing away.

=== 36
The poetry pauses, and the book turns to history. These four chapters tell the story of the Assyrian crisis in Hezekiah's reign, in an account that closely parallels Second Kings. The year is seven hundred and one before Christ. Hezekiah has rebelled against Assyria, and Sennacherib has marched west, capturing the fortified cities of Judah one after another. His own annals boast of taking forty-six of them, and carved stone reliefs from his palace at Nineveh, now in the British Museum, show the siege of Lachish, the strongest of them.

From Lachish, Sennacherib sends a senior official with an army to Jerusalem. The Bible gives him his title, the Rabshakeh. He takes his stand at the aqueduct of the upper pool, the very spot where Isaiah once met King Ahaz and urged him to trust the Lord. Three of Hezekiah's officials go out to meet him, among them Eliakim, now in charge of the palace, and Shebna, now the scribe.

What follows is a masterpiece of propaganda. The Rabshakeh mocks Egypt as a splintered reed, twists Hezekiah's religious reforms against him, and claims that the Lord himself has sent Assyria. When the officials ask him to speak Aramaic, the language of diplomacy, he turns and shouts in Hebrew to the men on the wall.

=== 37
Jerusalem has heard the Rabshakeh's speech, and Hezekiah's officials have brought it to the king with their clothes torn. Now the king tears his own clothes, puts on sackcloth, and goes into the house of the Lord. He sends his officials and the senior priests to Isaiah, asking him to pray for the remnant that is left. For the first time in the book, we see the prophet through other people's eyes, as the man the king turns to in his hour of need.

Isaiah's reply is calm and brief. Do not be afraid. Sennacherib will hear a rumour and go home. Then a second Assyrian message arrives, this time a letter, written as news comes that an army under Tirhakah of Cush is marching north. Hezekiah takes the letter into the temple, spreads it out before the Lord, and prays.

The answer comes through Isaiah in a taunt song, in which the virgin daughter of Zion tosses her head at the boastful king. What happens next is told in a few spare sentences. Sennacherib's own annals boast that he shut Hezekiah up in Jerusalem like a bird in a cage, but they never claim that he took the city.

=== 38
The opening words, in those days, link this story loosely to the time of the Assyrian crisis, and the promise within it, that God will deliver the city from the king of Assyria, suggests that it happened before the siege was lifted. As often in the Bible, events are arranged here by theme rather than strict sequence.

Hezekiah falls ill, and Isaiah comes with a stark message: put your house in order, for you will die. The king turns his face to the wall and weeps, and before long Isaiah is sent back with a different word. Fifteen years will be added to his life, and as a sign, the shadow on the stairway built by his father Ahaz will go back ten steps.

The longest part of the chapter is something found only here and not in the parallel account in Kings: a poem written by Hezekiah after his recovery. It moves from the despair of a man in the prime of life facing the gates of Sheol, rolled up like a weaver's cloth, to thanksgiving for his deliverance. Two details of the story, the poultice of figs and Hezekiah's request for a sign, are added at the very end, almost as footnotes.

=== 39
The last chapter of the historical section is short, and it looks a long way ahead. Envoys arrive from Merodach-baladan, king of Babylon, with letters and a gift to congratulate Hezekiah on his recovery. Merodach-baladan was a Chaldean leader who seized the throne of Babylon more than once in defiance of Assyria, and he had every reason to want friends in the west.

Hezekiah is flattered, and he shows the envoys everything, his silver and gold, his spices and oil, and his whole armoury. Isaiah comes with two questions: who were these men, and what did they see?

The prophet's answer introduces the name that will dominate the next part of the book. One day everything in the palace will be carried off to Babylon, and some of Hezekiah's own descendants will serve in the palace of its king. At the time Babylon was a junior player and Assyria the great threat. A little over a century later, Nebuchadnezzar would fulfil every word. The chapter ends with Hezekiah's quiet, unsettling reply.

=== 40
Between the last chapter and this one, the horizon of the book shifts dramatically. Chapter thirty-nine ended with the warning that Jerusalem's treasures and royal sons would be taken to Babylon. Chapter forty speaks as though that has already happened. Jerusalem lies in ruins, the people of Judah are in exile in Babylon in the sixth century before Christ, and Isaiah himself is not mentioned again. Whether these words were given to Isaiah long in advance or spoken by a later prophet in his tradition is the question the book introduction mentioned. The chapter itself simply begins.

Comfort, comfort my people. Jerusalem's hard service is over, and her sin is pardoned. A voice calls for a highway to be prepared in the wilderness for the Lord, words that all four Gospels apply to John the Baptist. All flesh is grass, but the word of our God stands forever.

The exiles, living in the shadow of Babylon's temples and gods, feel forgotten: my way is hidden from the Lord. The rest of the chapter answers them by setting the God of Israel against the whole world. He weighs the mountains, counts the stars by name, and regards the nations as a drop in a bucket, while the idols must be nailed down so they will not fall over.

=== 41
The comfort of chapter forty now takes the form of a trial. The Lord summons the islands and the peoples, the farthest nations of the earth, to come forward and argue their case. The question at issue is who controls history.

Exhibit one is a conqueror stirred up from the east, who turns nations to dust as he marches. He is not named here, but a few chapters later he will be: Cyrus, the Persian king who would capture Babylon in five hundred and thirty-nine before Christ. As his armies advance, the nations panic, and their craftsmen rush to make new idols, encouraging one another and nailing the images down.

In the middle of the trial, the Lord turns tenderly to his own people. You, Israel, my servant, Jacob, whom I have chosen, offspring of Abraham my friend, do not fear. This is the first appearance of a title that will become central in the coming chapters, the servant of the Lord, and here the servant is the nation itself, small and weak, called a worm, yet held by God's right hand. The chapter ends with the idols challenged to predict the future, and falling silent.

=== 42
At the end of the last chapter, the idols stood silent and empty. Now the Lord points to someone else: here is my servant, whom I uphold, my chosen one, in whom my soul delights. Since the nineteenth century, scholars have picked out four poems in these chapters that describe this servant in particular, and they have become known as the servant songs. This is the first of them.

The servant will bring justice to the nations, but not by conquest. He will not shout in the streets. He will not break a bruised reed or snuff out a smouldering wick. He will be a covenant for the people and a light to the nations. Matthew's Gospel quotes this song at length to describe the quiet ministry of Jesus.

Then the whole earth is called to sing a new song, from the seafarers and the islands to the villages of Kedar and the people of Sela in the desert. The Lord, silent for so long, goes out like a warrior. But the chapter ends with a painful paradox. Israel, also called his servant, is blind and deaf, plundered and imprisoned, and it was the Lord himself who handed them over, because they would not walk in his ways.

=== 43
Israel ended the last chapter blind, deaf and given up to plunderers. This chapter opens with two words that change the tone completely: but now. The Lord who created Jacob and formed Israel has redeemed them and called them by name. When they pass through the waters and walk through the fire, he will be with them.

The scattered people will be gathered from east and west, north and south. The courtroom scene returns, and this time Israel, blind and deaf as it is, is called into the witness box. You are my witnesses, says the Lord, that there is no saviour but me.

For the first time in this part of the book, Babylon is named outright, together with its Chaldean rulers, who will flee in their own ships. Then comes a call not to dwell on the former things, the old exodus through the sea, because the Lord is about to do something new: a road through the wilderness and rivers in the desert. The chapter ends with a frank reckoning. Israel has burdened God with its sins, yet he is the one who blots them out for his own sake.

=== 44
The promises continue, addressed to Jacob my servant and to Jeshurun, an old poetic name for Israel found in the song of Moses. God will pour out his Spirit on their descendants, and people will proudly write on their hands, belonging to the Lord. I am the first and I am the last, he says, and there is no other Rock.

At the centre of the chapter is the longest and most biting satire on idolatry in the Bible. The exiles were living among the great temples and processions of Babylon, and the prophet takes them into the workshop. A blacksmith sweats over his coals and grows faint with hunger. A carpenter measures and carves a figure of a man. Half of a tree goes into the fire to bake bread and roast meat, and from the other half the carpenter makes a god and prays to it, save me.

Against such gods stands the Lord who made all things and who confirms the word of his messengers. At the very end, the conqueror hinted at in chapter forty-one is finally named. He is Cyrus, the Lord's shepherd, who will order Jerusalem to be rebuilt and the foundations of the temple to be laid.

=== 45
The Lord now speaks directly to Cyrus, and calls him his anointed. In the Old Testament the word, from which messiah comes, is used of Israel's kings and priests. Here alone it is given to a foreign ruler, a Persian who does not know the God of Israel, chosen to bring down nations and open doors of bronze so that the exiles might go free.

History records how quickly Babylon fell. In five hundred and thirty-nine before Christ, Cyrus's army entered the city with little resistance. A clay cylinder bearing his inscription, now in the British Museum, describes his policy of sending conquered peoples and their gods back to their homelands. The book of Ezra preserves his decree allowing the Jews to return and rebuild their temple.

Some of the exiles may well have been troubled that God should use a pagan king. The prophet answers with the image of the potter and the clay. The rest of the chapter widens to all nations. Turn to me and be saved, all the ends of the earth. By myself I have sworn that every knee will bow and every tongue swear allegiance, words Paul would apply to Jesus in his letter to the Philippians.

=== 46
Babylon's fall is about to come, and the chapter begins with a scene from it. Bel and Nebo were the great gods of Babylon. Bel was a title of Marduk, the city's patron, and Nebo, or Nabu, was his son, a god of writing and wisdom whose name was carried by kings such as Nebuchadnezzar. At Babylon's New Year festival their images were carried in splendid procession. Here they are loaded onto weary pack animals and hauled away into captivity, a heavy burden that cannot save itself.

Against that picture the Lord sets a different one. The gods of Babylon have to be carried by their worshippers. The God of Israel has carried his people from the womb, and will carry them still when their hair turns grey.

The chapter repeats its challenge to the idols: to whom will you compare me? People pour out gold, hire a goldsmith, and set the result on a stand, where it cannot move or answer. Again the Lord announces his plan, summoning a bird of prey from the east, a man from a far country, to carry out his purpose.

=== 47
After the fall of Babylon's gods comes the fall of Babylon herself. This chapter is a taunt song addressed to the city as a pampered queen, the Virgin Daughter of Babylon, the Daughter of the Chaldeans. She is told to come down from her throne and sit in the dust, to take the millstones and grind flour like a slave girl, with her veil removed and her skirts lifted to wade the streams.

The reason is spelled out. God was angry with his people and handed them over to Babylon, but Babylon showed them no mercy, laying a heavy yoke even on the old. She said, I will be queen forever. Twice she speaks words that belong to God alone: I am, and there is none besides me.

Babylon was famous throughout the ancient world for its sorcerers and its astrologers, who studied the stars and made predictions month by month. The prophet invites her to call on them now, and they burn like stubble. The book of Revelation borrows the image of this boastful queen, who thinks she will never be a widow, for its own portrait of Babylon the great.

=== 48
This chapter closes the part of the book that has dealt with Cyrus and the fall of Babylon. Its tone is sterner than what has come before. The house of Jacob swears by the Lord's name and calls itself after the holy city, but not in truth. Their neck is iron, their forehead bronze.

The Lord explains why he announced events long before they happened. Had he not done so, his stubborn people would have given the credit to their idols. Now he declares new things, hidden until this moment, so that no one can say they knew them already. The exile has been a furnace of affliction, and he has refined them in it for the sake of his own name. Once more he points to Cyrus, his chosen ally, who will do his will against Babylon. Then, briefly and mysteriously, another voice speaks: the Lord God has sent me, with his Spirit.

The chapter ends with a ringing command. Leave Babylon, flee from the Chaldeans, shout it to the ends of the earth. As in the wilderness after Egypt, God will split the rock and give them water. A solemn line closes it: there is no peace for the wicked. The same words will return at the end of chapter fifty-seven.

=== 49
With Babylon's fall announced, the focus moves from Cyrus to the servant of the Lord, and to Zion. The servant himself now speaks, in the second of the servant songs, addressing the islands and the distant peoples. The Lord called him before he was born and made his mouth like a sharp sword. He is called Israel, and yet his mission is to bring Jacob back to God, so here the servant seems to be one who embodies Israel rather than the nation as a whole.

He confesses that he has laboured in vain, but the Lord answers that restoring Israel is too small a task. He will be a light to the nations, bringing salvation to the ends of the earth. In the book of Acts, Paul and Barnabas quote these words when they turn to the Gentiles.

The second half of the chapter turns to Jerusalem, pictured as a mother who believes she has been forsaken. The Lord's reply is among the tenderest in Scripture. Can a woman forget her nursing child? Even if she could, I will not forget you. I have inscribed you on the palms of my hands. Soon the bereaved city will find herself crowded with children she never knew she had.

=== 50
Zion complained that the Lord had forgotten her. Now the Lord answers a related charge, that he has divorced his people or sold them into slavery. Where is the certificate of divorce? he asks. The exile was the consequence of their own sins, not the end of the marriage, and his arm is not too short to redeem them.

Then the servant speaks again, in what has come to be called the third servant song. He describes himself as a disciple, one whose ear the Lord wakens morning by morning, so that he can sustain the weary with a word. For the first time, the songs speak of suffering. He gives his back to those who strike him and his cheeks to those who pull out his beard, and he does not hide his face from mocking and spitting. The Gospel accounts of the trial of Jesus would recall these words.

Yet he is not ashamed. He sets his face like flint, sure that the One who vindicates him is near. The chapter ends with a choice between two kinds of people, those who fear the Lord and trust him in the dark, and those who light their own fires and walk by their own torches.

=== 51
The servant has spoken of his faithfulness under suffering. Now the Lord turns to those in Israel who are seeking him, and directs them back to their beginnings. Look to the rock from which you were cut, to Abraham and Sarah. When God called Abraham he was only one man, and God made him many. In the same way he will comfort Zion's ruins and make her wilderness like Eden.

The chapter has the urgency of a series of wake-up calls. First the people cry out to the arm of the Lord: awake, awake, as in the days of old, when you cut Rahab to pieces and dried up the sea for the redeemed to cross. The ancient monster of chaos and the waters of the exodus are joined in one memory. A line from chapter thirty-five returns, as the ransomed of the Lord come to Zion with singing.

Then the call is turned around. Awake, awake, Jerusalem, the Lord says. The city has drunk the cup of his fury to the dregs, with no son left to take her by the hand. Now he takes that cup from her hand and gives it to her tormentors.

=== 52
The wake-up calls continue, but now Zion is told to put on her garments of splendour and shake off the dust of captivity. She was sold for nothing, and she will be redeemed without money. The Lord recalls her history of oppression, in Egypt and then under Assyria, and now in exile once more.

Then comes a scene on the hills around Jerusalem. A runner appears on the mountains with good news, and the watchmen on the ruined walls take up the shout: your God reigns. Paul quotes this verse about beautiful feet when he writes about the preaching of the gospel. The exiles are told to depart from Babylon, carrying the vessels of the Lord, the temple treasures Nebuchadnezzar had taken. The book of Ezra records them being handed back under Cyrus. Unlike the first exodus, this departure will not be in haste; the Lord will go before them and behind them.

In the final verses the fourth and greatest servant song begins. The chapter division falls in the middle of it. My servant will be raised and lifted up, and many will be appalled, because his appearance is marred beyond human likeness. Kings will be silenced by what they see.

=== 53
The fourth servant song, which began at the end of the last chapter, continues here, and this is perhaps the most quoted passage in the whole of the Old Testament. The voice changes. Now a group, speaking as we, looks back with astonishment on the servant they once despised.

He grew up like a tender shoot from dry ground, with nothing in his appearance to attract anyone. He was despised and rejected, a man of sorrows. They assumed that God had struck him down. Only later did they see that he was pierced for their transgressions and crushed for their iniquities. He went silently like a lamb to the slaughter, was cut off from the land of the living, and was given a grave with the wicked and with a rich man. Yet the song ends not in death but in vindication, as the servant sees his offspring and justifies many.

Jewish interpreters have often understood the servant as Israel, or as a faithful remnant within it. The New Testament reads the song as a portrait of Jesus, quoting it many times. In the book of Acts, an Ethiopian court official is reading this very chapter in his chariot when Philip asks him if he understands it, and the official asks who the prophet is speaking about.
