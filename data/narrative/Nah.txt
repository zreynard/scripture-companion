=== intro
The book of Nahum is about the fall of Nineveh. Its first line calls it a burden against Nineveh, and from beginning to end it is a single sustained cry over the coming destruction of the great Assyrian capital. Little is known of the prophet himself. He is called Nahum the Elkoshite, and his name means comfort, but where Elkosh was is uncertain. Suggestions range from Galilee to southern Judah.

The listener last saw Nineveh in the book of Jonah, repenting in sackcloth and spared by the Lord's compassion. More than a century has passed since then, and the city Nahum addresses is the capital of the most powerful empire the world had yet seen. In those years Assyria had destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel and carried its people away. It had overrun Judah under Sennacherib, besieging Jerusalem in the days of Hezekiah. Its kings had pushed as far as Egypt, and in about six hundred and sixty-three before Christ they sacked Thebes, the ancient royal city on the Nile, far up the river. Their own inscriptions and palace carvings celebrate deportations, impalements, and piles of severed heads. Sennacherib had rebuilt Nineveh on a magnificent scale, with vast walls, gardens, canals, and a palace he called the palace without rival.

Nahum mentions the fall of Thebes as something already past, so he spoke after it. He also speaks of Nineveh's fall as still to come, and that happened in six hundred and twelve before Christ, when the Babylonians and the Medes stormed the city. His prophecy therefore belongs somewhere in the half-century between those two events. During much of that time Judah was ruled by Manasseh, who reigned longer than any other king of Judah and was a loyal vassal of Assyria. Nahum may have spoken in those years, or later, under Josiah, as Assyria's grip began to fail. Either way, to his hearers the empire's collapse would have seemed almost unimaginable.

The book does not record any message to Nineveh's own people. It was spoken for Judah, as comfort for a small nation that had lived for a century under Assyria's yoke. Its first chapter is a hymn to the Lord as a God of justice who is slow to anger yet will not leave the guilty unpunished, words that deliberately recall the description of the Lord that Jonah had quoted with such resentment. The same God who spared Nineveh then will not spare it now. The second and third chapters are vivid battle poetry, among the most powerful in the Old Testament: chariots in the streets, the river gates opened, the palace collapsing, the plunder carried off.

Nahum is unusual among the prophets in another way. He has no word of rebuke for Judah. Its subject is the end of an oppressor, and the relief of all who had suffered under it. Habakkuk and Zephaniah, who follow in the Twelve, belong to roughly the same generation, and they would see that the power rising to replace Assyria, Babylon, was a danger of its own.

=== 1
The book opens with a hymn about the Lord himself before it ever names the enemy. In the Hebrew, part of it is an acrostic, its lines beginning with successive letters of the alphabet. The Lord is a jealous and avenging God. He is slow to anger and great in power, and he will by no means leave the guilty unpunished. He marches in the whirlwind, and the mountains quake before him, yet he is a stronghold for those who trust him.

Then come short oracles that turn back and forth between two addressees, sometimes without warning. To Nineveh comes judgement: it will be swept away as by a flood, and the Lord will prepare its grave. To Judah comes comfort: I have afflicted you, but I will afflict you no longer, and I will break the yoke from your neck.

The chapter ends with a messenger running across the mountains with good news. Judah can celebrate its festivals again, because the wicked will never again march through the land.

=== 2
The vision now moves to the walls of Nineveh. An attacker is advancing, and the city is told, with bitter irony, to guard its fortress and summon all its strength.

What follows is battle poetry in short, breathless lines. The attackers' shields are red and their warriors dressed in scarlet. Chariots flash like torches through the streets. The defenders stumble as they hurry to the wall. Then the river gates are thrown open and the palace collapses. Nineveh stood where the Khosr river flows into the Tigris, and its rulers had built canals and dams around it. Ancient writers later told of floods playing a part in its fall, though the details are uncertain.

The city is plundered of its endless treasure. Then Nahum turns to an image Assyria's own kings loved, the lion. Their palace walls showed them hunting lions, and they likened themselves to lions in their inscriptions. Now the lions' den is empty, and the Lord declares that he is against them.

=== 3
The final chapter is a woe pronounced over the city of blood. It opens with a picture in sound: the crack of the whip, the rumble of wheels, galloping horses, and then the dead lying in heaps. Nineveh, which had lived by plunder, is pictured as a seductive harlot who has ensnared nations, and now she will be exposed before them.

Then Nahum asks Nineveh a pointed question. Are you better than Thebes? That great city of Upper Egypt, ringed by the Nile and backed by allies from Cush, Put and Libya, had been sacked by the Assyrians themselves only a few decades before. What Nineveh had done to others would now be done to her.

The rest of the chapter mocks its preparations for siege. Its fortresses are ripe figs ready to fall, and its merchants and officials will vanish like a swarm of locusts. Nahum ends by addressing the king of Assyria, whose shepherds are asleep. Like Jonah, the other book about Nineveh, it closes with a question, but this one has no gentleness in it.
