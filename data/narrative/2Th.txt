=== intro
The second letter to the Thessalonians returns to the same young church in northern Greece, and it comes from the same three men: Paul, Silvanus and Timothy. It followed the first letter within months, around the year fifty, while Paul was still in Corinth.

The listener will remember how this church began. Paul and Silas came to Thessalonica, the capital of Macedonia, after being beaten and jailed at Philippi. They preached for a short time, a mob drove them out, and the new believers were left to face the hostility of their neighbours on their own. Timothy went back to see them and returned with good news, and Paul wrote his first letter in relief and gratitude. In it he spoke a great deal about the coming of the Lord: what would happen to believers who had died, and how the Day of the Lord would arrive like a thief in the night.

Since then, things have moved on, and not entirely for the better. The persecution has not eased, and the believers are still enduring it with growing faith. But confusion has crept in about the very subject Paul had written on. The church is being told that the Day of the Lord has already come, and the claim is being passed off as Paul's own, by a spirit, a message or a letter supposedly from him. Paul writes to steady them.

There is a practical problem too. In his first letter Paul had urged the Thessalonians to live quietly and work with their own hands. Now he hears that some members of the church are refusing to work at all, living off the generosity of others and busying themselves in everyone's affairs. Paul answers firmly, pointing again to his own example while he was among them.

The letter is brief and moves in three steps. The first chapter is a thanksgiving for the church's endurance under persecution, with an assurance that God will set things right when the Lord Jesus is revealed from heaven. The second takes on the false report about the Day of the Lord, describing events that must happen first, including the appearance of a figure Paul calls the man of lawlessness. He was reminding the Thessalonians of things he had already told them in person, and so he could be brief. The third chapter turns to prayer, to the problem of idleness, and to a closing greeting that Paul writes in his own hand.

Together the two letters to Thessalonica give a rare picture of a church only months old, under pressure from outside, unsettled within, and held close by the man who founded it.

=== 1
The greeting is almost identical to the one that opened the first letter. Paul, Silvanus and Timothy write again to the church of the Thessalonians, wishing them grace and peace.

Then comes the thanksgiving, and it builds on what Paul heard before. The believers' faith has grown and their love for one another has increased. Paul says he boasts about them among the other churches, because of their endurance in all the persecution and affliction they are bearing. The trouble that began when Paul was driven out of the city has clearly not ended.

The rest of the chapter speaks into that suffering. Paul assures the Thessalonians that God is just, and that he will repay those who afflict them and give rest to those who are afflicted. The setting for that justice is the day when the Lord Jesus is revealed from heaven with his mighty angels. The language is stark and solemn, drawn in places from the Old Testament prophets. The chapter ends with a prayer that God will make them worthy of his calling, so that the name of the Lord Jesus may be honoured in them.

=== 2
Having spoken of the coming of the Lord Jesus, Paul now turns to the real reason for writing. The Thessalonians have been shaken by a claim that the Day of the Lord has already come. It has even been passed off as coming from Paul himself.

Paul's answer is that certain things must happen first. There will be a rebellion, and a figure he calls the man of lawlessness will be revealed, who sets himself up in the temple of God and claims to be God. Something, or someone, is holding him back for now. Paul reminds them that he had told them all this in person, and his brief words lean on that earlier teaching.

The chapter then turns from warning to thanksgiving. In contrast to those who are deceived, the Thessalonians were chosen and called through the gospel. Paul urges them to stand firm and hold on to what he taught them, in person and by letter, and he closes with a prayer for their encouragement.

=== 3
The difficult teaching about the Day of the Lord is behind us, and Paul begins to wind the letter down. He asks for the Thessalonians' prayers, that the message may spread quickly and that he and his companions may be rescued from wicked men. Acts records fierce opposition to Paul in Corinth, where he was writing.

Then he takes up a problem he had touched on gently in the first letter. Some in the church are living in idleness, refusing to work and relying on others, and meddling in other people's affairs. Paul is now direct. He reminds them that while he was with them he worked night and day, paying for his own food, though he had the right to be supported. He quotes a rule he gave them at the time: anyone unwilling to work should not eat. The church is to keep its distance from those who refuse, but to treat them as brothers and not as enemies.

The letter closes with a prayer for peace, and then a final line in Paul's own handwriting. Paul usually dictated his letters, and this handwritten greeting is his mark of authenticity, a pointed touch in a letter that has warned against messages falsely claiming to be his.
