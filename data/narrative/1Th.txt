=== intro
This short letter takes us back to the early days of the Christian mission in Europe, some twenty years after the crucifixion. It was written by Paul, the former Pharisee who had once hunted down followers of Jesus and then became the most tireless of his messengers. With him were two companions whose names stand beside his in the opening line: Silvanus, the man Acts calls Silas, and the younger Timothy. It is one of the earliest of Paul's letters, and one of the oldest pieces of Christian writing we have.

Acts tells how the church in Thessalonica began. On his second great journey Paul crossed from Asia Minor into Macedonia, in northern Greece, after a vision of a man pleading with him to come over and help. At Philippi he and Silas were beaten and thrown into prison. Released and asked to leave, they travelled west along the great Roman road, the Via Egnatia, to Thessalonica, the largest city of the province and its capital, a busy port at the head of a wide gulf. There Paul spoke in the synagogue on three Sabbaths. Some Jews were persuaded, along with many Greeks who worshipped the God of Israel and a number of leading women. The letter itself shows that most of the believers had come straight out of the worship of idols.

The mission ended abruptly. Opponents gathered a mob, stormed the house of a man named Jason, where Paul was staying, and accused the newcomers of proclaiming another king in defiance of Caesar. The believers sent Paul and Silas away by night to Berea, and when trouble followed them there, Paul was taken on to Athens, and from there he went to Corinth. He had left behind a young church with no long history, few teachers, and hostile neighbours, and he had been with them only a short while.

That is the situation behind the letter. Paul was desperate to know whether the new believers had held on. He had tried more than once to return and could not. So he sent Timothy back to Thessalonica to strengthen them and bring word. When Timothy finally rejoined Paul in Corinth, he brought good news: the Thessalonians were standing firm, still loved Paul, and longed to see him. This letter is Paul's response, written around the year fifty, during the eighteen months he spent in Corinth.

The letter is warm and personal. Paul is relieved, grateful, and anxious to reassure. He reminds them how he lived among them, never with flattery or a pretext for greed, working with his own hands night and day so as to be no burden to anyone. He also turns to what is troubling them. Some members of the church have died since Paul left, and the others are grieving and unsure what this means for those who have fallen asleep before the Lord returns.

The first three chapters look back, recalling how the gospel arrived, how Paul behaved, how he was torn away, and how Timothy's report brought him joy. The last two chapters look forward, with instruction about holy living, quiet work, the hope of the resurrection, and the coming Day of the Lord, before a string of short closing appeals. The return of Jesus runs through the whole letter, closing nearly every chapter.

Before long Paul would write to the same church again. That second letter follows this one in the New Testament, and the two are best heard together.

=== 1
The letter opens in the plain style of an ancient greeting: the senders, Paul, Silvanus and Timothy, then the recipients, the church of the Thessalonians, then a wish of grace and peace. Paul does not add his title of apostle here, as he does in most of his letters.

What follows is a long, glad thanksgiving. Timothy has just come back with his report, and Paul's relief spills over. He remembers how the message first arrived in Thessalonica, not just as talk but with power and deep conviction, and how the believers received it with joy even while they suffered for it. He has already heard about them from others. Word of what happened in Thessalonica has travelled across Macedonia and Achaia, the two Roman provinces of Greece, and further still.

The chapter closes with a compact summary of what that change looked like: they turned to God from idols, to serve the living and true God, and to wait for his Son from heaven. Those last words introduce the hope that will run through every chapter of the letter.

=== 2
Paul now looks back at his time in Thessalonica, and he does so with unusual care. Travelling teachers were a familiar sight in Greek cities, and some lived off their audiences. Paul wants the believers to remember that he was nothing of the kind: no deceit, no flattery, no pretext for greed. God is his witness.

So he reminds the believers of what they saw for themselves. He had come to them still bruised from his treatment at Philippi. He worked night and day at his trade so as not to be a burden, and he cared for them like a nursing mother, and then like a father with his children. He turns to the opposition they have faced from their own countrymen, comparing it with what the churches in Judea suffered, and he speaks sharply of those who killed the Lord Jesus and the prophets, drove the apostles out, and now try to stop them telling the Gentiles how they may be saved.

The chapter ends with Paul's own side of the separation. He had been torn away from them, he says, and has longed to come back. He tried again and again, but Satan blocked the way. They, he says, are his hope and joy and crown when the Lord Jesus comes.

=== 3
Unable to return himself, Paul explains what he did instead. He could bear the silence no longer, so he chose to be left alone in Athens and sent Timothy back to Thessalonica. Timothy was to strengthen the believers and find out whether the hardships had shaken their faith. Paul had warned them, while he was still with them, that suffering would come. It had, and he feared his work among them might have been for nothing.

Now Timothy has returned with good news. The Thessalonians are holding firm in faith and love, they think of Paul with affection, and they long to see him as much as he longs to see them. Paul's response is close to joy at a reprieve: now we can go on living, he says, as long as you are standing firm in the Lord.

The chapter ends with a prayer that God would open the way for Paul to come to them, and would make their love overflow and their hearts blameless when the Lord Jesus comes with all his holy ones. With that prayer, the first half of the letter, looking back on their shared past, comes to a close.

=== 4
With the word finally, Paul turns from looking back to giving direction. He reminds the Thessalonians of the instructions he gave them in person, and urges them to go on living that way more and more.

He begins with sexual conduct. In the Greek cities of the day, sexual freedom outside marriage was widely accepted for men, and converts from that world needed to hear plainly what holiness meant in practice. He then speaks of brotherly love, which they are already showing across Macedonia, and of a quiet, industrious life, working with their own hands so as to earn the respect of outsiders and depend on no one.

The last part of the chapter addresses a real grief. Some of the believers in Thessalonica have died since Paul left, and those who remain are grieving for them, unsure what the Lord's return will mean for those who have fallen asleep. Paul does not want them to mourn like people who have no hope. He sets out what will happen at the coming of the Lord, for the dead and the living alike, and tells them to comfort one another with these words.

=== 5
Paul has just answered the question about believers who have died. Now he takes up a related one: when will the Lord come? Paul says they need no letter about the times and seasons, because they already know that the Day of the Lord will come like a thief in the night, unexpectedly, just when people are saying, peace and security.

Rather than dates, Paul gives them a way to live while they wait. He contrasts night and day, sleep and wakefulness, and calls them children of the day, to be alert and sober, armed with faith, love and hope.

The letter then gathers pace into a series of short instructions about life together in the church: respect for those who lead and care for them, patience with the weak, joy, prayer, thanksgiving, and careful attention to prophecy. It ends with a prayer for their complete holiness, a request for their prayers in return, and a solemn charge that this letter be read aloud to all the brothers.
