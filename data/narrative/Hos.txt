=== intro
With Hosea we come to a new part of the Old Testament. The last twelve books are short prophetic works that Jewish tradition copied together on a single scroll and called simply the Twelve. They are known as the minor prophets only because of their length. Between them they cover about three centuries, from the days when Assyria dominated the Near East to the years after the return from exile under Persia, and they are not arranged in the order in which they were spoken. Hosea stands first, and it belongs among the earliest of them.

Hosea son of Beeri spoke to the northern kingdom of Israel in the eighth century before Christ. The listener met that kingdom in the books of Kings: the ten tribes that broke away from Solomon's son Rehoboam, with their capital at Samaria and their royal shrines at Bethel and Dan, where Jeroboam son of Nebat set up the golden calves. Unlike Amos, who came up from Judah, Hosea was a man of the north speaking to his own people. His ministry began under a later Jeroboam, Jeroboam son of Jehoash, whose reign of some forty years gave Israel its last great season of strength. Its borders were pushed out almost to where they had been under Solomon, trade flourished, and the shrines were crowded with worshippers.

Hosea saw a sickness beneath the prosperity. Alongside the Lord, the people were serving Baal, the Canaanite storm god who was believed to send the rain and make fields and flocks fertile. Many did not think of this as abandoning the Lord at all. They simply mixed the two on the same hilltops. To Hosea this was adultery, and he describes it in the language of a broken marriage, because his own marriage had become the message. The book opens with it. The Lord tells Hosea to marry a woman who will be unfaithful, and he marries Gomer. Their children's names are prophecies, and Hosea's costly love for his wayward wife becomes a living picture of the Lord and Israel.

The years that followed were among the darkest in Israel's history. After Jeroboam's death the second book of Kings records a rapid run of murders and usurpers. His son Zechariah, the last of Jehu's dynasty, was killed after six months. Shallum lasted a month. Menahem paid a heavy tribute to the king of Assyria to keep his throne. His son Pekahiah was struck down by his officer Pekah, and Pekah in turn by Hoshea, the last king of Israel, whose name in Hebrew is the same as the prophet's. All the while Assyria, under Tiglath-pileser and then Shalmaneser, was pressing westward, and the kings of Samaria lurched between paying tribute to Assyria and looking to Egypt for help. In the end Hoshea rebelled, Samaria was besieged for three years, and in about seven hundred and twenty-two before Christ the city fell and its people were carried into exile.

Hosea lived through much of this. The opening line dates his ministry by the kings of Judah from Uzziah to Hezekiah, a span of thirty years or more. Most of his words look ahead to the fall of Samaria. His contemporaries were Amos, a little earlier, and in Judah, Isaiah and Micah.

After the three chapters about his family, the rest of the book is a long collection of oracles, seldom dated and not arranged as a chronicle. The poetry is dense and abrupt. It moves without warning from accusation to grief to tenderness, and it is full of images from farm and household: a stubborn heifer, a half-baked cake, morning mist, a lion, a parent teaching a child to walk. Again and again Hosea reaches back into Israel's past, to the exodus, the wilderness and the patriarch Jacob. His usual name for the nation is Ephraim, after its largest tribe, and Judah appears from time to time, sometimes warned, sometimes set in contrast.

Hosea's picture of the Lord as a wounded husband would be taken up by Jeremiah and Ezekiel more than a century later, and several of his lines are quoted in the New Testament. His words were kept in Judah after the northern kingdom was gone, and the book ends not with Samaria's ruin but with an invitation to return.

=== 1
The book begins with its date line and then moves straight to the first word the Lord gave Hosea. It is a command about his own life. He is to take a wife who will prove unfaithful, because the land itself has been unfaithful to the Lord.

He marries Gomer daughter of Diblaim, and three children follow, each named by the Lord. The first is Jezreel, after the valley and town where, nearly a century earlier, Jehu had seized the throne in a bloodbath and wiped out the house of Ahab. King Jeroboam is Jehu's great-grandson, and the name announces that the dynasty founded in blood will end. The second child, a daughter, is Lo-ruhamah, meaning no compassion. The third is Lo-ammi, not my people, words that strike at the heart of the covenant.

Yet the chapter does not end there. In its closing lines the names are turned inside out, and a future is promised in which Israel and Judah are gathered together again under one leader.

=== 2
The story of Hosea's household now becomes poetry, and the line between Gomer and Israel begins to blur. The Lord speaks as a husband bringing a case against an unfaithful wife, and the children are told to plead with their mother.

Her lovers are the Baals, the local forms of the god thought to give rain and harvest. The irony is that the grain, the new wine and the oil were the Lord's gifts all along, and Israel has credited them to someone else. So he will take them back and silence her festivals.

Then, midway, the tone changes. Instead of driving her away, the Lord will lead her into the wilderness, where Israel first knew him after Egypt, and speak tenderly to her. The Valley of Achor, remembered from Joshua's day as the valley of trouble, will become a gateway of hope. There is wordplay too: the Hebrew baal also means master, or husband. By the end the children's names are reversed, and the apostle Paul would later hear in these lines a promise reaching even to the Gentiles.

=== 3
This brief chapter returns to Hosea's own story, and now he tells it himself. His wife has gone to another man, and the Lord tells him to go and love her again, as the Lord loves Israel even while they turn to other gods. The text calls her simply his wife, and most readers take her to be Gomer.

Hosea pays to bring her back, partly in silver and partly in barley. He then sets terms: a long period in which she will belong to no one else, and he will wait.

The meaning follows at once. Israel too must live for many days without king or prince, without sacrifice and the objects of their worship. Only afterwards will they return and seek the Lord their God and David their king. Spoken to the north, that last phrase looks for a day when the divided kingdoms are united again under David's line. With this the first part of the book is complete.

=== 4
From here to the end, the book is a collection of Hosea's oracles. The story of the marriage is over, but its language runs through everything that follows.

The first oracle opens like a court case. The Lord has a charge against the people of the land: there is no truth, no loving devotion, and no knowledge of God. The commandments are broken one after another, and even the land mourns.

Much of the blame falls on the priests, whose task was to teach the law. Because they have forgotten it, the people are destroyed for lack of knowledge, and the verdict is blunt: like people, like priest. Worship has moved to the hilltops and the shade of sacred trees, bound up with ritual prostitution. Two shrines are named, Gilgal and Beth-aven. Beth-aven, house of wickedness, is Hosea's scornful name for Bethel, house of God, the royal sanctuary of the north. And here the nation is first called Ephraim, the name Hosea will use most often.

=== 5
The next oracle summons three groups at once: the priests, the house of Israel and the royal household. Leaders who should have guided the people have become a snare at Mizpah and a net spread on Mount Tabor.

Partway through comes a sudden alarm. Trumpets are to sound in Gibeah, Ramah and Beth-aven, towns on the border of Benjamin between Judah and Israel. These are the years of the war of about seven hundred and thirty-four before Christ, when Israel and Damascus attacked Judah, King Ahaz of Judah called on Assyria for help, and armies crossed these borderlands. Hosea finds both kingdoms at fault. Judah's princes are like men moving boundary stones.

Ephraim's answer to its troubles is to send to the great king of Assyria, as Menahem had done with his tribute. But Assyria cannot heal this wound. The Lord himself is tearing them like a lion, and he will withdraw until they seek him.

=== 6
The chapter opens with a song of return. Come, it says, let us return to the Lord, for he has torn us and he will heal us. These are the people's own words, a hopeful response to the warnings they have just heard.

The Lord's answer is full of grief. What shall I do with you, he asks Ephraim and Judah, for your loyalty is like a morning mist, like the early dew that vanishes. Their repentance is real in the moment and gone by mid-morning. Then comes one of Hosea's best-known lines: the Lord desires mercy, not sacrifice, and the knowledge of God rather than burnt offerings. Jesus would quote it twice in the Gospel of Matthew.

The rest of the chapter names specific crimes. Gilead, east of the Jordan, is tracked with bloody footprints, and bands of priests lie in ambush on the road to Shechem. A brief word to Judah closes the chapter: a harvest is appointed for them too.

=== 7
Hosea now takes us into the royal court at Samaria, in the years of plots and assassinations after Jeroboam's death. Courtiers flatter the king while they plan his murder. On the king's feast day the princes are inflamed with wine, and their conspiracy smoulders through the night like a baker's oven, until in the morning it blazes up. All their kings fall, the Lord says, and not one of them calls on me. Within about twenty years, four kings of Israel were killed by the men who replaced them.

The second half turns to foreign policy. Ephraim has mixed itself among the nations, and like a cake left unturned on the griddle, it is burnt on one side and raw on the other. Its strength drains away to foreigners, and like a man going grey, it has not noticed. Ephraim is a silly, senseless dove, fluttering first towards Egypt and then towards Assyria. Israel's last kings did exactly that.

=== 8
The ram's horn sounds again, a warning of attack, and an eagle hovers over the house of the Lord, the land and people as a whole. The danger from Assyria is coming closer.

The Lord gives his reasons. Israel has made kings without consulting him, a pointed description of the coups of these years. They have made idols of silver and gold, and above all they cling to the calf of Samaria, the golden calf at Bethel that had stood at the heart of the northern kingdom's worship since Jeroboam son of Nebat. A craftsman made it, the Lord says, and it is not God.

Here is one of Hosea's most memorable sayings: they sow the wind, and they shall reap the whirlwind. Ephraim has hired lovers among the nations, buying protection with tribute. Last comes a threat that will recur. They will return to Egypt. The people once rescued from slavery will lose their freedom again.

=== 9
This message is spoken at a harvest festival, when the threshing floors were full and the wine was being pressed. Hosea interrupts the celebrations: do not rejoice, O Israel, like the nations. The harvest has been treated as Baal's gift, and soon there will be no feasts at all. The people will eat unclean food in Assyria, and Memphis, the ancient city of Egypt, will bury them.

The chapter gives a rare glimpse of how Hosea himself was received. The prophet is called a fool, the inspired man insane, and snares are laid on his path.

Then he reaches back into Israel's history. The Lord found Israel like grapes in the wilderness, a delight, but at Baal-peor, on the edge of the promised land, they gave themselves to a foreign god. The days of Gibeah, the dreadful crime at the end of Judges, are recalled, and so is Gilgal. Its last words are hard: they will be wanderers among the nations.

=== 10
Israel is pictured as a luxuriant vine, and the image becomes an accusation. The more the land prospered, the more altars and sacred pillars the people built.

A time is coming, Hosea says, when they will admit they have no king, and that a king could not have saved them. The calf of Beth-aven, their pride, will be carried off to Assyria as tribute to the great king, and its priests will mourn it. Samaria's king will be swept away like a twig on the water.

Once more the past is called as a witness: since the days of Gibeah, Israel has sinned. Then the farm images return. Ephraim is a heifer that likes the easy work of threshing and will now be yoked to the plough. In the middle of the warnings comes a call to sow righteousness and break up the unploughed ground, because there is still time to seek the Lord. Its closing lines recall Shalman's destruction of Beth-arbel, an atrocity the first hearers knew well.

=== 11
After so much accusation, this chapter opens in a different voice. The Lord speaks not as a husband but as a parent. When Israel was a child, he says, I loved him, and out of Egypt I called my son. He remembers teaching Ephraim to walk, holding them by the arms, bending down to feed them.

The memory makes the rebellion all the more painful, and for a moment judgement seems to be the last word. But then the Lord's heart turns within him. How could I give you up, Ephraim, he asks. How could I make you like Admah and Zeboiim, two towns overthrown with Sodom and Gomorrah. For I am God, and not a man.

The chapter closes with the Lord roaring like a lion, and his children coming home trembling from Egypt and Assyria like birds returning to their nests. Matthew's Gospel would later hear the line about the son called out of Egypt in the childhood of Jesus.

=== 12
Ephraim feeds on the wind. The chapter opens with the double game of Israel's last years, a treaty with Assyria while olive oil is sent to Egypt to win its favour. The second book of Kings records that King Hoshea did just this, sending to So king of Egypt while still bound to Assyria.

Then Hosea turns to the nation's ancestor. Jacob, whose other name was Israel, grasped his brother's heel in the womb, wrestled with God, wept and sought his favour, and met him at Bethel. He fled to Aram and tended sheep to win a wife. Hosea holds up the patriarch's story as a mirror: the nation has inherited Jacob's grasping, but not his seeking.

The merchant with dishonest scales who boasts that no one can find fault in him is Ephraim itself. Against this the Lord sets his own long history with them, from Egypt onwards, and the prophets he sent, beginning with Moses, by whom Israel was brought out and preserved.

=== 13
The end of the northern kingdom is very near in this chapter. It begins with Ephraim's past greatness, when its word made others tremble. But through Baal it died, and now the people make silver idols and kiss the calves.

A sequence of fierce images follows. They will vanish like morning mist, like chaff from the threshing floor, like smoke from a window. The Lord, who knew them in the wilderness, will meet them like a lion, a leopard and a bear robbed of her cubs. The kings they demanded cannot save them. In my anger I gave you a king, the Lord says, and in my wrath I took him away. Israel's last kings were removed one by one, and Hoshea himself ended his reign in an Assyrian prison.

A line about death and Sheol follows that the apostle Paul would later echo in his great chapter on the resurrection. Here it stands amid judgement, and the chapter ends with Samaria facing the horrors of siege.

=== 14
The book ends with an invitation. After all the warnings, Hosea calls Israel to return to the Lord, and even gives them the words to say. They are to ask him to take away their iniquity, and to renounce the three things they have trusted: Assyria, the horses of war, and the idols made by their own hands.

The Lord's answer is the gentlest passage in the book. He will heal their unfaithfulness and love them freely. Earlier, Israel's loyalty vanished like the dew; now the Lord himself will be like the dew to Israel. The images come from the north's own landscape: the lily, the olive tree, the cedars and the wine of Lebanon.

The final line is addressed to anyone wise enough to understand, as though to the readers who would study this scroll in Judah long after Samaria was gone. The ways of the Lord are right, it says, and the righteous walk in them. The next book of the Twelve, Joel, comes from a time that is much harder to fix.
