=== intro
Zephaniah is the last of the prophets in the Twelve who spoke before the Babylonian exile. His book opens with an unusually long family line, tracing his ancestry back four generations to a man named Hezekiah. The book does not call him king, but many readers have thought this was King Hezekiah of Judah, which would make Zephaniah a distant member of the royal family. If so, it may explain how well he knew the court and the city of Jerusalem, which he describes district by district.

He prophesied in the days of Josiah son of Amon, king of Judah, who reigned for about thirty years in the second half of the seventh century before Christ. To understand the moment, the listener needs to recall what came just before. Josiah's grandfather Manasseh had reigned for more than half a century, the longest reign of any king in Jerusalem, and the second book of Kings remembers him as the worst. He rebuilt the high places, set up altars to Baal and to the stars of heaven in the temple courts, practised sorcery, and shed much innocent blood. His son Amon followed the same path and was assassinated after two years. Josiah came to the throne at the age of eight.

The Judah Zephaniah describes still bears Manasseh's stamp. People bow on their rooftops to the host of heaven. They swear by the Lord and by Milcom, the god of the Ammonites, in the same breath. Officials wear foreign clothes, merchants grow rich, and the comfortable assume that the Lord will do nothing, good or bad. This suggests that Zephaniah spoke early in Josiah's reign, before the king's great reform. That reform came in his eighteenth year, when the Book of the Law was found in the temple and Josiah tore down the pagan altars throughout the land. Zephaniah's preaching may have helped to prepare the ground for it, though the book does not say so.

Beyond Judah's borders the world was shifting. Assyria, which had dominated the region for more than a century, was weakening, and Zephaniah foresees Nineveh's ruin as still to come. Within a few decades Babylon would take its place. The young Jeremiah began his ministry in these same years, and Nahum and Habakkuk were near contemporaries.

The great theme of Zephaniah is the Day of the Lord, which he describes more fully and more fearfully than any other prophet. It is near, he says, a day of wrath, distress and darkness, and its reach will be universal. The book moves from judgement on Judah and Jerusalem, to judgement on the nations on every side, and back to Jerusalem again. But it does not end there. Its final scene is one of the most joyful in the prophets: a humble remnant gathered, their shame removed, and the Lord himself rejoicing over his people with singing.

=== 1
The book's first words after the family line are a sweeping announcement. The Lord will sweep away everything from the face of the earth: people and animals, birds of the air and fish of the sea. The order runs in reverse of creation, as if the world were being undone.

Then the focus narrows to Judah and Jerusalem. The Lord will cut off every remnant of Baal and the pagan priests, those who worship the stars on their rooftops, those who swear by Milcom as well as the Lord, and those who have simply stopped seeking him. The royal princes and officials who dress in foreign clothes are named too, and those who leap over the threshold, perhaps a pagan custom.

Zephaniah then walks the listener through the city: a cry from the Fish Gate, wailing from the Second District, the newer quarter of Jerusalem, and the silencing of merchants in the market quarter called the Hollow. The Lord will search Jerusalem with lamps for the complacent.

The chapter ends with a famous description of the Day of the Lord, a day of wrath, darkness and gloom. A medieval Latin hymn on the day of judgement took its opening words, Dies irae, day of wrath, from this passage.

=== 2
The second chapter begins with a call to act before it is too late. Gather together, Zephaniah tells the shameful nation, before the decree takes effect. Seek the Lord, all you humble of the land. Perhaps you will be sheltered on the day of his anger. It is the one note of possible escape in a book of warnings, and it is offered to the humble.

Then the prophet turns to the nations around Judah, looking in each direction in turn. To the west lie the Philistine cities on the coast, Gaza, Ashkelon, Ashdod and Ekron, whose land will become pasture for the remnant of Judah. To the east are Moab and Ammon, Israel's old neighbours across the Jordan, condemned for their taunts and threats. To the south are the Cushites, the people of the upper Nile, whose kings had ruled Egypt within living memory.

Last, and greatest, is Assyria to the north. In Zephaniah's day Nineveh was still standing, but he sees it already as a desert, with flocks lying down among its ruins and owls roosting on its pillars. The city that said in its heart, I am, and there is none besides me, will be a ruin.

=== 3
The final chapter opens with a woe against a city that is rebellious, defiled and oppressive. At first the listener might expect it to be another foreign capital, after Nineveh. It is Jerusalem. Her princes are roaring lions, her judges evening wolves, her prophets reckless and her priests profane. Yet the Lord is righteous within her, and has given her every warning.

The Lord's decision is to gather the nations for judgement. But from here the tone of the book turns completely. After the fire comes a promise: the Lord will purify the speech of the peoples, so that all of them may call on his name and serve him together, and worshippers will come even from beyond the rivers of Cush. In Jerusalem he will leave a humble and lowly people who trust in his name.

The last section is a song of joy. Daughter of Zion is told to sing and shout, for the Lord has taken away her punishment, and the King of Israel is among her. The book ends with the Lord himself rejoicing over his people, quieting them with his love, and gathering the scattered home.
