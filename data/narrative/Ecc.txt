=== intro
Ecclesiastes is the voice of a man who has had everything and has thought hard about what any of it means. He speaks as one who has been king in Jerusalem, who built and planted and gathered wealth beyond anyone before him, who tasted every pleasure and studied every kind of wisdom. And his verdict, which opens and closes the book, is as stark as anything in Scripture: futility of futilities, everything is futile.

The speaker is called the Teacher. The Hebrew word is Qoheleth, which comes from a word for an assembly and means the one who gathers people together. The Greek translators rendered it Ecclesiastes, a man of the assembly, and that became the book's name in our Bibles. He describes himself as the son of David, king over Israel in Jerusalem, and Jewish and Christian tradition has always identified him with Solomon, whose wisdom and wealth he echoes at every turn. The book lets him speak simply as the Teacher, without his name.

There are really two voices here. Most of the book is the Teacher speaking in the first person, I saw, I said to myself, I turned to consider. But at the beginning and the end, and once in the middle, another voice speaks about the Teacher, introducing him, summing him up, and finally offering a word to the reader. That framing voice tells us that the Teacher was a genuine wise man who weighed and arranged many proverbs, and it closes the book with its own conclusion.

The word the Teacher keeps returning to is hevel. Its basic meaning is a breath or a vapour, the mist of your breath on a cold morning, there and then gone. The Berean Standard Bible translates it futility; older English versions said vanity. Other modern versions say meaningless. The Teacher uses it of nearly everything: work, pleasure, wealth, wisdom, youth, life itself. Alongside it run two other phrases that set the terms of his search. One is under the sun, the world as it is seen from within, by observation and experience. The other is a pursuit of the wind, the sense of grasping at something that cannot be held.

Ecclesiastes stands next to Proverbs, and it is best heard in conversation with it. Proverbs describes how life generally goes: the diligent prosper, the wicked fall, the wise are honoured. The Teacher does not deny that, but he presses on the cases that will not fit. He sees the righteous perish and the wicked live long. He sees the wise man forgotten as quickly as the fool. And above all he sees death, which comes to the wise and the foolish, the human and the animal alike, and which seems to cancel every gain. Like the book of Job, Ecclesiastes is wisdom that has looked hard at the edges of wisdom.

Yet it is not a book of despair. Woven through it is a second refrain, heard again and again: there is nothing better than to eat and drink and find satisfaction in one's work, and this is the gift of God. The Teacher never stops believing in God. He insists that God has made everything beautiful in its time and set eternity in the human heart, even though no one can fathom what God is doing from beginning to end. His conclusion is that life must be received, with its joys and limits, from a God who cannot be managed or fully understood.

The argument does not move in a straight line, and the listener should not expect a tidy outline. The opening chapters set out the Teacher's great experiment, as he tests pleasure, achievement and wisdom and finds each one wanting. Then come his observations of life in the world, of time, injustice, loneliness, worship and wealth, punctuated by short sayings like those in Proverbs. The later chapters turn more to counsel, how to live wisely when you cannot know what tomorrow will bring. The book ends with a call to the young to rejoice and to remember their Creator, a haunting poem about growing old, and the framing voice's last word: fear God and keep his commandments.

In the Jewish tradition Ecclesiastes is one of the five short scrolls read at the festivals, and it is read at the Feast of Tabernacles, the autumn festival of harvest and of booths. Christian readers have heard it, too, as the honest backdrop to the hope of the New Testament. But the book asks first to be heard on its own terms, as a wise man's searching look at life under the sun.

=== 1
The book opens with a title, the words of the Teacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem, and then at once with the verdict that will echo through everything: futility of futilities, everything is futile.

The Teacher then asks the question that drives the whole book. What does a man gain from all his labour under the sun? Before he answers, he paints the world as he sees it in a short, beautiful poem. Generations come and go. The sun rises, sets and hurries back to rise again. The wind goes round and round. The rivers flow into the sea, and the sea is never full. Everything moves, and nothing arrives, and there is nothing new under the sun.

In the second half of the chapter the Teacher steps forward in his own person. He was king over Israel in Jerusalem, and he set his mind to explore by wisdom all that is done under heaven. His first finding is a sobering one. The more he knows, the more he grieves.

=== 2
Having found that wisdom brings sorrow, the Teacher turns to pleasure, and the opening of this chapter reads like the memoir of a king. He tested himself with wine and laughter. He built houses, planted vineyards, laid out gardens and parks with every kind of fruit tree, and dug reservoirs to water them. He gathered servants, herds and flocks, silver and gold, singers and many concubines. Whatever his eyes desired, he did not deny himself.

Then he looks back over it all, and the verdict comes again: futile, a pursuit of the wind.

He goes on to weigh wisdom against folly once more, and here he faces the hardest fact. Wisdom is better than folly, as light is better than darkness, yet the wise man dies just like the fool, and both are forgotten. Worse, everything he has built must be left to someone who comes after him, wise or foolish. The chapter ends, though, with the first appearance of a refrain that will return throughout the book: nothing is better than to eat and drink and enjoy one's work, and this comes from the hand of God.

=== 3
This chapter opens with the best known passage in the book, a poem about time. To everything there is a season, a time to be born and a time to die, a time to weep and a time to laugh, a time for war and a time for peace. The poem moves in fourteen pairs of opposites, covering the whole range of human experience.

The Teacher then reflects on what the poem means. God has made everything beautiful in its time, and he has set eternity in the human heart, a sense of something beyond the passing moment. Yet no one can fathom what God has done from beginning to end. So the Teacher returns to his refrain: eat, drink and find satisfaction in your work, for this is the gift of God.

The rest of the chapter turns darker. The Teacher has seen wickedness in the very place where justice should be done. And he looks at death, which comes to humans and animals alike. All come from dust, and all return to dust.

=== 4
The Teacher now looks outward, at the life of other people under the sun, and the first thing he sees is oppression. He sees the tears of the oppressed, and twice he notes that there is no one to comfort them. The power is all on the side of their oppressors, and the sight leads him to say the dead are better off than the living.

From there he moves to work, and notices that much human striving springs from envy of one's neighbour. He sketches a man entirely alone, without son or brother, who works without end, never content with his wealth, and who asks at last for whom he toils and why he robs himself of enjoyment. Against that picture comes a warmer passage about companionship: two are better than one, for if one falls the other can lift him up, and a cord of three strands is not quickly broken.

The chapter ends with a small parable about a poor but wise youth who rises from prison to replace a foolish old king, only to be forgotten in his turn.

=== 5
The Teacher turns now to worship, and his tone becomes direct counsel. Guard your steps when you go to the house of God, he says. Go to listen rather than to offer the sacrifice of fools. God is in heaven and you are on earth, so let your words be few. Above all, do not make a vow to God and then fail to keep it.

From the house of God he moves to the world of money and power. In the provinces, one official watches over another, and those above them profit in turn, so the oppression of the poor should come as no surprise. The love of money is never satisfied. The labourer sleeps soundly, while the rich man's abundance keeps him awake. A fortune can be lost in a single failed venture, and in any case a man leaves the world as naked as he came into it.

Yet once again the Teacher comes back to his refrain. To enjoy one's work and possessions, and to accept one's lot, is a gift from God.

=== 6
This short chapter continues the Teacher's reflections on wealth, and it presses on a painful case. Imagine a man to whom God gives riches, possessions and honour, so that he lacks nothing, and yet he is not able to enjoy them. A stranger enjoys them instead. Even if such a man had a hundred children and lived two thousand years, the Teacher says, a stillborn child would be better off.

Appetite is never satisfied, and the Teacher wonders aloud what advantage the wise really have over the fool. The chapter closes with a run of questions that look back over the first half of the book: who knows what is good for a man during the few days of his fleeting life, which passes like a shadow, and who can tell him what will come after him under the sun?

Those questions mark a turning point. From here the Teacher offers more of his own counsel about how to live.

=== 7
The second half of the book opens with a string of sayings in the style of Proverbs, many of them built on the word better. But the Teacher's better sayings are startling. A good name is better than fine perfume, and the day of death better than the day of birth. It is better to go to a house of mourning than to a house of feasting, for death is the end of everyone, and the living should take it to heart.

He goes on to counsel balance in a world he cannot straighten. In good days be glad, in bad days consider that God has made both. He has seen the righteous perish in their righteousness and the wicked live long in their wickedness, and he warns against being too righteous or too wicked.

The chapter ends with the Teacher admitting the limits of his own search. He set out to be wise, but it was beyond him. Here, for a moment, the framing voice breaks in with the words says the Teacher. What he has found is that God made mankind upright, but they have gone looking for many schemes.

=== 8
The Teacher opens this chapter with advice for someone who serves at a king's court. Keep the king's command, because of your oath before God. Do not rush out of his presence or stand firm in a bad cause, for the king's word is supreme. A wise man knows that there is a right time and a right way for everything.

But the Teacher's eye is never only on the court. No one has power over the wind, or over the day of his death. He has seen the wicked, who used to come and go from the holy place, buried and praised in the very city where they did so, and he has seen that when justice is slow, people's hearts are set on doing wrong. He still holds that it will go well with those who fear God, yet he also sees righteous people receiving what the wicked deserve, and the reverse.

So he commends joy again, to eat and drink and be glad, and ends by confessing that no one, not even the wise, can grasp the work of God under the sun.

=== 9
The Teacher has concluded that the righteous and the wise are in God's hands, but no one knows what lies ahead. And there is one fate for everyone, the good and the bad, the one who sacrifices and the one who does not. While there is life there is hope, he says, for a live dog is better than a dead lion.

Out of that comes the book's most urgent call to joy. Go, eat your bread with gladness and drink your wine with a cheerful heart. Keep your clothes white and your head anointed. Enjoy life with the wife you love. Whatever your hand finds to do, do it with all your might, for in Sheol, the realm of the dead, there is no work or planning or knowledge.

Then the Teacher observes that the race is not always to the swift nor the battle to the strong, for time and chance happen to all. The chapter ends with a parable of a small city under siege, saved by the wisdom of a poor man whom no one remembered.

=== 10
Now the Teacher speaks almost entirely in short sayings, closer to the form of Proverbs than anything else in the book, and the first comes from the perfumer's shop: as dead flies make fragrant oil stink, so a little folly outweighs wisdom and honour.

Many of the sayings concern rulers and the dangers of life at court. If a ruler's anger flares against you, stay calm and do not leave your post. The Teacher has seen the world turned upside down, with fools set in high places and slaves on horseback while princes walk on foot. He pities a land whose king is a mere youth and whose princes feast in the morning.

Others come from ordinary work, the man who digs a pit, the quarryman, the woodcutter with a blunt axe, and from the endless talk of fools. The chapter closes with a wry warning: do not curse the king even in your thoughts, for a bird of the air may carry your words.

=== 11
The Teacher begins to draw his reflections towards their close, and the tone becomes one of encouragement. Because no one knows what the future holds, he urges boldness rather than paralysis. Cast your bread upon the waters, and in time you will find it again. Divide your portion among seven, or even eight, for you do not know what disaster may come upon the land. The farmer who waits for perfect weather will never sow or reap, so sow your seed in the morning and keep working in the evening, since you cannot know which will succeed.

Then he turns to life itself. Light is sweet, and it is good to see the sun. However many years a person lives, let him rejoice in them all, while remembering that the days of darkness will be many.

The chapter ends by addressing the young directly. Rejoice in your youth, the Teacher says, follow the ways of your heart, but know that God will bring all these things to judgment. Youth and vigour are fleeting.

=== 12
The Teacher's address to the young runs straight into this final chapter. Remember your Creator in the days of your youth, he says, before the hard days come. What follows is one of the most haunting poems in the Old Testament, a picture of old age and death drawn in images from a great house in decline. The keepers of the house tremble, the grinders stop because they are few, those looking through the windows see dimly, and the sound of the mill fades away. Behind the images lie the failing hands, teeth, eyes and ears of an ageing body.

The poem ends with the silver cord snapped and the dust returning to the ground, and the spirit returning to God who gave it. Then the Teacher's opening words return one last time: futility of futilities, everything is futile.

The book closes with the framing voice. It speaks of the Teacher as a wise man who taught the people and arranged many proverbs, and it addresses the reader as my son. Its conclusion gathers the whole search into a single sentence: fear God and keep his commandments, for God will bring every deed into judgment.
