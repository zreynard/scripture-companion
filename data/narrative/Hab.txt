=== intro
Habakkuk is a prophet who argues with God. Most prophetic books carry the Lord's word to the people. This one begins with the prophet's own complaint to the Lord, and much of it is a conversation between them, honest and urgent, about why God allows evil to go on. It ends with a prayer meant to be sung.

The book gives Habakkuk only his name and his title, the prophet, and tells us nothing of his family, his home or his life. The setting, though, is clear. In the first chapter the Lord announces that he is raising up the Chaldeans, the people of southern Mesopotamia better known to us as the Babylonians.

The listener has just heard Nahum announce the fall of Nineveh. The power that brought it down was Babylon. In six hundred and twenty-six before Christ a Chaldean leader named Nabopolassar seized the throne of Babylon and threw off Assyrian rule. With the Medes as allies, the Babylonians destroyed Nineveh in six hundred and twelve, and in six hundred and five his son Nebuchadnezzar crushed the Egyptian army at Carchemish on the Euphrates. Within a few years the whole of the region, Judah included, lay at Babylon's feet.

Habakkuk spoke in these years, near the end of the seventh century before Christ. Judah was in turmoil. Good King Josiah, who had led a great reform of the nation's worship, had been killed in battle against Pharaoh Neco at Megiddo in six hundred and nine. His son Jehoiakim, placed on the throne by Egypt, was a harsh and grasping ruler, and Jeremiah, who was prophesying in Jerusalem at the same time, condemned him for building his palace by injustice. The violence and corruption Habakkuk complains of in his first lines fit those years well.

The book has a clear and simple shape. Habakkuk cries out about the injustice he sees in Judah, and the Lord answers that he is sending the Babylonians as his instrument of judgement. That answer only troubles the prophet more, for it seems to mean a holy God using a nation more wicked than the one it punishes. Habakkuk takes his stand on the watchtower to wait for a reply, and it comes as a vision to be written down plainly, together with a series of woes on the arrogant conqueror. Finally the prophet prays, recalling the Lord's mighty acts in the past, and resolves to trust him whatever comes.

One short line from the Lord's reply, the righteous will live by faith, would have a long history. The apostle Paul quotes it in his letters to the Romans and the Galatians, and it appears again in the letter to the Hebrews.

=== 1
The book begins with a cry. How long, O Lord, must I call for help, and you do not hear? Habakkuk looks around him in Judah and sees violence, strife and injustice. The law is paralysed, the wicked hem in the righteous, and nothing seems to change. He has prayed for a long time.

The Lord's answer is not the one he expected. Look at the nations, the Lord says, for I am doing something in your days that you would not believe if you were told. He is raising up the Chaldeans, the Babylonians, a ruthless and impetuous nation that sweeps across the earth. They scoff at kings and laugh at fortresses, and their own strength is their god.

That only deepens Habakkuk's distress, and his second complaint follows at once. The Lord's eyes are too pure to look on evil, he says, and yet he is silent while the wicked swallow those more righteous than themselves. He pictures the conqueror as a fisherman dragging nations in his net, and worshipping the net itself.

=== 2
Habakkuk has made his complaint, and now he waits, like a watchman at his post on the city wall, for the Lord's answer.

He is told to write the vision plainly on tablets, so that a herald can run with it. Its fulfilment may seem slow, but it will surely come. Then the Lord contrasts two kinds of people. The proud one, puffed up and never satisfied, gathers nations as greedily as death itself. But the righteous will live by faith, or, as the Hebrew can also be understood, by faithfulness.

The rest of the chapter is a taunt song that the conquered nations will one day sing over the conqueror. It comes in five woes, against the plunderer, the one who builds by unjust gain or with bloodshed, the one who shames his neighbours, and the one who prays to idols of wood and stone. In the middle of the woes stands a promise that the earth will be filled with the knowledge of the Lord's glory, and the chapter ends in silence before him.

=== 3
The book ends with a prayer, and it reads like a psalm. It has a heading with a musical term, Shigionoth, like the Shiggaion of the seventh psalm. It uses the word Selah, familiar from the Psalms, and it closes with a note for the choirmaster.

Habakkuk begins by asking the Lord to renew his great deeds in these years, and in wrath to remember mercy. Then he describes a vision of God coming in power from the south, from Teman and Mount Paran, the wilderness regions associated with Sinai and Edom. The imagery draws on Israel's oldest memories: plague and fever marching with him, Midian trembling, the sea trampled, the sun and moon standing still, as in the days of Moses and Joshua.

The vision leaves the prophet shaking, yet he resolves to wait quietly for the day when distress will fall on the invaders. The book's final lines are his response. Even if the fig tree fails and the fields yield nothing, he will rejoice in the Lord.
