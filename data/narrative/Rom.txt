=== intro
The book of Acts ended with Paul in Rome, a prisoner waiting for his case to be heard by the emperor. The letter to the Romans was written a few years before that, when Paul had never set foot in the city at all. It was a letter of introduction, sent ahead of a visit he had long hoped to make, and it became the fullest account of his gospel that he ever set down.

Paul was a Jew from Tarsus, in what is now southern Turkey, trained in Jerusalem as a Pharisee and once a fierce opponent of the followers of Jesus. On the road to Damascus the risen Jesus met him, and the persecutor became the apostle to the Gentiles. For the better part of twenty years he had carried the message about Jesus through Syria, Asia Minor, Macedonia and Greece, founding small communities of believers in one city after another. By the time of this letter that work in the eastern Mediterranean was, in his own words, complete, and he was looking west, to Rome and beyond it to Spain.

The letter was written from Corinth, during the three months Paul spent in Greece near the end of his third great missionary journey, in the middle fifties of the first century. The clues are in the final chapter. Paul is staying with a man named Gaius, who hosts the whole church. Greetings come from Erastus, the city treasurer. And the woman who carried the letter, Phoebe, serves the church at Cenchrea, the eastern harbour of Corinth. A secretary named Tertius wrote it down as Paul dictated.

The church in Rome was not Paul's. Visitors from Rome were in Jerusalem on the day of Pentecost, and Jewish travellers and traders moved constantly between the capital and the east. The believers met in households scattered across the city, and that they included both Jews and Gentiles. Some years earlier the emperor Claudius had expelled the Jews from Rome, an event Acts mentions in passing, because it brought the Jewish couple Priscilla and Aquila to Corinth, where they became Paul's friends and fellow workers. After Claudius died, many Jews returned. A church that had been, for a time, largely Gentile now had to welcome back Jewish believers, and the letter shows that relations between the two groups were not always easy. Nero was emperor, still in the early and relatively peaceful years of his reign.

Paul had several reasons to write. He wanted the Roman believers to know him and his message before he arrived, and to support his mission to Spain. His teaching had been misrepresented, and some claimed that his gospel of grace encouraged people to sin, so he lays out what he actually proclaims, carefully and at length. He was also about to set off for Jerusalem with a gift of money collected from the Gentile churches of Macedonia and Greece for the poor believers in Judea, and he was anxious about how it would be received. Behind all of these lies one great question: how Jews and Gentiles belong together in the one people of God, and what has become of God's promises to Israel now that the Messiah has come and so many of Israel have not believed in him.

The letter moves like a sustained argument. Paul begins by showing that the whole human race, Gentile and Jew alike, stands guilty before God. He then announces that God has provided a righteousness of his own, received through faith in Jesus, and he calls Abraham as his witness. From there he traces what this means: peace with God, freedom from the reign of sin and death, life in the Spirit, and a hope that nothing in all creation can take away. In the central chapters he wrestles with the unbelief of his own people and the faithfulness of God. Only then does he turn to how the believers in Rome should live, toward one another, toward the authorities, and across their differences over food and holy days. He closes with his travel plans and a long, warm list of greetings.

Paul did reach Jerusalem, and there he was arrested. When he finally came to Rome, about three years later, it was in chains. Believers from the city, the church to which this letter had been read aloud, came out along the Appian Way to meet him.

=== 1
Paul opens in the formal style of an ancient letter, writer first and then recipients, but he stretches the opening greeting into a compact summary of his message. He is a servant of Christ Jesus, called to be an apostle, and the gospel he serves was promised long ago through the prophets. It concerns God's Son, a descendant of David, declared with power to be the Son of God by his resurrection.

He then turns to the believers in Rome personally. He thanks God that their faith is spoken of everywhere, and he explains that he has often planned to come to them and been prevented. He is eager to preach in Rome too, because he is not ashamed of the gospel. It is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes, the Jew first and also the Greek, and in it the righteousness of God is revealed.

Then the tone changes sharply. Before Paul can explain that good news, he sets out the problem it answers, and he begins with the Gentile world, which knew enough of God from creation to honour him and turned instead to idols. The picture that follows is dark, and deliberately so.

=== 2
Paul has just described the Gentile world turning from God to idols and every kind of wrongdoing. Now he imagines someone listening with approval, and he turns to address that person directly. Anyone who passes judgment on others while doing the same things, he says, has no excuse.

This was a familiar style of teaching in the ancient world, in which a speaker addresses an imaginary conversation partner, raises objections and answers them. Paul uses it often in Romans. At first the listener is simply anyone who judges. As the chapter goes on, it becomes clear that he has in mind a fellow Jew, someone confident in possessing the law of Moses, proud of circumcision, and sure of being a guide to the blind.

Paul's point is that God shows no favouritism. He will judge every person according to what they have done, and having the law is not the same as keeping it. The chapter ends with a redefinition that would have startled many of his first hearers, of what it truly means to be a Jew.

=== 3
Paul has been pressing his fellow Jews hard. Possessing the law and bearing the mark of circumcision, he has said, does not protect anyone from God's judgment. That raises an obvious objection, and the chapter opens with it. Is there any advantage in being a Jew at all?

Paul's answer is yes, much in every way, above all because Israel was entrusted with the very words of God. But he refuses to let that answer become a shelter. In a quick exchange of questions, some of them echoing what his critics have said about him, he insists that God remains faithful and just even when people are not. Then he gathers a chain of quotations from the Psalms and Isaiah to bring his whole case to its conclusion: Jews and Gentiles alike are under sin, and no one will be declared righteous by keeping the law.

Then comes the turn the letter has been waiting for, marked by two words: but now. In a dense and carefully built passage, Paul announces what God has done in Christ Jesus to answer the problem he has spent nearly three chapters describing.

=== 4
Justified by faith, apart from the works of the law, and on the same terms for Jew and Gentile: that was the claim at the close of chapter three. A Jewish reader would naturally ask how this fits with Israel's own story, and especially with Abraham, the father of the nation.

So Paul turns to Genesis, and to the sentence from chapter fifteen that stands at the heart of his argument: Abraham believed God, and it was credited to him as righteousness. He adds a word from David in the Psalms, about the blessing of the one whose sins are forgiven. Then he makes an observation about timing. Abraham was counted righteous before he was circumcised, years before the sign of the covenant was given. That makes him the father of uncircumcised believers as well as circumcised ones.

The chapter ends with a portrait of Abraham's faith: a man of about a hundred, with a wife long past childbearing, holding on to God's promise that he would become the father of many nations.

=== 5
Paul has finished his case. Every human being stands guilty before God, and God has provided righteousness as a gift, received by faith, as it was for Abraham. Now he begins to explore what follows from it.

The first thing is peace with God. Those who have been justified now stand in grace, and they can face even suffering with hope, because God has poured his love into their hearts through the Holy Spirit. Paul then reflects on what kind of love this is, shown to people while they were still sinners and enemies.

In the second half of the chapter Paul widens the view to the whole of human history. He goes back to Adam, the first man, through whom sin and death entered the world. He sets Adam and Christ side by side, two men whose single acts have consequences for all who belong to them. The comparison is close and carefully argued, and it closes with a sentence that will provoke the question of the next chapter: where sin increased, grace increased all the more.

=== 6
The last chapter closed with a bold sentence: where sin increased, grace increased all the more. Paul knows how that can be heard, and indeed he has been accused of teaching it. If grace is so abundant, why not go on sinning, so that there is even more of it?

His answer draws on baptism, the rite by which every believer in Rome had entered the church. To be baptised into Christ, Paul says, is to be joined to his death and to share in his new life. A person who has died with Christ cannot simply carry on living in the old way.

In the second half of the chapter he changes the picture. Everyone in Rome knew what slavery was; a large part of the city's population were slaves or former slaves. Paul uses that everyday reality, apologising as he does so for speaking in such human terms. Everyone serves some master, he says, and he contrasts the two masters a person may serve, and the wages each one pays.

=== 7
Believers have died with Christ, Paul has said, and been set free from slavery to sin. Now he turns to a related question that has hovered over the whole letter: what about the law of Moses?

He begins with an illustration from marriage, aimed at those who know the law. A woman is bound to her husband only as long as he lives; once he has died, she is free to marry another. In the same way, believers have died to the law through the body of Christ, so that they may belong to the one who was raised from the dead.

That raises another question. If the law stirred up sin, is the law itself sinful? Paul answers with an emphatic no, and then speaks in the first person, of a struggle between wanting to do good and failing to do it. He delights in God's law, yet finds himself doing the very thing he hates. The chapter ends with a cry of anguish, followed at once by thanks to God.

=== 8
The previous chapter ended with a cry: who will rescue me from this body of death? Paul's answer opens this chapter. There is now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus.

This chapter is one of the high points of the letter. Paul has said little about the Holy Spirit so far; now he speaks of the Spirit again and again. The Spirit gives life where the law could not. The Spirit makes believers children of God, able to call God Abba, the Aramaic word a child would use for father. And the Spirit is the first instalment of a future that reaches beyond them to the whole creation, which Paul pictures groaning as if in childbirth while it waits to be set free.

Paul does not pretend that the present is easy. His readers face suffering, and he names it honestly. But the chapter builds steadily toward its close, a series of questions and answers that ends in one of the most famous passages Paul ever wrote, about the love of God in Christ, from which nothing in all creation can separate those who belong to him.

=== 9
Chapter eight closed in confidence: nothing in all creation can separate God's people from his love. But that confidence raises a painful question, and it brings him to a sudden change of tone. If God's promises are so sure, what has happened to Israel? Most of Paul's own people have not believed that Jesus is the Messiah.

This chapter begins a section of three chapters that answers a question the letter has raised since its opening lines, where the gospel came to the Jew first. Paul opens with grief, and a list of everything that belongs to Israel: the covenants, the law, the temple worship, the patriarchs, and the Messiah himself.

His first answer is that God's word has not failed. He traces Israel's history through Isaac rather than Ishmael, Jacob rather than Esau, and God's dealings with Pharaoh, to show that God's purposes have always moved by his own choosing and mercy. The argument is demanding, and Paul expects objections. He ends with Gentiles who have found righteousness by faith, and Israel stumbling over the stone laid in Zion.

=== 10
Paul is in the middle of his reflection on Israel's unbelief. He has argued that God's word has not failed, and that Israel stumbled because it pursued righteousness by works rather than by faith. Now he begins again with his own heart: his deepest desire and prayer is that his people may be saved.

He grants that they are zealous for God, but says their zeal was not based on knowledge. At the centre of the chapter he takes words of Moses from Deuteronomy, about God's command being near, in the mouth and in the heart, and he reads them as a description of the message of faith now being proclaimed. Anyone who confesses Jesus as Lord and believes that God raised him from the dead will be saved, and in this there is no difference between Jew and Greek.

The second half follows the message as it goes out to be heard. Paul anticipates the excuses that might be made for Israel, that they never heard or never understood, and answers each one with Scripture, from Moses, Isaiah and the Psalms. The chapter ends with God holding out his hands all day long to a disobedient people.

=== 11
Chapter ten ended with God holding out his hands to a disobedient people. The natural question follows, and Paul asks it himself: has God rejected his people? His answer is immediate. Certainly not. Paul himself is an Israelite, of the tribe of Benjamin.

He recalls Elijah, who once thought he was the only faithful one left in Israel, and was told that God had kept seven thousand who had not bowed to Baal. So now, Paul says, there is a remnant chosen by grace. And Israel's stumbling is not the end of the story. Through it salvation has come to the Gentiles, and Paul expects that it will one day provoke Israel to return.

Then he speaks directly to the Gentile believers in Rome. Using the image of an olive tree, he warns them against arrogance toward the Jewish people. Some natural branches have been broken off, and wild branches grafted in, but the root still holds them all. Paul then speaks of a mystery about Israel's future, and the chapter ends in a hymn of praise to the unsearchable wisdom of God, which closes the whole first part of the letter.

=== 12
With the hymn of praise at the end of chapter eleven, the great argument of the letter is complete. For eleven chapters Paul has set out what God has done in Christ, for Jew and Gentile alike. Now, with a single word, therefore, he turns to how the believers in Rome are to live in response.

The appeal is grounded in what came before. In view of God's mercy, Paul urges them to offer themselves to God as living sacrifices, a striking phrase for people who knew animal sacrifices from both the temple in Jerusalem and the pagan altars of Rome. He asks for a renewed way of thinking, no longer shaped by the pattern of the present age.

Much of the chapter concerns the life of the community. Paul uses the picture of a body with many members, each with its own gifts, to describe how the believers belong to one another. Then comes a rapid series of short instructions about love, hospitality and humility, which extends even to how they should treat those who persecute them.

=== 13
The last chapter described life within the community of believers, and ended with how they should treat enemies and persecutors. Now Paul turns outward, to their relationship with the governing authorities.

For readers living in the imperial capital, this was no abstract question. The authorities were close at hand: the officials, the tax collectors, the soldiers of the emperor. Some years earlier imperial power had driven the Jews out of the city. Nero was now on the throne, and his reign had not yet turned to the persecution of Christians that would come within a decade. Paul tells the believers to submit to the authorities as servants appointed by God, and to pay their taxes and give respect where it is due.

From what they owe the state he moves to the one debt that can never be fully paid, the debt of love, which sums up the commandments. The chapter closes with a sense of urgency. The night is nearly over and the day is near, and Paul calls the Roman believers to wake up and live as people of the daylight.

=== 14
After the broad instructions of the last two chapters, Paul takes up a specific problem among the believers in Rome.

Some ate only vegetables, while others ate anything. Some regarded certain days as special, while others treated every day alike. Paul does not explain the background. With Jewish and Gentile believers sharing meals in the same households, these differences were more than private matters.

Paul calls the more scrupulous believers the weak, and the others, among whom he counts himself, the strong. His concern is not to settle every question but to stop them judging and despising one another, because each one belongs to the same Lord and will answer to him.

=== 15
The disagreement between the strong and the weak over food and holy days runs on into this chapter. Paul opens by including himself among the strong and telling them to bear with the weak, as Christ did not please himself. He then gathers a series of passages from the Law, the Prophets and the Psalms, all of which speak of the Gentiles praising God alongside his people. It is a fitting close to a letter so concerned with Jew and Gentile together.

Then Paul turns to his own plans, and the letter becomes personal again. He describes his work so far, from Jerusalem all the way around to Illyricum, on the Adriatic coast, and his ambition to preach where Christ has not yet been named. Now he means to go to Spain, visiting Rome on the way.

First, though, he must go to Jerusalem, carrying the gift collected by the churches of Macedonia and Achaia, the province of southern Greece, for the poor believers there. He asks the Romans to pray that he will be rescued from unbelievers in Judea and that the gift will be accepted. Acts tells us how those prayers were answered: Paul did reach Jerusalem, and there he was arrested.

=== 16
The argument and the travel plans are complete, and Paul ends with people. The final chapter is one of the longest lists of names in the New Testament, and it offers a rare glimpse of an early church.

First comes Phoebe, a servant of the church at Cenchrea, the eastern port of Corinth. She is carrying the letter, and Paul asks the Romans to welcome and help her. Then come greetings to some two dozen people by name, and to households and house churches besides. Among them are Prisca and Aquila, the couple known in Acts as Priscilla and Aquila, who had worked alongside Paul in Corinth and Ephesus and had now returned to Rome, with a church meeting in their house. The list includes women and men, Jews and Gentiles, and a number of names common among slaves and freed slaves.

Paul adds a brief warning about those who cause divisions. Then greetings come from those with him in Corinth, including Timothy, his host Gaius, Erastus the city treasurer, and Tertius, who adds his own line as the one writing the letter down. It closes with a final ascription of glory to God.
