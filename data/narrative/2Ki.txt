=== intro
Second Kings opens with a king of Israel lying injured in his palace in Samaria, after falling through the lattice of an upper room. He sends messengers to ask a foreign god whether he will recover, and on the road they meet an old prophet in a leather belt. It is the last story of Elijah, and almost at once his mantle passes to his successor, Elisha. By the end of the book, some three centuries later, both kingdoms will be gone, Jerusalem and its temple will lie in ruins, and a descendant of David will be eating at the table of the king of Babylon.

First and Second Kings were originally a single work, so this book carries straight on from the last. Ahab has died in battle at Ramoth-gilead, and his son Ahaziah is on the throne of Israel. In Judah, Jehoshaphat's long and largely faithful reign is drawing to an end. The two royal houses are allied by marriage, a bond that will bring the worship of Baal into Jerusalem itself. As before, the writer moves between the two kingdoms, dating each reign by the other, and judging each king by his faithfulness to the Lord. The listener should be warned that the names become confusing. For a time there is a Jehoram, also called Joram, on both thrones at once, and an Ahaziah in each kingdom too.

The first part of the book belongs to Elisha. His ministry runs through the reigns of several kings of Israel, and the stories about him are some of the most vivid in the Old Testament: a widow's oil, a Shunammite woman's son, a Syrian general healed in the Jordan, an army struck blind, a besieged city fed overnight. Behind them lies a long struggle with the Arameans of Damascus, first under Ben-hadad and then under Hazael, who repeatedly invade Israel. At the centre of these chapters is a violent revolution. Jehu, a commander of Israel's army, is anointed at Elisha's command, and he wipes out the house of Ahab and the worship of Baal in the north. In Judah, Ahab's daughter Athaliah seizes the throne and tries to destroy the line of David, until a hidden infant prince is brought out of the temple and crowned.

The middle chapters move faster, through a long line of kings. In the eighth century before Christ the northern kingdom enjoys a final season of prosperity under Jeroboam the second, and it was in his days that the prophets Amos and Hosea spoke in the north. Then a far greater power arrives. Assyria, from its heartland on the Tigris in what is now northern Iraq, begins to march west under a series of formidable kings. Israel's last decades are a chaos of assassinations and shifting alliances, and in chapter seventeen Samaria falls. The people of the northern kingdom are deported and scattered, and foreign settlers are brought in to take their place. The writer pauses there for his longest reflection on why it happened.

Judah survives for more than a century longer. Its story now centres on a handful of kings. Hezekiah, a reformer, trusts the Lord when the Assyrian king Sennacherib surrounds Jerusalem, and the prophet Isaiah stands beside him. His son Manasseh reigns longer than any other king of Judah and undoes everything his father did. Josiah, who came to the throne as a boy of eight, repairs the temple, where the book of the law is found, and leads the most thorough reform in Judah's history. The prophet Jeremiah began his work in Josiah's reign, and much of his book belongs in the final chapters here.

By then Assyria itself has fallen, and Babylon has taken its place. Josiah dies in battle, and his sons and grandson are caught between Egypt and Babylon. Nebuchadnezzar, king of Babylon, takes Jerusalem in two stages. First the young king Jehoiachin is carried off with the leading citizens, among them the priest Ezekiel. About ten years later, after the last king, Zedekiah, rebels, the city is besieged, the temple is burned and the walls are broken down.

The book does not end in silence, though. Its final paragraph moves forward to the thirty-seventh year of exile, when a new king of Babylon releases Jehoiachin from prison and gives him a place at his table. The line of David has not been extinguished. The books of Chronicles retell much of this history from another angle, and the story of the return from exile is taken up in Ezra and Nehemiah. Many of the prophetic books, from Amos to Jeremiah, belong inside the years this book describes.

=== 1
Ahab is dead, killed by an arrow at Ramoth-gilead, and his son Ahaziah now rules Israel from Samaria. The chapter opens with a brief note that Moab, the kingdom east of the Dead Sea, has seized the moment to throw off Israel's rule. That rebellion will be taken up again in chapter three.

Ahaziah has fallen through the lattice of an upper room and is badly hurt. He sends messengers to consult Baal-zebub, the god of the Philistine city of Ekron, near the coast, to ask whether he will recover. The name means lord of the flies, and it may be a mocking Israelite twist on a title of Baal. For a king of Israel, the choice says everything.

The messengers never reach Ekron. They are met on the road by a man whose description the king recognises at once, and the rest of the chapter is a confrontation between the king and the prophet in which the king sends soldiers three times. It is the last time we see Elijah confronting the house of Ahab. The chapter ends with a succession, and with the first of the book's confusing names: Ahaziah's brother Jehoram takes the throne of Israel, while another Jehoram, the son of Jehoshaphat, is reigning in Judah.

=== 2
This chapter belongs to the hand-over between two prophets. It opens by telling the listener what is about to happen: the Lord is going to take Elijah up to heaven in a whirlwind. Elisha, the farmer Elijah called from the plough years before, has been his servant ever since, and everyone seems to know that today is the day.

Elijah sets out on a last journey, from Gilgal to Bethel, then down to Jericho, and finally to the Jordan. At each stop he tells Elisha to stay behind, and at each stop Elisha refuses. Along the way we meet the sons of the prophets, communities of prophets living together at Bethel and Jericho, who will appear often in the Elisha stories. At the river, Elijah's cloak plays the part that Moses' staff once played at the Red Sea, and the two men cross on dry ground, into the country where Moses himself was taken from Israel. Elisha's request on the far bank is for a double portion of his master's spirit, the inheritance of a firstborn son.

What happens next made Elijah a figure of expectation for centuries; the prophet Malachi spoke of his return, and the Gospels take up that hope. The rest of the chapter shows Elisha beginning his own work, at Jericho and at Bethel, with the same authority, and the same severity, as his master.

=== 3
Elisha has begun his ministry, and the writer now turns to the reign of Jehoram son of Ahab, king of Israel, also called Joram. He is not as bad as his parents, Ahab and Jezebel, and he removes the pillar of Baal his father set up, but he keeps Jeroboam's calves.

The chapter takes up the rebellion of Moab mentioned at the start of chapter one. Moab lay on the high plateau east of the Dead Sea, and its king, Mesha, had been paying Israel a heavy tribute in lambs and wool. Remarkably, Mesha's own account of his revolt has survived. A black basalt stone found in the nineteenth century, now known as the Moabite Stone, records his victory over Israel and even names Omri.

Jehoram sets out to bring Moab back under control. He calls on Jehoshaphat of Judah, who answers in the same words he once used to Ahab, and the king of Edom, a land then under Judah's control. The three armies march by a long route around the southern end of the Dead Sea, and in the desert their water runs out. Jehoshaphat asks, as he did before Ramoth-gilead, whether there is a prophet of the Lord nearby. There is: Elisha. The chapter ends on the walls of the Moabite city of Kir-haraseth, with an act that ends the campaign.

=== 4
The battles of chapter three are left behind, and the book now gathers a series of stories about Elisha's ministry among ordinary people. They are not necessarily in the order they happened, and the kings fade into the background. Here the prophet's concern is with widows, children and hungry communities.

The first story concerns the widow of one of the sons of the prophets, whose creditor is about to take her two sons as slaves to pay off a debt, as the law of the time allowed. The second, and the longest, takes us to Shunem, a village in the Jezreel valley in the north. A wealthy woman there has noticed the prophet passing by, and she and her husband build him a small room on their roof. We also meet Elisha's servant Gehazi, who will appear in several stories to come. The Shunammite woman will return later in the book, so her story is worth following closely.

The chapter ends with two shorter episodes during a famine, at Gilgal among the prophets. One involves a pot of stew, and the other twenty loaves of barley bread and a hundred hungry men. Readers of the Gospels will hear an echo of that last story in the feeding of the crowds by Jesus.

=== 5
Elisha's fame has spread, even beyond Israel's borders. This chapter takes us first to Damascus, the capital of Aram, Israel's great rival to the north-east. Its army is commanded by Naaman, a celebrated soldier and a favourite of the king, and the writer makes a striking remark: it was through him that the Lord had given Aram victory. But Naaman has a skin disease, the kind the Bible calls leprosy, which could make a man an outcast.

The story turns on someone with no name and no power at all, an Israelite girl captured in a border raid who now serves Naaman's wife. She knows of a prophet in Samaria. What follows involves two kings, a diplomatic letter that causes alarm in Israel, and a great man arriving with horses and chariots at the door of Elisha's house, expecting to be received with ceremony.

Naaman's journey ends at the river Jordan, but the chapter does not end there. Elisha's servant Gehazi, whom we met at Shunem, has his own ideas about Naaman's gifts. Centuries later, in the synagogue at Nazareth, Jesus would remind his hearers that there were many lepers in Israel in Elisha's day, yet the one who was cleansed was Naaman the Syrian.

=== 6
After the story of Naaman, the writer returns briefly to the community of prophets gathered around Elisha. They have outgrown their meeting place and go down to the Jordan to cut timber for a new one. A small accident with a borrowed axe head shows the prophet's care for a poor man's loss.

Then the scene widens again to the war between Israel and Aram, which in these years took the form of constant raids across the border. The king of Israel is not named in these stories, but it is probably Jehoram son of Ahab. The king of Aram finds that his plans are known in Samaria before he can carry them out, and he discovers why. He sends a force to capture Elisha at Dothan, a town on the road north of Samaria, the place where, long ago, Joseph's brothers sold him. Elisha's frightened servant is about to see something hidden from ordinary eyes.

The last part of the chapter, which runs straight on into the next, takes place in very different circumstances. Ben-hadad, king of Aram, brings his whole army to besiege Samaria, and the famine inside the walls becomes desperate. The king of Israel, walking the wall in sackcloth, hears something that drives him to blame Elisha, and he sends a man to take his life.

=== 7
Samaria is starving under the Aramean siege, and the king has just sent a messenger to Elisha's house, blaming the Lord, and the prophet, for the disaster. This chapter follows straight on from the last, and it opens with Elisha's answer.

He announces that by this time tomorrow, food will be sold cheaply at the gate of Samaria. The royal officer on whose arm the king leans mocks the very idea, and Elisha gives him a personal word of his own.

Then the story moves outside the city walls, to four men with leprosy sitting at the gate. Barred from the city by their disease and starving like everyone else, they decide they have nothing left to lose by walking over to the enemy camp. They set out at twilight. What they find, and what they decide to do about it, brings the siege to an end in a way no one in Samaria could have planned. The chapter closes by returning to the officer at the gate, and the writer takes care to show that every part of Elisha's word came true.

=== 8
The chapter opens with a story that seems to belong to an earlier time. The woman of Shunem, whose son Elisha raised to life, was warned by the prophet of a coming seven-year famine and left the country to live among the Philistines. Now she has returned to find her house and land taken over, and she comes to the king to appeal. She arrives just as Gehazi is telling the king about Elisha's deeds.

Then the scene shifts to Damascus. Years before, at Mount Horeb, the Lord told Elijah to anoint Hazael as king of Aram. Now Elisha goes to Damascus, where Ben-hadad the king lies ill, and Hazael comes to meet him with a question from his master. Elisha's answer, and what he sees in Hazael's face, are among the most haunting moments in his story. Assyrian records call Hazael the son of a nobody.

The last part of the chapter returns to Judah. Jehoram son of Jehoshaphat now reigns in Jerusalem, and he has married a daughter of Ahab. Her name is Athaliah. Their son Ahaziah succeeds him, and joins his uncle, Joram of Israel, in another war against Aram at Ramoth-gilead, the same town where Ahab died. Joram is wounded and goes to recover at Jezreel, and Ahaziah goes to visit him. Both kings of the house of Ahab are now in one place.

=== 9
Two kings are at Jezreel: Joram son of Ahab, king of Israel, recovering from wounds taken in battle with the Arameans, and his nephew Ahaziah, king of Judah, who has come to visit him. Israel's army remains in the field at Ramoth-gilead, east of the Jordan, under its commanders. One of them is Jehu son of Nimshi.

Years earlier, at Horeb, Elijah was told to anoint Jehu as king of Israel. Elijah never did it himself. Now Elisha sends a young man from the company of prophets with a flask of oil and very precise instructions, including the instruction to run as soon as he has finished. Jehu's fellow officers respond at once.

What follows is told at a gallop, and the narrator lets Jehu's furious chariot driving set the pace. From the watchtower at Jezreel the approaching troops are seen in the distance, and messengers ride out to ask the same question again and again: is it peace? The confrontation that ends the reign takes place, of all places, on the plot of ground that once belonged to Naboth, the man Ahab and Jezebel had killed for his vineyard, and the words Elijah spoke against them there are remembered. The chapter's final scene belongs to Jezebel herself, looking down from a palace window.

=== 10
Joram, Ahaziah and Jezebel are dead, and Jehu holds Jezreel. But the house of Ahab is large. Seventy of Ahab's descendants are being brought up in Samaria, the capital, under the care of its leading men, and any of them could become a rallying point against the new king.

Jehu moves against them by letter, and then against everyone connected with Ahab's rule, and even against a party of Ahaziah's relatives from Judah whom he meets on the road. Along the way he picks up an ally, Jehonadab son of Rechab, the founder of a strict and austere clan who will reappear generations later in the book of Jeremiah. Then Jehu turns to the worship of Baal itself, which Ahab and Jezebel brought into Israel, with a plan that depends on deception.

The writer's final verdict on Jehu is mixed. He destroys Baal worship in Israel, and the Lord promises his descendants the throne for four generations, but he keeps the golden calves of Jeroboam. A century later the prophet Hosea would speak of the blood shed at Jezreel as a debt still owed. Assyrian records add a detail the Bible does not give: a black stone monument of Shalmaneser shows Jehu, or his envoy, bowing low with tribute. Meanwhile, in the east, Hazael of Damascus begins to take Israel's lands beyond the Jordan.

=== 11
Jehu's revolution has left the throne of Judah empty, for Ahaziah king of Judah was killed in the same purge. His mother, Athaliah, the daughter of Ahab, is now in Jerusalem, and she sees her chance. She sets out to destroy the entire royal family, her own grandchildren among them, and takes the throne for herself. For six years Judah is ruled by a queen from the house of Ahab, the only time in its history that a ruler not descended from David sat on the throne.

But one child escapes. Ahaziah's sister Jehosheba rescues his infant son Joash and hides him, with his nurse, in the house of the Lord. The book of Chronicles tells us that she was the wife of Jehoiada, the chief priest, which explains how the boy could be kept hidden within the temple precincts for so long.

In the seventh year, Jehoiada acts. He takes the captains of the royal guard into his confidence and lays his plans carefully around the changing of the guard on the Sabbath. The chapter tells of the day the hidden prince is brought out, with David's own spears and shields in the hands of the guard. The promise of a lamp for David in Jerusalem has come down, for a time, to a single child of seven.

=== 12
Joash, the boy who was hidden in the temple, has been crowned at the age of seven and is now king of Judah. He will reign for forty years. The writer's verdict on him comes with a careful qualification: he did what was right in the eyes of the Lord all the days that Jehoiada the priest instructed him.

The main business of his reign, as Kings tells it, is the repair of the temple, which had fallen into disrepair and, Chronicles tells us, had been plundered by Athaliah's sons for the worship of Baal. Joash orders the priests to use the temple's income for the work, but years pass and nothing is done. The solution, in the twenty-third year of his reign, is a simple collection chest with a hole bored in the lid, set beside the altar.

The end of the reign is darker. Hazael of Damascus, who has already taken Israel's lands east of the Jordan, captures the Philistine city of Gath and then turns toward Jerusalem, and Joash buys him off with the temple's treasures. The book of Chronicles tells more of these later years, including the stoning of Jehoiada's son Zechariah after the old priest's death. Kings notes only that Joash's own servants conspired against him and killed him.

=== 13
The story crosses back to the northern kingdom, where Jehu's son Jehoahaz now reigns in Samaria. These are dark years for Israel. Hazael of Damascus, whose future cruelty Elisha foresaw with tears, presses Israel so hard that its army is reduced to a remnant, like dust at threshing time. When Jehoahaz seeks the Lord, a deliverer is sent, though the writer does not say who it was.

Jehoahaz is followed by his son Jehoash. The names are tangled again here: Jehoash of Israel, also called Joash, reigns at the same time as Joash of Judah, the boy king of chapter eleven, now grown old. The writer summarises Jehoash's reign almost at once, and then steps back to tell one more story from it.

Elisha is dying. He has been a prophet for more than forty years, through the reigns of Joram, Jehu, Jehoahaz and now Jehoash, though he has not appeared in the story since Jehu's anointing. The young king comes to his bedside and weeps, crying out the same words Elisha himself once cried as Elijah was taken up: my father, my father, the chariots and horsemen of Israel. The prophet's last act involves a bow, some arrows and an open window facing east, toward Aram. Even after his burial, the writer has one more remarkable story to tell about Elisha's tomb.

=== 14
The book returns to Judah, where Amaziah, the son of Joash, takes the throne. He is judged to have done right, though not like David. He punishes his father's murderers, sparing their children as the law of Moses required, and he wins a victory over Edom in the south.

Flushed with success, he challenges Jehoash, king of Israel, who replies with a mocking fable about a thistle and a cedar. Amaziah will not listen, and the battle at Beth-shemesh, in the low hills west of Jerusalem, ends in a humiliation for Judah that reaches the walls and the treasures of Jerusalem itself. Amaziah later dies, like his father, at the hands of conspirators.

The chapter closes in the north with the long reign of Jeroboam son of Jehoash, often called Jeroboam the second. For forty-one years he reigns in Samaria, and with Damascus weakened and Assyria not yet pressing west, he restores Israel's borders almost to their extent in Solomon's day. The writer notes that this was foretold by a prophet named Jonah son of Amittai, from Gath-hepher in Galilee, the same Jonah whose book tells of his mission to Nineveh. Jeroboam's reign was a time of wealth for some and injustice for many, and it was in these years that Amos and Hosea delivered their warnings to the north.

=== 15
This chapter covers half a century and a long line of kings at a rapid pace. In Judah, there is stability. Azariah, also called Uzziah, reigns for fifty-two years, and his son Jotham after him. Azariah is struck with a skin disease, and for his last years Jotham governs on his behalf. It was in the year King Uzziah died, the book of Isaiah tells us, that Isaiah saw the Lord in the temple and received his call.

In Israel, it is the opposite. After the death of Jeroboam the second, the northern kingdom slides into chaos. His son Zechariah reigns six months before he is assassinated, and with him Jehu's dynasty ends, after the four generations promised. Shallum lasts one month. Menahem seizes power with great brutality, Pekahiah his son is murdered by one of his own officers, Pekah, and Pekah himself is killed by Hoshea. Four of Israel's last six kings die by assassination. The prophet Hosea, speaking in these years, complains that Israel sets up kings without the Lord's consent.

Over all this falls the shadow of Assyria. Its king Tiglath-pileser, here first called Pul, appears in the land, and Menahem pays him a vast sum to keep his throne. Under Pekah, Tiglath-pileser returns, takes Galilee and Gilead, and deports their people. The chapter closes with a warning: Rezin of Damascus and Pekah of Israel have begun to move against Judah.

=== 16
Jotham has died, and his son Ahaz reigns in Jerusalem. He is the first king of Judah since the days of Athaliah to be judged without any qualification as doing wrong, and the writer records that he even offers his own son in the fire, a practice of the surrounding nations.

The threat at the end of chapter fifteen now arrives. Rezin, king of Aram, and Pekah, king of Israel, are allied against Assyria, and they march on Jerusalem, probably to force Judah into their alliance or to put a king of their own choosing on its throne. The book of Isaiah, in its seventh chapter, describes the same crisis from inside the city: the king's heart shaking like trees in the wind, and the prophet sent to meet him with a sign and a call to trust the Lord.

Ahaz chooses another course. He sends silver and gold to Tiglath-pileser, king of Assyria, with a message calling himself the Assyrian king's servant and son. Assyria answers by capturing Damascus and ending its kingdom. When Ahaz goes to Damascus to meet his new overlord, he sees an altar there, and the rest of the chapter describes the changes he makes to the temple in Jerusalem on his return. Among the things he takes apart are the bronze stands and the great Sea that Solomon's craftsman made.

=== 17
Damascus has fallen to Assyria, and Galilee and Gilead have been stripped from Israel. What remains of the northern kingdom is little more than the hill country around Samaria, ruled by Hoshea, the man who murdered Pekah. He will be Israel's last king.

Assyria is now ruled by Shalmaneser, the son of Tiglath-pileser, and Hoshea becomes his vassal, paying tribute. Then he gambles on rebellion, sending envoys to a king of Egypt named So, whose identity is still debated, and withholding the tribute. Assyria's response is swift. The siege of Samaria lasts three years, and when the city falls, around seven hundred and twenty-two years before Christ, its people are carried away to distant parts of the Assyrian empire, in northern Mesopotamia and the mountains of the Medes. Assyrian records credit the final conquest to Shalmaneser's successor, Sargon.

The writer then does something he has done nowhere else in Kings. He stops the narrative to explain, at length, why this happened, gathering up the warnings of the prophets and the long history of the sins of Jeroboam. The final part of the chapter describes the peoples Assyria brought in from elsewhere to resettle the land, and the mixed worship that grew up among them. Their descendants, the Samaritans, will still be living in these hills in the time of the Gospels, and still regarded with suspicion in Jerusalem.

=== 18
With the northern kingdom gone, the rest of Second Kings belongs to Judah alone. It begins with one of its finest kings. Hezekiah, the son of Ahaz, comes to the throne in Jerusalem, and the writer's praise for him is unusually warm: no king of Judah was like him in his trust in the Lord. He removes the high places that earlier good kings left standing, and even breaks up the bronze snake Moses made in the wilderness, to which the people have been burning incense.

Hezekiah also rebels against Assyria, refusing to pay the tribute his father agreed. The fall of Samaria, retold briefly here from Jerusalem's side of the border, shows what that could mean. In the fourteenth year of his reign, about seven hundred and one years before Christ, the Assyrian king Sennacherib invades Judah and takes its fortified towns one by one. Sennacherib's own annals boast of shutting Hezekiah up in Jerusalem like a bird in a cage, and carved stone panels from his palace at Nineveh, now in the British Museum, show the siege of Lachish, Judah's second city.

Hezekiah pays a heavy price to end the invasion, but the Assyrians come anyway. Three high officials arrive outside Jerusalem, and their spokesman, the Rabshakeh, addresses the city in Hebrew, so that the people on the wall will understand. His speech is a masterpiece of intimidation.

=== 19
The Rabshakeh's words have been reported to Hezekiah, and his officials stand before him with their clothes torn. The king's response is to go into the house of the Lord, and to send for the prophet Isaiah son of Amoz. Isaiah has been speaking to the kings of Judah since the year of Uzziah's death, and the book that bears his name contains these same events, told in almost the same words.

The Assyrian army is still at work elsewhere in Judah, and a new danger appears from the south: an Egyptian army led by Tirhakah, of the Cushite line of pharaohs from the upper Nile. Sennacherib sends Hezekiah a letter, repeating his challenge. Hezekiah takes the letter into the temple, spreads it out before the Lord, and prays.

Isaiah's answer, much of it in poetry, is addressed not only to Hezekiah but to the Assyrian king himself, and it answers his boasts with the Lord's own words. The chapter ends with Jerusalem's deliverance, told in a single sentence, and with Sennacherib's return to Nineveh. Its final lines leap forward some twenty years to the manner of his death.

=== 20
Jerusalem has been delivered from the Assyrians. This chapter tells two more stories from Hezekiah's reign, and it begins with the vague phrase in those days. The promise it contains, that the Lord will deliver the city from the king of Assyria, suggests that these events took place before or during the crisis of the last two chapters, rather than after it.

Hezekiah falls mortally ill, and Isaiah brings him a blunt message: put your house in order, for you will not recover. What follows involves a prayer, a poultice of figs, and a sign on a stairway built by Hezekiah's father Ahaz, where a shadow marked the passing of the day.

The second story brings a new name into the book. Envoys arrive from Merodach-baladan, a prince of Babylon who spent years leading revolts against Assyrian rule and was looking for allies. Babylon is still a restless subject of Assyria, not yet a great power. Hezekiah welcomes the envoys warmly and shows them everything he has. Isaiah's response looks far ahead, more than a century, to the day when Babylon will take it all away. The chapter closes with a mention of the tunnel Hezekiah cut to bring water into the city. It can still be walked today, and an inscription found inside it describes the moment the two teams of diggers met.

=== 21
Hezekiah has died, and his son Manasseh comes to the throne at the age of twelve. He will reign for fifty-five years, longer than any other king of Judah or Israel, and the writer judges him more harshly than any other.

These were the years when Assyria was at the height of its power, under Sennacherib's son Esarhaddon and grandson Ashurbanipal, and Assyrian records list Manasseh among the loyal kings who paid them tribute. Inside Judah, he reverses everything his father did. He rebuilds the high places, raises altars to Baal, and sets up altars to the stars in the very courts of the temple, and he is remembered for shedding innocent blood throughout Jerusalem. The writer compares him explicitly to Ahab, the king who brought Baal into Samaria.

The Lord's response comes through unnamed prophets, and now it is declared plainly that Jerusalem will go the way of Samaria. The book of Chronicles adds that Manasseh was later taken captive to Babylon and humbled himself before the Lord, but Kings does not mention it. The chapter ends with the short reign of his son Amon, who follows his father's ways and is murdered by his own servants. The people of the land put his eight-year-old son on the throne. His name is Josiah.

=== 22
Josiah, the boy king, has now reigned for eighteen years and is about twenty-six years old. Three centuries earlier, a man of God stood at Jeroboam's altar in Bethel and spoke his name. He is the last great king of Judah, and the writer praises him without reservation: he did not turn aside to the right or to the left.

Assyria's grip on the west is weakening in these years, and that gave Judah room to act. It was also in Josiah's reign that a young man from a priestly family at Anathoth, Jeremiah, received his call to be a prophet, and the prophet Zephaniah was speaking in Jerusalem too.

The chapter begins with the repair of the temple, arranged much as it was in the days of Joash, through the king's secretary Shaphan and Hilkiah the high priest. In the course of the work, Hilkiah finds something: the Book of the Law. Many scholars think it was some form of Deuteronomy, the book of the covenant Moses gave on the plains of Moab, but the text does not say. When Shaphan reads it aloud to the king, Josiah's response is immediate, and he sends to inquire of the Lord. The answer comes through a woman, the prophetess Huldah, who lives in the newer quarter of Jerusalem.

=== 23
Huldah's words have been brought to the king: judgement will come on Jerusalem, but not in Josiah's lifetime. Josiah calls the elders and all the people to the temple, reads the book aloud to them, and leads them into a covenant to keep its words.

Then comes the most thorough reform in the history of Judah. The chapter reads like an inventory of three centuries of accumulated idolatry, removed, broken and burned. Some of it goes back to Manasseh and Ahaz, some to Solomon himself, whose shrines east of Jerusalem are still standing. It reaches north to Bethel, where Jeroboam's altar still stands, and there the word spoken by the man of God in First Kings is fulfilled in detail. The reform ends with a Passover kept as it had not been kept since the days of the judges.

Yet the writer adds a sombre note: even this did not turn aside the Lord's anger over Manasseh. The last part of the chapter is set in a changing world. Nineveh, the Assyrian capital, has fallen to the Babylonians and Medes, an event the prophet Nahum celebrated. Pharaoh Neco of Egypt marches north to support what is left of Assyria against Babylon, and Josiah goes out to meet him at Megiddo. Afterwards, Egypt controls Judah, and Neco chooses who will sit on David's throne.

=== 24
Josiah is dead, and Judah is now a vassal of Egypt. Pharaoh Neco has deposed Josiah's son Jehoahaz after three months and placed another son, Eliakim, on the throne under the new name Jehoiakim. But the balance of power is about to shift again. At Carchemish on the Euphrates, around six hundred and five years before Christ, the Babylonian crown prince Nebuchadnezzar defeats the Egyptian army, and soon afterwards he becomes king of Babylon. Egypt, the writer notes, never marched out of its own land again.

Jehoiakim submits to Babylon for three years and then rebels. The book of Jeremiah gives a vivid picture of this king, who cut up the prophet's scroll and burned it piece by piece in his winter fire. The book of Daniel places the first deportation of young nobles to Babylon in his reign. Jehoiakim dies before Babylon's punishment arrives, and it falls instead on his eighteen-year-old son Jehoiachin.

Jehoiachin reigns only three months before Nebuchadnezzar's army surrounds Jerusalem. This first fall of the city, about five hundred and ninety-seven years before Christ, strips it of its king, its leaders, its soldiers and its craftsmen. Among those carried to Babylon was a young priest named Ezekiel, who would receive his visions among the exiles. Nebuchadnezzar places Jehoiachin's uncle on the throne, another son of Josiah, renaming him Zedekiah. He will be the last king of Judah.

=== 25
Zedekiah has rebelled against Babylon, despite the warnings of Jeremiah, whose book tells these final years from inside the city, with the prophet imprisoned and consulted in secret by the frightened king.

The chapter is dated with care, almost day by day. In the ninth year of Zedekiah's reign, Nebuchadnezzar's army arrives and builds siege works around Jerusalem. The siege lasts about a year and a half, until famine has done its work. What happens to Zedekiah takes place at Riblah, in Syria, where Nebuchadnezzar has his headquarters, and the destruction of the city is carried out a month later by his captain of the guard, Nebuzaradan. Among the things broken up and carried away are the bronze pillars, the stands and the great Sea that Solomon's craftsman made.

The account does not end with the burning of the city. Nebuchadnezzar appoints a governor over those left in the land, Gedaliah, the grandson of Shaphan, the secretary who read the book of the law to Josiah. Then the final paragraph moves forward many years, to Babylon, where Jehoiachin, the king taken captive as a young man, is still alive in prison. Clay tablets found in Babylon record the rations issued to him there. The last word of the book belongs to him, a son of David.
