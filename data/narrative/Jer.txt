=== 1
The book opens with a heading that frames everything after it. These are the words of Jeremiah, son of Hilkiah, from a priestly family in Anathoth, a village in the hills of Benjamin a few miles north of Jerusalem. The heading also marks out his years: from the thirteenth year of King Josiah, through the reign of Jehoiakim, to the eleventh year of Zedekiah, when Jerusalem went into exile. Before a word of the message is spoken, the listener already knows how the story ends.

Then Jeremiah speaks for himself, and tells how it began. The Lord tells him that he was known and set apart before he was formed in the womb, appointed a prophet to the nations. Jeremiah protests that he is only a youth and cannot speak. The Lord touches his mouth and puts his words there.

Two brief visions follow. The first turns on a play on words in Hebrew, where the word for almond sounds like the word for watching. The second is a cooking pot, boiling and tipped toward Judah from the north. The chapter closes with a promise to the young prophet: kings, officials, priests and people will fight against him, but they will not overcome him.

=== 2
Jeremiah's first public message is addressed to Jerusalem, and it begins not with a threat but with a memory. The Lord recalls Israel's devotion in the early days, when she followed him through the wilderness like a young bride. Everything that follows is measured against that beginning.

These chapters carry no date of their own, but they seem to belong to the early years of Jeremiah's ministry, in the reign of Josiah. The poetry moves quickly, almost like a lawsuit, with God bringing a case against his people. Its images are some of the most memorable in the book: a fountain of living water abandoned for cracked cisterns, a choice vine gone wild, a restless young camel running in every direction.

Listen too for the politics of the day. Judah is looking for security among the great powers, toward Egypt, with its cities of Memphis and Tahpanhes and the waters of the Nile, and toward Assyria and the Euphrates. Assyria has dominated the region for a century and more, but its strength is now fading. The chapter ends with a warning that Egypt will disappoint Judah just as Assyria did.

=== 3
The charge of unfaithfulness continues, now framed in the language of marriage and divorce. Under the law, a man who divorced his wife could not take her back once she had married another. Judah has gone after many lovers. Can she simply return?

Midway through, the poetry gives way to prose, and for once the setting is named: this word came in the days of King Josiah. The Lord compares two sisters. Faithless Israel is the northern kingdom, conquered by Assyria about a century earlier, its people scattered. Unfaithful Judah watched that happen and learned nothing. Her return to the Lord, he says, was only in pretence. Josiah was a reforming king, and some readers hear in this a judgement on how shallow that reform remained among the people, though the text does not say so directly.

The surprise is where the message is sent next. Jeremiah is told to proclaim it toward the north, to the scattered exiles of Israel. Return, faithless children. The chapter looks ahead to a day when Israel and Judah walk together again, and it ends with words of confession placed in the people's mouths.

=== 4
The call to return reaches its conclusion in the opening lines. If Israel will put away its idols and swear by the Lord in truth and justice, the nations themselves will find blessing in him. Judah is urged to break up its unploughed ground and circumcise its hearts.

Then the tone changes abruptly, and the alarm sounds. Blow the ram's horn, raise the signal flag, run for the fortified cities. A lion has come out of its thicket, a destroyer of nations is on the move. The invader from the north, first glimpsed in the vision of the boiling pot, is coming closer, though he has not yet been named. Reports arrive from Dan, at the far northern edge of the old kingdom, and from the hills of Ephraim, tracing the route an army would take toward Jerusalem.

The chapter also lets us hear Jeremiah himself, in anguish at what he sees. In one passage he looks out on the land and finds it formless and empty, without light, without people or birds, in words that deliberately echo the opening of Genesis. It is as if creation itself were being undone.

=== 5
Jeremiah is sent on a search through the streets and squares of Jerusalem. If he can find one person who acts justly and seeks the truth, the Lord will pardon the city. He looks first among the poor and assumes their failure comes from ignorance. Then he goes to the powerful, who surely know the way of the Lord, and finds that they too have thrown off the yoke.

The chapter gathers its charges around this failed search: idolatry, adultery, the rich growing fat on deceit while the orphan and the needy go undefended. Three times the same question returns, should I not punish them for these things?

Alongside the charges comes a fuller description of the coming enemy. It is a distant nation, an ancient one, whose language Judah will not understand, with archers whose quivers are like open graves. Yet even here a limit is set: the Lord will not make a full end of his people. The chapter closes on a disturbing note. The prophets speak lies, the priests rule as they please, and the people love it that way.

=== 6
The warning sharpens into the sound of an approaching siege. This time it is the people of Benjamin, Jeremiah's own tribe, who are told to flee from Jerusalem. Trumpets are to sound in Tekoa and signals rise over Beth-haccherem, villages to the south of the city, as though the alarm were passing from hilltop to hilltop.

We hear the enemy commanders talking among themselves, eager to attack at noon and impatient when the light begins to fail. Trees are cut down for siege ramps. Inside the city, prophet and priest alike offer easy reassurance, peace, peace, when there is no peace, a phrase that will return in the book.

Two images stand out. One is the crossroads, where the people are urged to ask for the ancient paths and walk in the good way, and refuse. The other closes the chapter. Jeremiah is appointed as an assayer, testing his people as a metalworker tests ore. The bellows blow and the fire burns, but the dross will not separate from the silver.

=== 7
The poetry of the opening chapters gives way to prose, and Jeremiah is given a place and an audience. He is to stand in the gate of the temple and speak to the worshippers as they come in.

This is often called the Temple Sermon. Most readers connect it with the scene in chapter twenty-six, where a sermon very like it is dated to the beginning of Jehoiakim's reign. If so, the story has moved on. Josiah is dead, killed in battle against Egypt, and his son Jehoiakim is on the throne. The people's confidence rests on the temple itself. As long as the house of the Lord stands among them, they believe, Jerusalem cannot fall. Jeremiah's reply points north to Shiloh, where the tabernacle once stood in the days of Eli and Samuel, and where nothing was left standing.

The rest of the chapter widens the charge. Families bake cakes for the Queen of Heaven, a goddess of the region. In the Valley of Ben-hinnom, just outside the city walls, children are burned at a place called Topheth. And Jeremiah is told something no prophet wants to hear: do not pray for this people.

=== 8
The final verses of the Temple Sermon look ahead to a grim scene. When the invader comes, the bones of kings, priests, prophets and people will be dragged from their graves and spread out before the sun, moon and stars they worshipped.

Then the chapter returns to poetry, and the voices begin to mingle. Sometimes the Lord speaks, sometimes the people, sometimes Jeremiah, and the listener has to follow the changes. The Lord marvels that the stork and the swallow know their seasons while his own people do not know his ways. He rebukes those who claim to be wise because they have the law, while the pen of the scribes has turned it into a lie.

The sound of enemy horses is heard again from Dan in the north. The last verses belong to Jeremiah, and they are full of grief. He hears the cry of his people from a distant land, asking whether the Lord is no longer in Zion. The harvest is past, the summer has ended, and still they are not saved. The chapter ends with a famous question: is there no balm in Gilead, the region east of the Jordan known for its healing resin?

=== 9
The grief of the last chapter runs straight on into this one. Jeremiah wishes his head were a spring of water, so that he could weep day and night for his people. In the same breath he wishes he could leave them for a lodging place in the desert. Lines like these have earned him the name the weeping prophet.

The Lord describes a society in which no one can trust a neighbour or a brother, where tongues are bent like bows to shoot lies. Jerusalem will become a heap of ruins, a haunt of jackals. The professional mourning women, who were hired to lead the lament at funerals, are summoned and told to teach their daughters to wail, because death has climbed in through the windows.

Near the end comes a short saying that stands apart from its surroundings. Let not the wise boast in their wisdom, the strong in their strength or the rich in their riches, but in knowing the Lord. The apostle Paul would draw on it centuries later when he wrote to the church in Corinth. The chapter closes by setting Judah alongside Egypt, Edom, Ammon and Moab: circumcised in body, uncircumcised in heart.

=== 10
Chapter ten steps back from the approaching army to set the Lord beside the gods of the nations. The people are told not to learn the ways of the nations, nor to be terrified by signs in the heavens, which the stargazers of the ancient world studied anxiously.

What follows is a satire on idols. A tree is cut from the forest, shaped by a craftsman, overlaid with silver from Tarshish and gold from Uphaz, dressed in blue and purple, and nailed down so that it will not fall over. Like a scarecrow in a cucumber field, it cannot speak or walk and must be carried. Against this the poem sets the living God, the Maker of all things, who stretched out the heavens and brings the wind from his storehouses. One verse in the middle is written not in Hebrew but in Aramaic, the international language of the day, as though it were a message the exiles could repeat to their neighbours.

The last verses return abruptly to the crisis at hand. Pack your bundle, you who live under siege. Jeremiah grieves over a tent torn down and children gone, and closes with a prayer that the Lord would correct him, gently, with justice and not in anger.

=== 11
The poetry yields to prose, and the subject is the covenant. Jeremiah is told to proclaim the words of this covenant in the cities of Judah and the streets of Jerusalem: the covenant made at the time of the exodus, when the Lord brought his people out of the iron furnace of Egypt. Its terms were simple. Obey me, and you will be my people, and I will be your God. Its curses for disobedience were plain as well.

Many readers connect this passage with the reign of Josiah, when a book of the law was found in the temple and the king led the nation in renewing the covenant, as the second book of Kings describes. The chapter does not give a date, and the point it makes is that the covenant has been broken all the same. Judah's gods are as many as its towns.

Then the chapter turns personal. Jeremiah discovers that he has been like a lamb led unknowingly to the slaughter. There is a plot to kill him, and it comes from the men of Anathoth, his own home town. This is the first of several passages in which Jeremiah pours out his heart to God, often called his confessions or laments.

=== 12
Jeremiah's complaint continues, and now it is a question that has troubled believers in every age. He acknowledges that the Lord is righteous, and then he presses his case. Why does the way of the wicked prosper? The Lord's reply is unexpected. It offers no explanation, only a warning that things will get harder. If you have raced with men on foot and they have worn you out, how will you compete with horses? Even Jeremiah's own family have betrayed him.

Then the voice changes, and the Lord himself grieves. He has forsaken his house and given the beloved of his soul into the hands of her enemies. Many shepherds, the rulers of the nations, have trampled his vineyard into a wilderness.

The chapter ends with a word about Judah's hostile neighbours, the peoples who have attacked the land. They too will be uprooted, and Judah with them. Yet there is a surprising promise. If those nations learn the ways of God's people and swear by his name, they too will be built up among them.

=== 13
Jeremiah is given the first of several acted parables, prophetic signs performed in public as well as spoken. He buys a linen loincloth, wears it, and then takes it to a place called Perath and hides it in a crack in the rocks. The name is the Hebrew word for the Euphrates, far to the east, though some think a town of similar name near Anathoth is meant. Many days later he digs it up, and it is ruined. In the same way the Lord will ruin the pride of Judah, a people he had bound to himself as closely as a garment, and who would not listen.

A saying about wine jars follows, turned into a picture of a nation staggering in drunken confusion. Then comes a word for the king and the queen mother, telling them to come down from their thrones because their crowns have fallen. They are not named, but many think of the young king Jehoiachin and his mother Nehushta, who were taken into exile together, as we will see later in the book.

The chapter ends with a famous question about the depth of Judah's habits. Can the Ethiopian change his skin, or the leopard its spots?

=== 14
A new heading introduces a word about the drought. In a land that depended on the rains of autumn and spring, a failed season was a catastrophe. The poem pictures it closely: servants returning from empty cisterns with empty jars, farmers covering their heads in shame over cracked ground, a doe abandoning her newborn fawn because there is no grass.

What follows is a kind of dialogue. The people pray, confessing their sins and appealing to the Lord as the hope of Israel. The Lord answers that they love to wander, and he tells Jeremiah once again not to pray for them. Jeremiah protests that the other prophets are promising peace and plenty. The Lord's reply is blunt: he did not send them, and the sword and famine they deny will fall on them too.

The chapter ends with a second prayer, an appeal to the Lord not to despise Zion or break his covenant, and a confession that only he can send the rain.

=== 15
The Lord answers the people's prayer from the end of chapter fourteen, and the answer is no. Even if Moses and Samuel stood before him, the two great intercessors of Israel's past, his heart would not turn toward this people. Sword, famine and captivity await them.

The reason given looks back two generations, to King Manasseh, son of Hezekiah, whose long reign filled Jerusalem with idolatry and innocent blood. The second book of Kings makes the same judgement, that even Josiah's reforms could not undo what Manasseh had done.

From here the chapter turns to Jeremiah's own anguish, in another of his laments. He wishes his mother had never borne him, a man of strife with whom the whole land contends. He remembers how he received God's words with joy and ate them, and how he has sat alone ever since because the Lord's hand was on him. Then he says something startling: the Lord has become to him like a deceptive brook, a stream that dries up when it is needed. The Lord answers with a rebuke and a renewal. If Jeremiah returns, he will stand before God again, and the promise of his call is repeated: a fortified wall of bronze, against which his enemies will fight but not prevail.

=== 16
After the renewal of his call, Jeremiah is told that his own life must become part of the message. He is not to marry or have children in this land, because the sons and daughters born there will die of disease, sword and famine. In the world of ancient Israel, where marriage and children were expected of almost everyone, this alone would have made him a living sign.

He is also forbidden to enter a house of mourning to comfort the bereaved, or a house of feasting to celebrate. The ordinary rhythms of grief and joy are to be withdrawn from the land, and Jeremiah's absence from both is meant to be noticed. When people ask what they have done to deserve such disaster, he is to give the answer: their fathers forsook the Lord, and they have done worse.

In the middle of the chapter, a sudden promise breaks in. A day will come when people no longer swear by the Lord who brought Israel out of Egypt, but by the Lord who brought them back from the land of the north. The return from exile will be a new exodus. The chapter ends with a vision of the nations themselves coming to the Lord, confessing that their fathers inherited only lies.

=== 17
Judah's sin is written with an iron pen, engraved with a diamond point on the tablets of the heart and on the horns of the altars. The chapter begins with that image of something cut too deep to be erased, and then gathers a series of shorter sayings.

One will sound familiar to anyone who knows the first psalm. The person who trusts in mere human strength is like a shrub in the desert, while the one who trusts in the Lord is like a tree planted by the water, green even in a year of drought. Another speaks of the human heart, deceitful above all things, which only the Lord can search. Another compares the man who grows rich unjustly to a partridge hatching eggs it did not lay.

Then Jeremiah prays again. His opponents are taunting him that the word of the Lord has not come to pass, and he asks to be healed and saved, and vindicated. The chapter closes with a sermon preached in the gates of Jerusalem, where traders and porters carried their loads in and out, calling the city to keep the Sabbath holy. Its future, the Lord says, depends on whether they will listen.

=== 18
Jeremiah is sent down to the potter's house, and there he watches a craftsman at work. The pot the potter is shaping goes wrong in his hands, so he presses the clay down and forms it into something else. The Lord draws the lesson. Israel is clay in his hand. When he announces judgement on a nation and that nation turns from its evil, he relents. When he promises good and the nation turns to evil, he reconsiders the good.

The message to Judah follows from this: turn now from your evil ways. The people's answer is recorded as bluntly as the call. It is hopeless, they say; we will follow our own plans.

Then the opposition to Jeremiah organises itself. His enemies reason that the nation will not miss one troublesome prophet, since there will always be priests to teach the law, wise men to give counsel and prophets to bring a word. Let us attack him with our tongues. Jeremiah's response is one of his fiercest prayers, in which he asks the Lord to deal with them in his anger. The listener should expect such honesty in this book. Jeremiah's prayers are not tidied up.

=== 19
The potter's clay becomes a finished jar. Jeremiah is told to buy one, an earthenware flask of the kind used for water, and to take some of the elders and senior priests out of the city to the Valley of Ben-hinnom, by the entrance of the Potsherd Gate. The name suits the place. It was likely where broken pottery was thrown, and it overlooked Topheth, where children had been burned in sacrifice.

There, in front of these witnesses, Jeremiah announces that the valley will be renamed the Valley of Slaughter, and he describes in terrible terms what a siege of Jerusalem will bring. Then he smashes the jar. A pot still soft on the wheel can be reshaped, but a fired jar, once broken, cannot be mended. That is the point, and it is not lost on the men who watch.

Jeremiah then walks back into the city, stands in the court of the temple, and repeats the warning to everyone gathered there. It is a public act in the most public place in Jerusalem, and the next chapter tells us what it cost him.

=== 20
Jeremiah's words in the temple court reach Pashhur son of Immer, a priest and the chief officer in the house of the Lord, responsible for order in the temple. He has Jeremiah beaten and put in the stocks overnight at the Upper Gate of Benjamin.

When he is released in the morning, Jeremiah gives Pashhur a new name, Terror on Every Side, and for the first time in the book the enemy from the north is named outright. The Lord will hand Judah over to the king of Babylon. Babylon, the old city on the Euphrates, has risen under a new dynasty to replace Assyria as the great power of the age, and from here on it stands at the centre of Jeremiah's message.

The second half of the chapter is the last and darkest of Jeremiah's laments. He accuses the Lord of deceiving him and overpowering him. He has become a laughing stock, and when he tries to keep silent, the word burns like fire shut up in his bones. He sings praise for a moment, and then curses the day he was born. The first half of the book ends its personal story here, on a cry without an answer.

=== 21
The book now leaps forward, perhaps twenty years. Jehoiakim has come and gone, and so has his son Jehoiachin. On the throne is Zedekiah, the last king of Judah, a son of Josiah placed there by Nebuchadnezzar himself. Zedekiah has rebelled, and the Babylonian army is outside the walls.

The king sends two men to Jeremiah: the priest Zephaniah, and another Pashhur, the son of Malchijah, not the man who put Jeremiah in the stocks. Their request is hopeful. Perhaps the Lord will do one of his wonders, as in the old days, and Nebuchadnezzar will withdraw. Many would have remembered how, a century earlier, the Assyrian army camped outside Jerusalem in the days of Hezekiah had suddenly gone home.

Jeremiah's answer offers no such hope. The Lord himself will fight against the city. To the people he sets out a stark choice, the way of life and the way of death: stay in the city and die, or go out and surrender to the Chaldeans, as the Babylonians were also called, and live. The chapter ends with a word for the royal house, to do justice every morning. This passage begins a long section about the kings of Judah.

=== 22
Jeremiah is sent down to the royal palace, which stood just below the temple on the slope of the city, to address the king of Judah in his own house. The charge is the one given at the end of chapter twenty-one: do justice, rescue the oppressed, protect the foreigner, the orphan and the widow. If the kings obey, David's line will continue. If not, the palace will become a ruin.

What follows is a series of words about the last kings, gathered here together though they come from different years. It helps to have the names straight. Josiah, the good king, died in battle. His son Shallum, better known in the book of Kings as Jehoahaz, reigned for only three months before Pharaoh took him away to Egypt; weep for him, Jeremiah says, for he will never return. Next is Jehoiakim, another son of Josiah, who builds himself a grand palace panelled in cedar and painted red, using forced labour and refusing to pay the workers. The Lord sets him beside his father, who defended the poor and needy, and asks whether that is not what it means to know him.

The chapter ends with Jehoiakim's son, here called Coniah, also known as Jeconiah and Jehoiachin. Even if he were the signet ring on the Lord's right hand, he would be pulled off and thrown into a foreign land, he and his mother, and no son of his would sit on David's throne.

=== 23
The words against the kings end with a broader charge against the shepherds, the rulers who have scattered God's flock instead of caring for it. Then comes a promise. The Lord himself will gather the remnant from the lands of exile and give them shepherds who will truly tend them. And he will raise up for David a righteous Branch, a king who will reign wisely and do justice, and his name will be The Lord Our Righteousness.

The name would have carried a sting in Jeremiah's day. The reigning king, Zedekiah, bore a name that means the Lord is righteous, and he fell far short of it. The true king, the promise says, is still to come. Christians have long read this passage as pointing to Jesus, the Son of David.

The rest of the chapter, and the longest stretch of it, concerns the prophets. Jeremiah is heartbroken and shaken, like a man drunk with wine, at what he sees among them. They promise peace to those who despise the Lord. They trade dreams and borrow one another's words. None of them has stood in the council of the Lord. The chapter ends with a play on a Hebrew word that means both an oracle and a burden. People keep asking Jeremiah, what is the burden of the Lord? The answer is that they are.

=== 24
This vision comes from the early years of Zedekiah's reign. Nebuchadnezzar has already taken the young king Jeconiah to Babylon, together with the leading officials and the skilled craftsmen and smiths. The second book of Kings describes this first great deportation. Among those taken, though Jeremiah does not mention him, was a young priest named Ezekiel.

Jeremiah sees two baskets of figs set in front of the temple. One holds very good figs, like the first ripe fruit of the season. The other holds figs so bad they cannot be eaten.

The meaning reverses what everyone in Jerusalem assumed. Those left behind in the city might easily have thought themselves the favoured ones, spared while others were punished. The Lord says the opposite. The exiles in Babylon are the good figs. He will watch over them, bring them back, and give them a heart to know him. Zedekiah, his officials and those who remain in Jerusalem, along with those who have gone down to Egypt, are the bad figs. The future of God's people, strange as it sounded, lay with the exiles.

=== 25
This chapter is carefully dated, and the date matters. It is the fourth year of Jehoiakim, which the text tells us was the first year of Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon. That was the year Nebuchadnezzar defeated the army of Egypt at Carchemish, on the Euphrates, and Babylon became the master of the whole region. Jeremiah has now been prophesying for twenty-three years, and he looks back over all of them. You have not listened.

For the first time the length of the coming servitude is given. Judah and the surrounding nations will serve the king of Babylon for seventy years. After that, Babylon itself will be judged. Daniel, generations later, will be found reading this prophecy in Babylon and counting the years.

The second half of the chapter widens to the whole world. Jeremiah is handed a cup filled with the wine of the Lord's wrath, and every nation must drink it, beginning with Jerusalem and then Egypt, the Philistine cities, Edom, Moab, Ammon, Tyre and Sidon, the desert tribes, Elam and Media. Last of all, the king of Sheshach, a coded name for Babylon. Many of these nations will be addressed one by one near the end of the book. In the ancient Greek translation of Jeremiah, those oracles follow on directly from here.

=== 26
The book now turns from collected messages to stories about Jeremiah himself, and it steps back in time to do it. This scene is set at the beginning of the reign of Jehoiakim, just after Josiah's death, and it tells what happened when Jeremiah preached the sermon given in chapter seven: make this house like Shiloh.

The response is immediate. Priests, prophets and people seize him and shout that he must die. Word reaches the royal officials, who come up from the palace and sit in judgement at the New Gate of the temple. The scene unfolds like a trial, with accusers, a defendant and a verdict. Jeremiah does not retract a word, and he warns his judges that if they kill him they will bring innocent blood on the city.

Some of the elders recall a precedent. A century earlier, in Hezekiah's day, the prophet Micah of Moresheth had said that Zion would be ploughed like a field, and the king did not kill him, but sought the Lord. The book of Micah still contains that saying. Then, as if to show how close Jeremiah came, the chapter tells of another prophet, Uriah, who spoke the same message and did not survive. Jeremiah is protected by Ahikam son of Shaphan, from a family of royal officials. Shaphan had been the scribe who read the newly found book of the law to King Josiah, and his family will appear again.

=== 27
Some years have passed. Jehoiakim is dead, his son Jeconiah is in Babylon, and Zedekiah reigns in Jerusalem as Nebuchadnezzar's appointee. Envoys have arrived in the city from the kings of Edom, Moab, Ammon, Tyre and Sidon. The next chapter suggests this was around Zedekiah's fourth year, and it seems that these small kingdoms were exploring a joint rebellion against Babylon.

Into that meeting walks Jeremiah with a yoke on his neck, made of wooden bars tied with leather straps, the kind laid across the shoulders of an ox. He sends a message back with the envoys to their kings. The Lord made the earth and gives it to whom he pleases, and he has given these lands to his servant Nebuchadnezzar. Any nation that bows its neck to Babylon will remain in its land. Any nation that resists will be destroyed.

He says the same to Zedekiah, and then to the priests and people, who are hearing from other prophets that the temple treasures taken to Babylon will soon be returned. Jeremiah answers that even the great bronze pillars and the bronze sea still standing in the temple court will follow them to Babylon.

=== 28
That same year, in the temple, Jeremiah is publicly contradicted. The challenger is Hananiah son of Azzur, a prophet from Gibeon, and he speaks just as Jeremiah does, in the name of the Lord of Hosts. His message is that the yoke of Babylon is broken. Within two years the temple treasures will be returned, and King Jeconiah and the exiles will come home.

Jeremiah's first reply is remarkable. Amen, he says. May the Lord do so. He would like nothing better. But he reminds Hananiah that the prophets of old spoke of war and disaster, and that a prophet who promises peace will be known as truly sent only when his words come true.

Then Hananiah takes the wooden yoke from Jeremiah's neck and breaks it in front of everyone, and Jeremiah, without another word, simply walks away. Only later does the word of the Lord come to him, and he returns with a message for Hananiah. The chapter is short, and its ending is told in a single plain sentence.

=== 29
While the conflict with false prophets plays out in Jerusalem, a similar struggle is going on among the exiles in Babylon. Jeremiah sends them a letter. It travels with two royal messengers whom Zedekiah is sending to Nebuchadnezzar, one of them a son of Shaphan, the family that has already protected Jeremiah once.

The exiles are hearing from their own prophets that their time in Babylon will be short. Jeremiah's letter tells them to do the opposite of waiting. Build houses and live in them. Plant gardens. Marry and have children and grandchildren. Seek the welfare of the city where you have been sent, and pray to the Lord for it, because in its welfare you will find your own. Only when Babylon's seventy years are complete will the Lord bring them back. The letter contains one of the most quoted promises in the book, about the plans the Lord has for his people, plans for a future and a hope, spoken to people who would mostly not live to see the return.

The letter also names false prophets among the exiles, and the chapter ends with an angry reply from one of them, Shemaiah, who writes back to Jerusalem demanding that the priests put Jeremiah in the stocks.

=== 30
After so many chapters of warning, the book opens a new section, and its tone is almost entirely different. Jeremiah is told to write in a book all the words the Lord has spoken to him, because the days are coming when the Lord will restore the fortunes of his people, Israel and Judah together, and bring them back to the land given to their fathers. These four chapters, from thirty to thirty-three, are often called the Book of Consolation.

Most of this chapter and the next are poetry, and they carry no date. The sorrow is not forgotten. The poem begins with a cry of terror, men clutching themselves like women in labour, a time of distress for Jacob unlike any other. Judah's wound is described as incurable, her allies gone, her punishment deserved. Then the turn comes, and it comes from the Lord alone. I will restore your health and heal your wounds, he says, because they have called you an outcast.

The promises are concrete: the city rebuilt on its ruins, the palace standing in its proper place, songs of thanksgiving, children as in former days, and a ruler who comes from among the people themselves. Running through it all is the covenant formula first spoken at Sinai, you will be my people, and I will be your God.

=== 31
The promises of restoration continue, and they now reach out to the northern tribes, Ephraim and Samaria, lost to Assyria long before Jeremiah's birth. I have loved you with an everlasting love, the Lord says. Again there will be vineyards on the hills of Samaria, and watchmen on the hills of Ephraim calling the people up to Zion. The blind and the lame, mothers with child, will come back from the land of the north in a great company.

One brief passage stands out for its sorrow. A voice is heard in Ramah, a town of Benjamin north of Jerusalem: Rachel, the mother of Joseph and Benjamin, weeping for her children because they are no more. The Lord tells her to dry her tears, for they will return. Matthew's Gospel would later recall these words at the slaughter of the children of Bethlehem.

Then comes the passage for which this chapter is best known. The Lord promises a new covenant with Israel and Judah, unlike the one made at the exodus, which they broke. This time the law will be written on their hearts, all will know him, and he will remember their sins no more. Jesus spoke of the new covenant in his blood at the Last Supper, and the letter to the Hebrews quotes this passage at length. The chapter ends with Jerusalem measured out for rebuilding, holy to the Lord.

=== 32
The poetry gives way to a dated narrative, and it is set at the darkest point of all. It is the tenth year of Zedekiah, the eighteenth of Nebuchadnezzar. The Babylonian army is besieging Jerusalem, and Jeremiah is confined in the courtyard of the guard at the palace, imprisoned by the king for prophesying that the city will fall and Zedekiah himself will be taken to Babylon.

Into this setting comes a visit from a relative. Jeremiah's cousin Hanamel arrives from Anathoth asking him to buy the family field there. Under the law, when land had to be sold, the nearest kinsman had the right and duty to buy it and keep it in the family. The land in question is behind enemy lines, and its value must have seemed close to nothing.

Here we meet, for the first time by name, Baruch son of Neriah, Jeremiah's scribe and companion. The purchase is carried out with legal care: the silver weighed, the deed signed, sealed and witnessed, two copies made and stored in a clay jar so that they will last. Then Jeremiah prays, and his prayer is honest about how strange this seems. The Lord's answer is that nothing is too hard for him. The city will fall, and yet fields will be bought and sold in this land again.

=== 33
Jeremiah is still shut up in the courtyard of the guard when the word of the Lord comes to him a second time. Call to me and I will answer you, the Lord says, and show you great and hidden things.

The present is described without softening. Houses in Jerusalem, even royal buildings, are being torn down to strengthen the walls against the siege ramps, and the places they leave will be filled with the bodies of the dead. Yet the Lord promises healing for this very city. In the streets now empty of man and beast, the sounds silenced in so many earlier chapters will be heard again: the voices of bride and bridegroom, and of worshippers bringing thank offerings to the temple and singing that the Lord is good, his loving devotion endures forever. Shepherds will once more count their flocks in the towns of Judah.

The promise of the righteous Branch from chapter twenty-three returns, now with Jerusalem itself bearing the name The Lord Our Righteousness. The chapter closes by tying the covenant with David and with the Levitical priests to the fixed order of day and night. People are saying that the Lord has rejected the two families he chose, Israel and Judah. He answers that he would sooner break his covenant with the sun and moon.

=== 34
We remain in the final siege, but this chapter steps back a little from the scene in the courtyard of the guard. Nebuchadnezzar's army, swelled with troops from the kingdoms under his control, is fighting against Jerusalem and the towns around it. Only two other fortified cities of Judah still hold out, Lachish and Azekah, in the lowlands to the south-west. Archaeologists working at Lachish found letters written on broken pottery by Judean officers from about this time, and one of them reports that the signals from Azekah can no longer be seen.

Jeremiah brings Zedekiah a personal word. The city will burn, and the king will meet the king of Babylon face to face. Yet he will not die by the sword.

The second half records a broken promise. Early in the siege, Zedekiah and the people made a solemn covenant in the temple to set free their Hebrew slaves, as the law of Moses required after six years of service. They did it, and then they took the slaves back. The text hints at the reason: the Babylonian army had withdrawn for a time, and with the danger apparently past, the promise was forgotten. The covenant had been sealed in an ancient way, by walking between the halves of a slaughtered calf. The Lord says the fate of the calf will now be theirs.

=== 35
This chapter steps back again, to the days of Jehoiakim, probably near the end of his reign, when Nebuchadnezzar's armies and raiding bands of Arameans were already in the land. Among the people who have taken refuge inside Jerusalem's walls is an unusual clan, the Rechabites.

They trace their customs to their ancestor Jonadab son of Rechab, who appears in the second book of Kings as an ally of Jehu, some two and a half centuries earlier. Jonadab commanded his descendants never to drink wine, build houses, sow fields or plant vineyards, but to live all their days in tents. They have kept those rules ever since, and only the threat of invasion has driven them into the city.

Jeremiah brings the whole family into a room in the temple, sets bowls of wine and cups in front of them, and invites them to drink. They refuse, politely and firmly. The test is not meant to trap them. It is a lesson staged for Jerusalem to watch. A family has kept the command of a human ancestor for generations, while the people of Judah have not listened to the Lord, though he has spoken to them again and again. The chapter ends with a promise to the Rechabites themselves.

=== 36
We go back to the fourth year of Jehoiakim, the year of Carchemish, when Babylon took control of the region. Jeremiah is told to take a scroll and write down everything the Lord has said to him from the days of Josiah until now. Perhaps, hearing it all together, the people will turn.

Jeremiah dictates and Baruch writes. Jeremiah himself is barred from the temple, so Baruch is sent to read the scroll aloud on a fast day, when crowds have come in from the towns of Judah. The reading takes place in the following year, from the room of Gemariah son of Shaphan, another member of that family of officials. Gemariah's son hears it and reports it to the royal officials, and they summon Baruch to read it to them too. Among them is Elnathan son of Achbor, the man who once fetched the prophet Uriah back from Egypt for execution.

The officials are afraid. They tell Baruch and Jeremiah to hide, and they take the scroll to the king. It is winter, and Jehoiakim is sitting in his winter apartment with a fire burning in a brazier in front of him. What he does next is one of the most vivid scenes in the book, and it is watched by men who plead with him to stop. The chapter ends with a second scroll, and it is likely that the book we now have grew from it.

=== 37
The story jumps forward again, to Zedekiah's reign and the siege. The chapter opens with a reminder of who Zedekiah is: placed on the throne by Nebuchadnezzar in place of his nephew Coniah, and like those before him, not listening to the word of the Lord. Yet he keeps sending to Jeremiah.

This scene comes earlier in the siege than the courtyard chapters we have just heard. Jeremiah is still free to come and go. An Egyptian army has marched out to help Judah, and the Babylonians have lifted the siege to deal with it. In Jerusalem, hope revives. Jeremiah's message is that it will not last. Even if the Babylonian army were reduced to wounded men lying in their tents, they would rise up and burn the city.

During this lull, Jeremiah sets out for the land of Benjamin to see to family property, and at the city gate he is arrested as a deserter going over to the enemy. The officials beat him and throw him into a cistern turned dungeon beneath the house of Jonathan the scribe, where he stays for many days. Then the king sends for him in secret, and asks a question he will ask more than once: is there any word from the Lord?

=== 38
Jeremiah is held in the courtyard of the guard, but he is still able to speak to those around him, and his message has not changed. Whoever stays in the city will die. Whoever goes out to the Chaldeans will live. To a group of the king's officials, among them Pashhur son of Malchijah, who carried the king's question to Jeremiah back in chapter twenty-one, this is treason. He is weakening the hands of the soldiers. They demand his death.

Zedekiah's reply reveals the kind of king he is. He is in your hands, he says, for the king can do nothing against you. So Jeremiah is lowered by ropes into a cistern, empty of water now in the long siege, and he sinks into the mud at the bottom.

Help comes from an unexpected direction. Ebed-melech, a Cushite, from the kingdom on the upper Nile south of Egypt, is a court official in the palace, and he goes to the king to plead for the prophet. The rescue that follows is told with tender detail. Then Zedekiah sends for Jeremiah once more and meets him secretly at an entrance of the temple. Their conversation is the last between them recorded in the book, and it shows a king who is afraid of almost everyone: of the Babylonians, of his own officials, and of the Judeans who have already deserted.

=== 39
The long siege comes to its end, and the chapter tells it briefly, with dates. Nebuchadnezzar's army arrived in the ninth year of Zedekiah, in the tenth month. In the eleventh year, on the ninth day of the fourth month, a year and a half later, the wall was broken through. The second book of Kings records the same events, and chapter fifty-two at the end of this book tells them again in fuller detail.

Babylonian officers take their seats in the Middle Gate, the sign that the city now belongs to them. Zedekiah and his soldiers slip out by night through the gate between the two walls near the king's garden and run east toward the Jordan valley. They are caught on the plains of Jericho and brought north to Nebuchadnezzar's headquarters at Riblah, in the land of Hamath in Syria. What happens to Zedekiah there is exactly what Jeremiah warned him of.

The palace and houses are burned and the walls pulled down. Nebuzaradan, the captain of the guard, takes the people into exile and leaves only the poorest to work the land. Jeremiah, by Nebuchadnezzar's own order, is treated with care and placed with Gedaliah, the grandson of Shaphan. The chapter closes by remembering a promise given earlier to Ebed-melech, the man who pulled Jeremiah out of the mud.

=== 40
Jerusalem has fallen, and the book enters its last stage. The story continues with the remnant in Judah, and with the last of Jeremiah's ministry.

The chapter begins by filling in a detail. In the confusion after the city's capture, Jeremiah was caught up among the captives and taken in chains to Ramah, the assembly point north of Jerusalem for those being sent to Babylon. There Nebuzaradan finds him and sets him free. The Babylonian commander even speaks of the disaster as the judgement of Jeremiah's God. He offers the prophet a choice: come to Babylon under his protection, or stay in the land. Jeremiah chooses to stay, and goes to Gedaliah.

Nebuchadnezzar has appointed Gedaliah son of Ahikam as governor over what is left of Judah, with his seat at Mizpah, a few miles north of the ruined city. Army officers who had scattered into the countryside come to him, and Judeans who had fled to Moab, Ammon and Edom begin to return. Gedaliah's advice to all of them is Jeremiah's: serve the king of Babylon and it will go well with you. That summer there is an abundant harvest. But one of the officers, Johanan son of Kareah, brings Gedaliah a warning about another, Ishmael son of Nethaniah, and Gedaliah refuses to believe it.

=== 41
The warning Gedaliah dismissed proves true. In the seventh month, Ishmael son of Nethaniah, a man of the royal family, comes to Mizpah with ten men, and they share a meal with the governor. The book has already hinted at the forces behind him: the king of the Ammonites, and perhaps resentment that a man outside David's line now governed Judah under Babylon.

What follows is told without comment. It is one of the darkest stories in the book, and its violence spreads beyond Gedaliah himself, to the Judeans and Babylonian soldiers with him, and then to a group of pilgrims from Shechem, Shiloh and Samaria. They come in mourning, with beards shaved and clothes torn, bringing offerings to the site of the house of the Lord, unaware that anything has happened. The cistern they end in is an old one, dug centuries earlier by King Asa in a war with the northern kingdom.

Johanan son of Kareah and the other officers pursue Ishmael and catch up with him at the great pool of Gibeon, and the captives are freed, though Ishmael himself escapes to Ammon. Now the survivors are afraid. The man Babylon appointed has been murdered, and they expect reprisals. They stop near Bethlehem, already intending to go down to Egypt. Jews have long kept a fast in the seventh month in memory of Gedaliah's death.

=== 42
The survivors are camped near Bethlehem, on the road south, with Egypt in their minds. Before they go any further, the officers, led by Johanan son of Kareah, come to Jeremiah with every appearance of humility. Pray to the Lord your God for us, they say, that he will show us the way we should go. Whatever the answer is, pleasant or unpleasant, we will obey.

Jeremiah agrees, and then he waits. For ten days there is no answer. It is a small detail, but it says something about how the word of the Lord came to Jeremiah. He did not produce it on demand.

When the answer comes, it is clear. If they stay in the land, the Lord will build them up and not tear them down, plant them and not uproot them, the language of Jeremiah's first call. They need not fear the king of Babylon. But if they go to Egypt, looking for a land with no war, no trumpet and no hunger, the sword and famine they are running from will follow them there. Before he has even finished speaking, Jeremiah seems to sense that their minds are already made up.

=== 43
The officers' answer comes at once, and it is the answer Jeremiah expected. Johanan and Azariah and the men with them call him a liar. The Lord has not sent you, they say. They blame Baruch for turning Jeremiah against them, so that they will be handed to the Babylonians.

So the whole remnant sets off for Egypt: the soldiers, the women and children, the king's daughters, those who had come back from neighbouring lands, and Jeremiah and Baruch with them, apparently against their will. They cross the border and come to Tahpanhes, a frontier town on the eastern edge of the Nile Delta, where Pharaoh had a residence.

Jeremiah's ministry continues on foreign soil. He performs another sign, burying large stones in the brick pavement at the entrance to the royal building while his fellow Judeans watch. Nebuchadnezzar, he says, will one day set his throne on these very stones and spread his canopy over them. Egypt, the place where they had hoped to escape Babylon, will feel its power too. Its temples will burn, and the obelisks of the temple of the sun, at the city the Greeks called Heliopolis, will be broken. A fragment of a Babylonian chronicle suggests that Nebuchadnezzar did campaign against Egypt some years later, though how far he went is not known.

=== 44
Some time has passed, perhaps years. Judeans are now living in several parts of Egypt: at Migdol and Tahpanhes in the Delta, at Memphis, the ancient capital near modern Cairo, and in Pathros, the region of Upper Egypt to the south. Jeremiah addresses them all. This is the last message of his that the book records.

He reminds them of what they have seen with their own eyes, the ruins of Jerusalem and the towns of Judah, and why it happened. Now in Egypt they are doing the same things again, burning incense to other gods.

What makes this chapter unforgettable is the reply. A great crowd, men and women together, tell Jeremiah plainly that they will not listen. They will go on making offerings to the Queen of Heaven, as they and their fathers and their kings always did. Back then, they say, there was plenty of food and no disaster. It was only when they stopped, perhaps a reference to Josiah's reforms, that everything went wrong. It is a rival reading of the same history, and Jeremiah answers it. The chapter ends with a sign. Pharaoh Hophra, king of Egypt, will be handed over to his enemies, as Zedekiah was. Egyptian and Greek sources tell us that Hophra did lose his throne and his life in an uprising some years later.

=== 45
This short chapter is a postscript, and it takes us back almost twenty years, to the fourth year of Jehoiakim, the year Baruch first wrote Jeremiah's words on a scroll. It belongs, in other words, beside chapter thirty-six. It may have been placed here, after the stories of the fall and the flight to Egypt, because Baruch's own life has now played out against everything the scroll foretold.

Baruch, like Jeremiah before him, has been complaining to the Lord. Woe is me, he says, the Lord has added sorrow to my pain. I am worn out with groaning and find no rest. Writing down those words of judgement had cost him something. He seems to have come from a family of standing; his brother, we will learn, served at the royal court. Perhaps he had hoped for a different future.

The Lord's answer is gentle and unflinching at once. He is tearing down what he built and uprooting what he planted, across the whole land. Is this a time to seek great things for yourself? Do not seek them. But Baruch is given a promise of his own, the same one given to Ebed-melech: wherever he goes, his life will be spared.

=== 46
A new heading opens the last major section of the book: the word of the Lord to Jeremiah concerning the nations. At his call, Jeremiah was appointed a prophet to the nations, and these chapters show what that meant. They run from Egypt in the west to Babylon in the east.

The first oracle concerns Egypt, and it is dated to a single battle. In the fourth year of Jehoiakim, Pharaoh Neco's army was defeated by Nebuchadnezzar at Carchemish, on the upper Euphrates. This was the same Neco who had killed King Josiah at Megiddo four years earlier. The poem is vivid, almost a war report: orders shouted, horses harnessed, armour polished, and then the sudden rout, with warriors stumbling over one another in the north by the river. Egypt rising like the Nile in flood is a proud image, and the poem turns it to scorn.

A second oracle looks further ahead, to Nebuchadnezzar coming against Egypt itself, against Memphis and the god Amon of Thebes. Yet even here a later restoration for Egypt is promised. The chapter ends with words of comfort for Jacob that echo the Book of Consolation: do not be afraid, I am with you.

=== 47
From Egypt the oracles move up the coast to the Philistines, Israel's old neighbours and rivals on the southern coastal plain. The heading gives a time marker, before Pharaoh struck down Gaza, though which pharaoh and which attack are not known for certain. The Philistines are called the remnant from the coasts of Caphtor, the island, probably Crete, from which their ancestors were remembered to have come.

The poem is short and intense. The danger is pictured as waters rising out of the north and overflowing the land, and then as the noise of an army: the hammering of horses' hooves, the rumble of chariots, the rattle of wheels. Fathers are so terrified that they do not turn back for their children. Gaza shaves its head in mourning and Ashkelon falls silent. Tyre and Sidon, the Phoenician cities to the north, lose their last allies.

The poem ends with a cry to the sword of the Lord to go back into its sheath and rest, and an answer: how can it rest, when the Lord has given it its orders? Babylonian records tell us that Nebuchadnezzar sacked Ashkelon in the winter after his victory at Carchemish.

=== 48
The next oracle, one of the longest in the book, concerns Moab, Israel's neighbour across the Dead Sea. The Moabites lived on the high tableland to the east, and Genesis traced their ancestry to Lot. Relations between the two peoples had been tangled for centuries, sometimes friendly, more often hostile, and Ruth, the great-grandmother of David, was a Moabite.

The poem moves from town to town across Moab's territory, naming them one after another: Nebo, Heshbon, Horonaim, Dibon, Aroer by the Arnon gorge, and many more. Many of these same places appear on a famous stone inscription set up by a Moabite king named Mesha, about three centuries earlier, and found at Dibon in the nineteenth century. Moab's god Chemosh, also named on that stone, will go into exile with his priests.

One image is especially memorable. Moab has been left undisturbed like wine settled on its dregs, never poured from jar to jar, so it has kept its old taste. Now it will be poured out. Some passages closely resemble Isaiah's earlier oracle on Moab, and the grief in them is striking. The Lord's heart laments for Moab like a flute. The chapter ends, unexpectedly, with a promise that in days to come Moab's fortunes will be restored.

=== 49
This chapter gathers five shorter oracles, moving around the map of Judah's neighbours.

First comes Ammon, north of Moab, with its capital at Rabbah, the site of modern Amman. Its god Milcom has taken possession of land belonging to the tribe of Gad, east of the Jordan. Then comes Edom, south of the Dead Sea, the people descended from Esau, Jacob's brother. Teman was famed for its wisdom and Bozrah was a stronghold, and Edom trusted in its rocky heights like an eagle's nest. Several lines here are shared with the short book of Obadiah, which also concerns Edom.

The third oracle turns north to Damascus, the old Aramean capital, with Hamath and Arpad, cities further north in Syria. The fourth concerns Kedar and the kingdoms of Hazor, desert tribes of Arabia who lived in tents with their camels and flocks, and here the attacker is named: Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon. This Hazor is not the old Canaanite city in Galilee but somewhere in the Arabian desert.

The last oracle is dated to the beginning of Zedekiah's reign, and it is the most distant of all. Elam lay east of Babylon, in what is now south-western Iran, famous for its archers. Several of these oracles, like Moab's, end with a promise of restoration.

=== 50
The oracles against the nations end where all of Jeremiah's prophecy has been heading: with Babylon itself. These two chapters, fifty and fifty-one, are by far the longest of the oracles, and they turn the whole message of the book around. Babylon, the Lord's instrument of judgement, the servant Nebuchadnezzar, will now be judged.

The opening announces it as if it had already happened. Babylon is captured. Bel is put to shame and Marduk is shattered, the two names for the great god of Babylon, whose temple stood at the heart of the city. A nation from the north will come against her. The language of the early chapters, where the enemy from the north threatened Jerusalem, is now aimed at Babylon. Some lines from chapter six that once described the army coming against the Daughter of Zion are repeated almost word for word, against the Daughter of Babylon.

Woven through the poem is the return of God's people. Israel and Judah will come together, weeping, asking the way to Zion. The Lord describes them as a scattered flock: first Assyria devoured them, then Nebuchadnezzar gnawed their bones. Babylon is called the hammer of the whole earth, now broken, and the exiles are told to flee from her, like the goats that lead the flock out of the fold.

=== 51
The oracle against Babylon continues, and now the attackers are named: the kings of the Medes, from the highlands east of Mesopotamia, with peoples from the northern mountains, Ararat, Minni and Ashkenaz. Babylon is called a gold cup in the Lord's hand that made the whole earth drunk, and Babylon's walls, gates and great river all appear in the poem. The book of Revelation would later draw on several of these images when it described the fall of another Babylon.

Babylon did fall, to Cyrus of Persia and the Medes, in the year five hundred and thirty-nine before Christ, about seventy years after Babylon's rise at Carchemish. In fact it was taken with little fighting, and the city faded over centuries rather than vanishing in a day. The poetry here speaks in the language of total judgement, as the prophets often did.

The chapter ends with a dated story, from the fourth year of Zedekiah, the year of the yoke and of Hananiah. Zedekiah travels to Babylon, and with him goes his quartermaster Seraiah son of Neriah, who is Baruch's brother. Jeremiah gives him a scroll containing these words against Babylon, with instructions about what to do with it once it has been read aloud. Then comes a short line that marks the end of Jeremiah's own words.

=== 52
Jeremiah's words have ended, but the book has one more chapter. It is a historical appendix, telling again the fall of Jerusalem, and it matches almost word for word the closing chapters of the second book of Kings. Whoever added it wanted the reader to finish Jeremiah with the plain record of how his prophecies came true.

The chapter opens with Zedekiah, twenty-one years old when he became king, reigning eleven years, and doing evil in the sight of the Lord. Then the siege, the breach in the wall, the flight and capture, and the judgement at Riblah, all told more fully than in chapter thirty-nine. A month after the wall fell, Nebuzaradan arrives and burns the house of the Lord. The bronze pillars, the great bronze Sea and its twelve bulls, made by Solomon nearly four hundred years before, are broken up and carried to Babylon, just as Jeremiah told the prophets who promised their return. The chapter also lists three deportations by number. The figures are smaller than those in the book of Kings, and they may count only certain groups.

The last scene takes place in Babylon, about twenty-five years later. Nebuchadnezzar has died, and his son Evil-merodach releases King Jehoiachin from prison and gives him a seat at the royal table. Clay tablets found in Babylon list oil rations for Jehoiachin, king of Judah, and his sons. The book ends there, with David's heir alive in exile, and the seventy years not yet complete.
