=== intro
Jeremiah lived through the end of a world. He was called as a young man, in the days of a reforming king, and he went on speaking for some forty years, until he had watched the Babylonian army burn Jerusalem, destroy the temple Solomon built, and carry the last of David's royal line away into exile. His book is one of the longest in the Bible, and it tells us more about the prophet himself than any other prophetic book: his calling, his loneliness, his arguments with God, and the price he paid for the message he carried.

He was the son of Hilkiah, from a family of priests in Anathoth, a village in the territory of Benjamin a short walk north-east of Jerusalem. The word of the Lord first came to him in the thirteenth year of King Josiah, about six hundred and twenty-seven years before Christ. He was told not to marry. He was beaten, put in the stocks, threatened with death, lowered into a muddy cistern and imprisoned by the king. Beside him for much of that time was his scribe and friend Baruch son of Neriah, who wrote down his words and many of the stories about him.

To understand Jeremiah, it helps to know the world he was born into. For more than a century the great power of the region had been Assyria. It had destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel about a hundred years before Jeremiah's call, and Judah had survived only as a subject state. Under King Manasseh, Josiah's grandfather, Jerusalem had filled with the worship of other gods. Now, in Jeremiah's youth, Assyria was collapsing. Its capital Nineveh fell to the Babylonians and the Medes in six hundred and twelve before Christ, and the land between the rivers and the Nile became a prize fought over by Egypt and the rising power of Babylon. In Jerusalem, Josiah seized the moment. A book of the law was found in the temple, and the king purged the land of idols and led the people in renewing the covenant. The second book of Kings tells that story, and its final chapters cover the same years as Jeremiah, seen from the palace rather than from the street.

Then Josiah was killed in battle against Pharaoh Neco of Egypt, and the last kings followed one another quickly. Josiah's son Jehoahaz, also called Shallum, reigned three months before Pharaoh took him away to Egypt. Pharaoh put another son, Jehoiakim, on the throne. He reigned eleven years, despised Jeremiah, and burned his scroll in the fire. During his reign, Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon defeated Egypt at Carchemish on the Euphrates, and Judah passed under Babylonian control. Jehoiakim submitted, then rebelled, and died as Babylon's army closed in. His young son Jehoiachin, also called Jeconiah or Coniah, reigned three months before surrendering, and he was taken to Babylon with the leading men of the city. Nebuchadnezzar then set up Zedekiah, another son of Josiah. Zedekiah was the last king of Judah. He respected Jeremiah enough to consult him in secret, but he was too afraid of his own officials to obey him. He rebelled too, the Babylonians returned, and after a long siege Jerusalem fell. After the fall, Babylon appointed a governor named Gedaliah, and when he was murdered, the survivors fled to Egypt, taking Jeremiah and Baruch with them.

Through all of this, Jeremiah's message cut against everything his hearers wanted to believe. They trusted that the temple made Jerusalem safe. Other prophets promised peace and a quick return of the exiles. Jeremiah said that Babylon was the Lord's instrument of judgement, that Judah should submit and live, and that the exile would last seventy years. To many, this sounded like treason. Yet his commission had two halves, to uproot and tear down, and also to build and to plant. The same book that announces the fall promises the return, and speaks of a new covenant written on the heart.

The book does not keep to the order of events, and one chapter may leap years forward or back from the last. It grew from the scrolls Jeremiah dictated to Baruch, with many more words added over the years. The first half gathers his messages to Judah and Jerusalem, mostly poetry and mostly undated, and among them his own anguished prayers. Then the stories take over: Jeremiah on trial for his life, a wooden yoke on his neck, a scroll cut up and fed to the king's fire, the prophet sinking into the mud of a cistern. At the heart of the book stand four chapters of hope. Then come the final siege, the burning of the city and the flight of the survivors, a series of oracles against the nations ending with Babylon itself, and a last chapter that tells the fall once more. Wherever the text names a king or a year, these introductions will say so.

Jeremiah's contemporaries included the prophets Zephaniah and Habakkuk, and Ezekiel, who was taken to Babylon in the first deportation. The short book of Lamentations, which follows Jeremiah in our Bibles, mourns the fallen city. Decades later, Daniel in Babylon would read Jeremiah's prophecy of seventy years, and the book of Ezra opens with the Persian king Cyrus sending the exiles home, so that the word of the Lord through Jeremiah might be fulfilled.

=== 1
The book's heading frames everything after it. These are the words of Jeremiah of Anathoth, spoken from the thirteenth year of Josiah, through the reign of Jehoiakim, to the eleventh year of Zedekiah, when Jerusalem went into exile. Before a word of the message is heard, the listener already knows how the story ends.

Then Jeremiah speaks for himself and tells how it began. The Lord tells him that he was known and set apart before he was formed in the womb, appointed a prophet to the nations. Jeremiah protests that he is only a youth and does not know how to speak. The Lord touches his mouth and puts his words there.

Two brief visions follow. The first turns on a play on words in Hebrew, where the word for an almond tree sounds like the word for watching. The second is a cooking pot, boiling and tilted toward Judah from the north. The chapter closes with a promise to the young prophet: kings, officials, priests and people will fight against him, but they will not overcome him.

=== 2
Jeremiah's first public message is addressed to Jerusalem, and it begins not with a threat but with a memory. The Lord recalls Israel's devotion in the early days, when she followed him through the wilderness like a young bride. Everything that follows is measured against that beginning.

These opening messages carry no date; they stand at the head of a ministry that began under Josiah. The poetry moves like a lawsuit, with God bringing a case against his people, and its images are some of the most memorable in the book: a fountain of living water abandoned for cracked cisterns, a choice vine gone wild, a restless young camel running in every direction.

Listen too for the politics of the day. Judah is looking for security among the great powers, toward Egypt, with its cities of Memphis and Tahpanhes and the waters of the Nile, and toward Assyria and the Euphrates. Egypt, the last lines warn, will disappoint Judah just as Assyria did.

=== 3
The charge of unfaithfulness continues in the language of marriage and divorce. Under the law of Moses, a man who divorced his wife could not take her back once she had married another. Judah has gone after many lovers. Can she simply return?

Midway through, the poetry gives way to prose, and for once the setting is named: the days of King Josiah. The Lord compares two sisters. Faithless Israel is the northern kingdom, conquered by Assyria a century earlier and scattered. Unfaithful Judah watched it happen and learned nothing; her return to the Lord was only pretence.

The surprise is where the message goes next. Jeremiah is to proclaim it toward the north, to the lost tribes of Israel: return, faithless children. The chapter looks ahead to a day when Israel and Judah walk together again, and ends with words of confession placed in the people's mouths.

=== 4
The call to return reaches its conclusion in the opening lines. If Israel will put away its idols and swear by the Lord in truth and justice, the nations themselves will find blessing in him. Judah is told to break up its unploughed ground and circumcise its hearts.

Then the alarm sounds. Blow the ram's horn, raise the signal flag, run for the fortified cities. A lion has come out of its thicket, a destroyer of nations is on the move. The invader from the north, glimpsed in the boiling pot of chapter one, is drawing closer, though he is not yet named. Reports arrive from Dan, at the far northern edge of the old kingdom, and then from the hills of Ephraim, tracing the route an army would take toward Jerusalem.

We also hear Jeremiah himself, in anguish at what he sees. In one passage he looks at the land and finds it formless and empty, without light, without people or birds, in words that deliberately echo the opening of Genesis. It is as if creation itself were being undone.

=== 5
Jeremiah is sent through the streets and squares of Jerusalem on a search. If he can find one person who acts justly and seeks the truth, the Lord will pardon the city. He looks first among the poor, and assumes their failure comes from ignorance. Then he goes to the powerful, who surely know the way of the Lord, and finds that they too have thrown off the yoke.

The chapter gathers its charges around this failed search: idolatry, adultery, the rich growing fat on deceit while the orphan and the needy go undefended. The same question comes back more than once: should I not punish them for these things?

The coming enemy is described more fully now, a distant and ancient nation whose language Judah will not understand, its quivers like open graves. Yet a limit is set: the Lord will not make a full end of his people. The last word is a disturbing one. The prophets speak lies, the priests rule as they please, and the people love it so.

=== 6
The warning sharpens into the sound of an approaching siege. This time it is the people of Benjamin, Jeremiah's own tribe, who are told to flee from Jerusalem. Trumpets are to sound in Tekoa and a signal rise over Beth-haccherem, as though the alarm were passing from hilltop to hilltop.

We overhear the enemy commanders, eager to attack at noon and impatient when the light begins to fail. Trees are cut down for siege ramps. Inside the city, prophet and priest offer easy comfort, peace, peace, when there is no peace, words that will be heard again in chapter eight.

Two images stand out. At a crossroads the people are urged to ask for the ancient paths and walk in the good way, and they refuse. And at the end, Jeremiah is appointed an assayer, testing his people as a metalworker tests ore. The bellows blow and the fire burns, but the dross will not separate from the silver.

=== 7
The poetry gives way to prose, and Jeremiah is given a place and an audience. He is to stand in the gate of the temple and speak to the worshippers as they come in.

This is often called the Temple Sermon, and chapter twenty-six will tell of the day Jeremiah gave this same warning in the temple court, at the beginning of Jehoiakim's reign, and of the uproar that followed. The worshippers repeat their watchword like a charm: the temple of the Lord, the temple of the Lord, the temple of the Lord. As long as the house of the Lord stands, they believe, Jerusalem cannot fall. Jeremiah points them north to Shiloh, where the tabernacle stood in the days of Eli and Samuel, and where nothing now remained. Centuries later, Jesus would quote this sermon's den of robbers when he cleared the temple courts.

The charge then widens. Families bake cakes for the Queen of Heaven, a goddess of the region. In the Valley of Ben-hinnom, just outside the walls, children are burned at a place called Topheth. And Jeremiah is told something no prophet wants to hear: do not pray for this people.

=== 8
The Temple Sermon ends with a grim scene. When the invader comes, the bones of kings, priests, prophets and people will be dragged from their graves and spread out before the sun, moon and stars they worshipped.

Then the chapter returns to poetry, and the voices begin to mingle. Sometimes the Lord speaks, sometimes the people, sometimes Jeremiah, and the listener has to follow the changes. The stork and the swallow know their seasons, the Lord says, but his people do not know his ways. Those who claim to be wise because they have the law have let the pen of the scribes turn it into a lie.

The sound of enemy horses is heard again from Dan. The last verses belong to Jeremiah, and they are full of grief. He hears his people crying from a distant land, asking whether the Lord is no longer in Zion. The harvest is past, the summer has ended, and still they are not saved. Then he asks whether there is no balm in Gilead, the region east of the Jordan known for its healing resin.

=== 9
The grief runs straight on into this chapter. Jeremiah wishes his head were a spring of water, so that he could weep day and night for his people, and in the same breath wishes he could leave them for a lodging place in the desert. Lines like these have earned him the name the weeping prophet.

The Lord describes a society in which no one can trust a neighbour or a brother, where tongues are bent like bows to shoot lies. Jerusalem will become a heap of ruins, a haunt of jackals. The professional mourning women, hired to lead the lament at funerals, are summoned and told to teach their daughters to wail, because death has climbed in through the windows.

Near the end comes a saying that stands apart from its surroundings: let not the wise boast in their wisdom, nor the strong in their strength, but in knowing the Lord. Paul would draw on it when he wrote to the church in Corinth. Its final lines set Judah beside Egypt, Edom, Ammon and Moab, circumcised in body, uncircumcised in heart.

=== 10
The approaching army fades for a moment, and the Lord is set beside the gods of the nations. Judah is told not to learn the ways of the nations, nor to be terrified by signs in the heavens, which the stargazers of the ancient world studied anxiously.

What follows is a satire on idols. A tree is cut from the forest, shaped by a craftsman, overlaid with silver from Tarshish and gold from Uphaz, dressed in blue and purple, and nailed down so that it will not topple. Like a scarecrow in a cucumber field, it cannot speak and must be carried. Against it stands the living God, who made the earth by his power and brings the wind from his storehouses. One verse is written not in Hebrew but in Aramaic, the international language of the day, as if it were a sentence to be repeated to foreign neighbours.

The last verses return abruptly to the crisis. Pack your bundle, you who live under siege. Jeremiah grieves over a tent torn down and children gone, and prays to be corrected with justice, not in anger.

=== 11
The subject now is the covenant made at the exodus, when the Lord brought his people out of the iron furnace of Egypt. Its terms were simple, obey me and you will be my people, and its curses were plain. Jeremiah is told to proclaim its words in the towns of Judah and the streets of Jerusalem.

In Jeremiah's own lifetime, King Josiah had found a book of the law in the temple and led the nation in renewing this covenant. The point of the chapter is that it has been broken all the same. Judah's gods are as many as its towns.

Then the chapter turns personal. Jeremiah discovers that he has been like a lamb led unknowingly to the slaughter. There is a plot to kill him, and it comes from the men of Anathoth, his own home town. This is the first of several passages, often called his confessions, in which Jeremiah pours out his heart to God.

=== 12
Jeremiah's complaint continues, and it is a question that has troubled believers in every age. He grants that the Lord is righteous, and then presses his case: why does the way of the wicked prosper? The Lord's reply offers no explanation, only a warning that things will get harder. If you have raced with men on foot and they have worn you out, how will you compete with horses? Even Jeremiah's own family have betrayed him.

Then the voice changes, and the Lord himself grieves. He has forsaken his house and given the beloved of his soul into the hands of her enemies. Many shepherds, the rulers of the nations, have trampled his vineyard into a wilderness.

Last comes a word about Judah's hostile neighbours. They too will be uprooted. Yet there is a surprising promise: if those nations learn the ways of God's people and swear by his name, they will be built up among them.

=== 13
Jeremiah is given the first of several acted parables, signs performed as well as spoken. He buys a linen loincloth, wears it, and then hides it in a crack in the rocks at a place called Perath. The name is the Hebrew word for the Euphrates, far to the east. Many days later he digs it up, and it is ruined. So the Lord will ruin the pride of Judah, a people he had bound to himself as closely as a garment, and who would not listen.

A saying about wine jars becomes a picture of a nation staggering in drunken confusion. Then comes a word for the king and the queen mother: take a lowly seat, for your glorious crowns have fallen from your heads. Jeremiah, for his part, will weep in secret for the Lord's flock, carried away captive.

Then a single proverb measures how deep Judah's habits run. Can the Ethiopian change his skin, or the leopard its spots?

=== 14
A new heading introduces a word about the drought. In a land that depended on the rains of autumn and spring, a failed season was a catastrophe, and the poem pictures it closely: servants coming back from the cisterns with empty jars, farmers covering their heads over cracked ground, a doe abandoning her newborn fawn because there is no grass.

What follows is a kind of dialogue. The people pray, confessing their sins and appealing to the Lord as the hope of Israel. The Lord answers that they love to wander, and tells Jeremiah once again not to pray for them. Jeremiah protests that the other prophets are promising peace. The Lord's reply is blunt: he did not send them, and the sword and famine they deny will fall on them too.

A second prayer follows, an appeal not to despise Zion or break the covenant, and a confession that only the Lord can send the rain.

=== 15
The Lord answers the people's prayer, and the answer is no. Even if Moses and Samuel stood before him, the great intercessors of Israel's past, his heart would not turn toward this people. The reason looks back two generations, to King Manasseh, whose long reign filled Jerusalem with idolatry and innocent blood. The second book of Kings makes the same judgement, that even Josiah's reforms could not undo what Manasseh had done.

Jeremiah's own voice returns, in another lament. He wishes his mother had never borne him, a man the whole land contends with. He remembers how he received God's words with joy and ate them, and how he has sat alone ever since because the Lord's hand was on him. Then he says something startling: the Lord has become to him like a deceptive brook that dries up when it is needed.

The Lord answers with a rebuke and a renewal. If Jeremiah returns, he will stand before God again, and the promise of his call is repeated: a fortified wall of bronze, against which his enemies will fight and not prevail.

=== 16
Jeremiah's own life is now to become part of the message. He is not to marry or have children in this land, because the sons and daughters born there will die of disease, sword and famine. In a world where marriage and children were expected of almost everyone, this alone made him a living sign.

He is also forbidden to enter a house of mourning to comfort the bereaved, or a house of feasting to celebrate. The ordinary rhythms of grief and joy are about to be withdrawn from the land, and his absence from both is meant to be noticed. When people ask what they have done, he is to tell them.

In the middle of the chapter a promise breaks in. A day will come when people no longer swear by the Lord who brought Israel out of Egypt, but by the Lord who brought them back from the land of the north. The return from exile will be a new exodus. By its close, the nations themselves are coming to the Lord, confessing that their fathers inherited only lies.

=== 17
Judah's sin is written with an iron stylus, engraved with a diamond point on the tablets of the heart and the horns of the altars. The chapter begins with that image of something cut too deep to erase, and then gathers a series of shorter sayings.

One will sound familiar to anyone who knows the first psalm: the one who trusts in mere human strength is like a shrub in the desert, while the one who trusts in the Lord is like a tree planted by the water, green even in drought. Another speaks of the human heart, deceitful above all things, which only the Lord can search.

Then Jeremiah prays again. His opponents are taunting him that his words have not come true, and he asks to be healed, saved and vindicated. The chapter closes with a sermon preached in the city gates, where porters carried their loads in and out, calling Jerusalem to keep the Sabbath holy. The city's future, the Lord says, depends on whether they will listen.

=== 18
Jeremiah goes down to the potter's house and watches a craftsman at the wheel. The pot he is shaping goes wrong in his hands, so he presses the clay down and makes something else. The Lord draws the lesson. Israel is clay in his hand. If he announces judgement on a nation and it turns from its evil, he relents. If he promises good and it turns to evil, he reconsiders the good.

So the message to Judah is: turn now from your evil ways. The people's answer is recorded as bluntly as the call. It is hopeless, they say; we will follow our own plans.

Then the opposition to Jeremiah organises itself. The nation will not miss one troublesome prophet, his enemies reason, since there will always be priests, wise men and prophets. Let us attack him with our tongues. Jeremiah's response is one of his fiercest prayers, asking the Lord to deal with them in his anger. His prayers in this book are not tidied up.

=== 19
The potter's clay becomes a finished jar. Jeremiah buys an earthenware flask, takes some of the elders and senior priests out to the Valley of Ben-hinnom by the Potsherd Gate, and there, overlooking Topheth, where children had been burned in sacrifice, he speaks.

The valley, he says, will be renamed the Valley of Slaughter, and he describes in terrible terms what a siege of Jerusalem will bring. Then he smashes the jar in front of the men who came with him. A pot still soft on the wheel can be reshaped. A fired jar, once broken, cannot be mended.

Jeremiah then walks back into the city, stands in the court of the temple and repeats the warning to everyone there. It is a public act in the most public place in Jerusalem, and the next chapter tells us what it cost him.

=== 20
Jeremiah's words in the temple court reach Pashhur son of Immer, the priest in charge of order in the house of the Lord. He has Jeremiah beaten and put in the stocks overnight at the Upper Gate of Benjamin.

When he is released in the morning, Jeremiah gives Pashhur a new name, Magor-missabib, terror on every side, and for the first time in the book the danger from the north is named outright: the Lord will hand Judah over to the king of Babylon. Babylon, the ancient city on the Euphrates, has risen under a new dynasty to take Assyria's place as the great power of the age, and from here on it stands at the centre of Jeremiah's message.

The second half of the chapter is the last and darkest of his laments. He accuses the Lord of deceiving and overpowering him. He has become a laughing stock, and when he tries to keep silent, the word burns like fire shut up in his bones. He sings praise for a moment, and then curses the day he was born. No answer is given.

=== 21
The book now leaps forward to the last years of the kingdom. Jehoiakim has come and gone, and so has his son Jehoiachin. On the throne is Zedekiah, placed there by Nebuchadnezzar himself. Zedekiah has rebelled, and the Babylonian army is at the walls.

The king sends the priest Zephaniah and another Pashhur, son of Malchijah, not the man who put Jeremiah in the stocks. Their hope is that the Lord will do one of his wonders, as in the old days, and Nebuchadnezzar will withdraw. More than a century earlier, in Hezekiah's day, an Assyrian army camped outside these same walls had suddenly gone home.

Jeremiah's answer offers no such hope. The Lord himself will fight against the city. To the people he sets out the way of life and the way of death: stay and die, or go out and surrender to the Chaldeans, as the Babylonians were also called, and live. A word to the royal house follows, to do justice every morning, and it opens a section about the kings of Judah.

=== 22
Jeremiah is sent down from the temple to the royal palace, to address the king of Judah in his own house. Do justice, rescue the oppressed, protect the foreigner, the orphan and the widow. If the kings obey, David's line will go on. If not, the palace will become a ruin.

What follows is a series of words about the last kings, gathered here though they come from different years. Josiah, the good king, is dead. His son Shallum, called Jehoahaz in the book of Kings, reigned three months before Pharaoh took him to Egypt; weep for him, Jeremiah says, for he will never return. Next is Jehoiakim, another son of Josiah, who builds himself a palace panelled in cedar and painted red with forced labour, and refuses to pay the workers. The Lord sets him beside his father, who defended the poor and needy, and asks whether that is not what it means to know him.

Last comes Jehoiakim's son, here called Coniah, also known as Jeconiah and Jehoiachin. Even if he were the signet ring on the Lord's right hand, he would be pulled off and thrown into a foreign land with his mother, and no son of his would sit on David's throne.

=== 23
The words against the kings end with a charge against the shepherds, the rulers who have scattered God's flock. Then comes a promise. The Lord himself will gather the remnant from the lands of exile, and he will raise up for David a righteous Branch, a king who will reign wisely and do justice. His name will be The Lord Our Righteousness.

The name carried a sting. The reigning king, Zedekiah, bore a name meaning the Lord is righteous, and fell far short of it. The true king was still to come. Christians have long read this promise as pointing to Jesus, the Son of David.

The rest of the chapter concerns the prophets. Jeremiah is shaken, like a man drunk with wine, at what he sees among them. They promise peace to those who despise the Lord, trade dreams and borrow one another's words, and none of them has stood in the council of the Lord. Finally Jeremiah plays on a Hebrew word that means both an oracle and a burden. People ask, what is the burden of the Lord? The answer is that they are.

=== 24
This vision comes from the early years of Zedekiah's reign. Nebuchadnezzar has already taken the young king Jeconiah to Babylon, with the leading officials and the skilled craftsmen and smiths. Among those taken, though Jeremiah does not mention him, was a young priest named Ezekiel.

Jeremiah sees two baskets of figs set in front of the temple. One holds very good figs, like the first ripe fruit of the season. The other holds figs so bad they cannot be eaten.

The meaning reverses what everyone in Jerusalem assumed. Those left in the city might easily have thought themselves the favoured ones, spared while others were punished. The Lord says the opposite. The exiles in Babylon are the good figs. He will watch over them, bring them back and give them a heart to know him. Zedekiah, his officials and those who remain in the land, with those living in Egypt, are the bad figs. The future of God's people, strange as it sounded, lay with the exiles.

=== 25
This chapter is carefully dated, and the date matters. It is the fourth year of Jehoiakim, the first year of Nebuchadnezzar, the year Nebuchadnezzar defeated Egypt at Carchemish and Babylon became master of the region. Jeremiah has been prophesying for twenty-three years, and he looks back over all of them. You have not listened.

For the first time the length of the servitude is given. Judah and the surrounding nations will serve the king of Babylon seventy years, and then Babylon itself will be judged. Near the end of those years, Daniel will be found in Babylon reading this prophecy and counting.

The second half widens to the whole world. Jeremiah is handed a cup of the wine of the Lord's wrath, and every nation must drink it: Jerusalem first, then Egypt, the Philistine cities, Edom, Moab and Ammon, Tyre and Sidon, the desert tribes, Elam and Media, and last of all the king of Sheshach, a coded name for Babylon. Many of these nations will hear their sentence one by one near the end of the book.
