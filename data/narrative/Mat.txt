=== intro
Between the last page of the Old Testament and the first page of the New lie about four hundred years. When the prophet Malachi spoke, the Jews who had come home from exile were a small and weary community gathered around a modest rebuilt temple, living quietly as a province of the Persian Empire. When Matthew's story opens, the same land has a king named Herod, answers to Rome, speaks Aramaic in its villages and Greek in its cities, and is waiting, with mounting impatience, for God to act. Matthew stands first in the New Testament, and to enter his world we have to cross those silent centuries.

Persia fell to Alexander the Great of Macedon in the three hundred and thirties before Christ. Alexander died young, but the Greek language and way of life he carried east outlasted him: Greek cities, theatres and gymnasiums, and a common Greek tongue spoken from Egypt to Mesopotamia. His generals divided his empire between them, and Judea, caught in the middle, was ruled first by the Ptolemies of Egypt and then by the Seleucid kings of Syria. In Egypt, Jewish scholars translated their Scriptures into Greek, the version later called the Septuagint, and it became the Bible of Jews scattered across the Greek-speaking world.

Under the Seleucid king Antiochus the Fourth, pressure turned to persecution. In the one hundred and sixties before Christ he banned circumcision and the Sabbath and set up a pagan altar in the temple itself. An old priest named Mattathias and his sons rose in revolt, and under Judas, nicknamed Maccabeus, the Hammer, they drove out the Syrian forces and cleansed and rededicated the temple, an event Jews still remember at Hanukkah, the Feast of Dedication. Judas's family, the Hasmoneans, became priest-kings, and for about a century the Jews were independent. But the dynasty grew worldly and divided, and in sixty-three before Christ the Roman general Pompey, drawn in to settle a quarrel between two rival brothers, marched into Jerusalem.

Rome governed through local strongmen, and the strongest was Herod. His family came from Idumea, the old land of Edom, and many Jews never fully accepted him. The Roman Senate named him king of the Jews, and he held the throne for more than thirty years. He was a brilliant builder and a brutal ruler. He rebuilt the temple on a magnificent scale, raised the port of Caesarea and fortresses such as Masada, and put to death a wife and three of his own sons when he suspected them of plotting. Jesus is born in the last years of his reign. When Herod died, Rome divided his kingdom among his sons: Archelaus in Judea, Herod Antipas in Galilee and in Perea across the Jordan, Philip in the lands to the north-east. Archelaus ruled so badly that Rome soon removed him, and Judea passed to Roman governors, among them Pontius Pilate.

The religious landscape was crowded. The temple was run by the priests, and the high priest and his council, the Sanhedrin, held real power under Rome. Many of the leading priestly families were Sadducees, conservative aristocrats who accepted only the written law as binding and denied any resurrection of the dead. The Pharisees were a devout and largely lay movement, respected in the towns and villages, who sought to bring the law into every corner of daily life and kept a growing body of oral rules called the tradition of the elders. Scribes, the experts in the law, are often found alongside them. The Essenes withdrew into communities of their own, and the Dead Sea Scrolls found at Qumran are linked with them. Others dreamed of driving out Rome by the sword. In every town there was a synagogue where the Scriptures were read on the Sabbath, and in many hearts there was a hope for the Messiah, the anointed king from the line of David who would set Israel free.

The land itself matters. Judea lay in the south, with Jerusalem in its hills. Samaria lay in the middle, home to a people whom most Jews avoided. Galilee lay in the north, green and fertile and crowded with villages, around a freshwater lake the Gospels call the Sea of Galilee, where fishing was a living. Jerusalem regarded Galilee as provincial, and it is in a Galilean village, Nazareth, that Jesus grows up.

The Gospel is the work of Matthew, the tax collector whom Jesus called to be one of the Twelve, and the church has known it by his name from its earliest days. He wrote for people steeped in the Jewish Scriptures. Matthew quotes the prophets again and again, often with the words this was to fulfil what was spoken, and he usually prefers the phrase kingdom of heaven, in keeping with the Jewish habit of reverently avoiding the name of God.

The book alternates between story and teaching. Five great blocks of Jesus' words stand out, each closing with a phrase like when Jesus had finished saying these things: the Sermon on the Mount, the sending out of the Twelve, the parables of the kingdom, instructions for life among the disciples, and the long discourse on the end of the age. Around them the story moves from the birth in Bethlehem to the ministry in Galilee, then to a turning point at Caesarea Philippi when Jesus begins to speak of going to Jerusalem to suffer, then south along the road to the city, and into the last week, the Passover, the trial before Pilate, the cross and the empty tomb. The Gospel opens with a child to be called Immanuel, God with us, and it closes on a mountain in Galilee with the promise, I am with you always.

=== 1
Matthew begins where any Jewish reader would expect a claim to kingship to begin: with a family tree. Jesus is introduced as the son of David and the son of Abraham, the two men to whom God's great promises were made, and the line runs from one to the other and on down through the kings of Judah to the exile in Babylon and beyond.

The list is carefully shaped into three sets of fourteen generations, and it holds some surprises. Four women appear besides Mary: Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, and Uriah's wife, Bathsheba, though Matthew does not name her. Each has an irregular or foreign story behind her.

Then the birth itself, told from Joseph's side. Mary is pledged to him, a betrothal as binding as marriage, which only a divorce could end, when she is found to be with child. Joseph is a righteous man, unwilling to disgrace her, and he resolves to end the betrothal quietly. An angel speaks to him in a dream, the first of several in these chapters, and the child is given two names, Jesus, which means the Lord saves, and Immanuel, God with us. Luke tells this story from Mary's side.

=== 2
Jesus has been born in Bethlehem, the small town of David's family a few miles south of Jerusalem. The king on the throne is Herod the Great, now old, suspicious and dangerous, a man who had already killed members of his own family to protect his crown.

Into his capital come the Magi, scholars of the stars from the east. Matthew does not say how many there were; the tradition of three comes from their three gifts. Their question, where is the one born king of the Jews, could hardly have been put to a worse person.

What follows is a story of rescue and of grief. Joseph is again guided by dreams, and the little family escapes to Egypt, which had long been a refuge for Jews in trouble. Matthew sees in each turn of the story the echo of an older prophecy, and he quotes them one after another. By the last scene, Herod is dead. His son Archelaus now rules Judea, and Joseph, afraid of him, takes the family north to the obscure Galilean village of Nazareth.

=== 3
Matthew passes over the years of Jesus' growing up in silence. Luke tells us he was about thirty when the next events took place. The scene moves to the wilderness of Judea, the barren country falling away toward the Jordan valley and the Dead Sea, where a new prophet has appeared.

John the Baptist dresses like Elijah, in camel's hair with a leather belt, and he lives on locusts and wild honey. His message is urgent: repent, because the kingdom of heaven is near. Crowds go out from Jerusalem and all Judea to be baptised by him in the Jordan, confessing their sins. Among them come Pharisees and Sadducees, making their first appearance in the Gospel, and John's words to them are fierce.

John speaks of one coming after him, far more powerful. Then Jesus himself arrives from Galilee and asks to be baptised. John protests, but Jesus insists. As he comes up out of the water, the heavens open, the Spirit descends like a dove, and a voice from heaven speaks. It is the first time in Matthew that God himself speaks, and his words are about who Jesus is.

=== 4
The voice from heaven has just declared Jesus to be God's beloved Son. Now the Spirit leads him into the wilderness to be tempted by the devil. For forty days and nights he fasts, and the number recalls Israel's forty years in the desert after the exodus. Three times the devil tests him, twice beginning, if you are the Son of God, and three times Jesus answers with words from Deuteronomy, the book of Moses' speeches to Israel on the edge of the promised land.

Then the scene shifts north. John has been arrested, and Jesus withdraws to Galilee. He leaves Nazareth and settles in Capernaum, a fishing town on the north-west shore of the Sea of Galilee, which will become the base for his ministry. Matthew notes the words of Isaiah about the land of Zebulun and Naphtali, Galilee of the Gentiles, where a great light would dawn.

From that time on, Matthew says, Jesus begins to preach. Beside the lake he calls his first followers, two pairs of fishermen brothers, and by the end of the chapter crowds are gathering from every direction.

=== 5
The crowds have come from Galilee, from Jerusalem and Judea, from the Decapolis and from beyond the Jordan. Jesus goes up on the mountain and sits down, the usual posture of a Jewish teacher, and his disciples gather close. This is the first of the five great blocks of teaching in Matthew's Gospel, and it runs through chapter seven. It has come to be called the Sermon on the Mount.

It opens with the Beatitudes, a series of sayings that each begin with the word blessed, and they describe a people quite unlike the powerful and the self-assured. Then he takes up the question his teaching raises: what is his relationship to the Law of Moses? His answer is that he has come not to abolish it but to fulfil it.

The rest of the chapter is built around a striking refrain. You have heard that it was said, Jesus begins, and then he quotes the law or a common teaching about it, and adds, but I tell you. He speaks with an authority that does not appeal to any other teacher, and the subjects are close to home: anger, desire, marriage, oaths, revenge, and love for an enemy.

=== 6
Jesus is still on the mountainside, and the sermon moves from the law to the practice of devotion. Three things marked a devout Jewish life in his day: giving to the poor, prayer and fasting. Jesus takes each in turn, and each time the contrast is the same, between doing it to be seen by others and doing it for the Father who sees in secret. The hypocrites he describes are, literally, play-actors, the word used for performers on a Greek stage.

At the centre of the chapter, in the section on prayer, is the short prayer Jesus gives his disciples, which has been prayed by Christians ever since and is known as the Lord's Prayer. It begins by addressing God as Father.

The second half of the chapter turns to money and worry, matters that touched everyone who heard it in a land of small farmers, fishermen and day labourers. Jesus draws on the world in front of them, the birds overhead and the wild flowers on the hillside, and he contrasts Solomon in all his splendour with the lilies of the field.

=== 7
This is the final part of the Sermon on the Mount. Jesus turns to judging others, and the image he chooses comes straight from the carpenter's workshop: a speck of sawdust in a brother's eye, and a whole beam in one's own.

The sayings that follow are short and memorable, among them ask and it will be given, and the rule of doing to others what you would have them do to you. Then the sermon begins to draw to its close with a series of contrasts, each forcing a choice. There are two gates and two roads, two kinds of tree and their fruit, and finally two builders, one who builds on rock and one on sand, in a land where a dry riverbed could become a torrent after a winter storm.

When Jesus finishes, the crowds are astonished, Matthew says, because Jesus teaches as one who has authority, not as their scribes. The scribes of the day taught largely by citing the opinions of earlier teachers. Jesus has cited no one but Scripture and himself.

=== 8
Jesus comes down from the mountain, and Matthew turns from his words to his deeds. Chapters eight and nine gather a series of miracles, showing the same authority at work that the crowds sensed in the sermon.

The first to come is a man with leprosy, a skin disease that made a person ritually unclean and cut him off from community and worship. Jesus touches him. Back in Capernaum a centurion asks for help, a Roman officer and a Gentile, and his understanding of authority amazes Jesus. Then, in Peter's own house, Peter's mother-in-law is lying ill with a fever.

The chapter moves outward. Would-be followers learn what following will cost. Jesus and the disciples set out across the lake, which is notorious for sudden, violent storms when the wind drops down from the surrounding hills. On the far side they land in the region of the Gadarenes, on the eastern shore, largely Gentile country, as the herd of pigs makes clear. Mark and Luke tell this encounter with a single man; Matthew tells of two.

=== 9
Jesus crosses the lake again and returns to Capernaum, which Matthew now calls his own town. The miracles continue, but now a new element enters the story: open disagreement. When Jesus tells a paralysed man that his sins are forgiven, some of the scribes say to themselves that this man is blaspheming.

Then comes a call that touches the book itself, because the man concerned is the writer of this Gospel. Jesus sees a man named Matthew sitting at the tax booth and tells him to follow. Tax collectors worked for the authorities, often took more than was due, and were despised as collaborators, and Jesus goes on to eat with a crowd of them. The Pharisees object, and the disciples of John the Baptist raise a question about fasting.

A run of healings follows: a synagogue leader's daughter who has died, a woman who has suffered bleeding for twelve years, two blind men, and a man unable to speak. The reactions divide. The crowds are amazed. The Pharisees suggest that Jesus works by the power of the prince of demons. And Jesus, seeing the crowds harassed and helpless like sheep without a shepherd, speaks of a harvest that needs workers.

=== 10
The harvest is plentiful and the workers few, and Jesus now answers his own words. He calls his twelve disciples and sends them out. This is the second of the five blocks of teaching in Matthew.

For the first time the twelve are named, as apostles, which means those who are sent. They are listed in pairs: Simon Peter and Andrew, James and John, the sons of Zebedee, and the rest, including Matthew the tax collector, who had served the authorities, and Simon the Zealot, whose name speaks of fierce zeal. The list ends, ominously, with Judas Iscariot.

Their first mission is limited to the lost sheep of Israel, and they are to travel light, depending on the hospitality of the villages they visit. But as Jesus goes on, his words reach far beyond this first short mission. He speaks of councils and synagogues, governors and kings, of families divided and of disciples hated for his name. The instructions look ahead to a time when following him will be costly in ways the twelve cannot yet imagine.

=== 11
The twelve have been sent out, and Jesus continues teaching in the towns of Galilee. Meanwhile John the Baptist is in prison, held by Herod Antipas; the historian Josephus places him in the fortress of Machaerus, east of the Dead Sea. From his cell John sends a question, and it is a searching one. Is Jesus really the one who was to come?

Jesus answers by pointing to what is happening, in words that echo Isaiah's pictures of the age to come. Then he speaks to the crowds about John, identifying him as the messenger foretold by Malachi and the Elijah who was to come, the figure whose return Malachi had promised in the very last lines of the Old Testament.

The mood darkens as Jesus turns to the towns where most of his miracles have been done, Chorazin, Bethsaida and Capernaum itself, all within a few miles of one another around the northern end of the lake. They have seen his mighty works, and still they have not repented. Yet the chapter closes with one of the gentlest invitations in the Gospels, addressed to the weary and burdened.

=== 12
Jesus has just offered rest to the weary. Now, fittingly, the conflict that has been simmering breaks out over the day of rest itself, the Sabbath. The Pharisees had built up detailed rules about what counted as work on the Sabbath, and plucking ears of grain counted. Jesus answers them from Scripture, with the example of David, and claims a remarkable authority: the Son of Man is Lord of the Sabbath.

A healing in a synagogue brings matters to a head, and for the first time Matthew records that the Pharisees go out and plot how to kill Jesus. Jesus withdraws, and Matthew quotes at length from Isaiah's song of the gentle servant of the Lord.

The rest of the chapter is a sharp exchange. When Jesus heals a blind and mute man, the crowds begin to wonder aloud whether he could be the Son of David, and the Pharisees reply that he drives out demons by Beelzebul, the prince of demons. Jesus answers with strong words, and when he is asked for a sign, he points to Jonah. At the very end his mother and brothers arrive, wanting to speak with him.

=== 13
On the same day, Jesus goes out to the lakeshore. The crowd is so large that he gets into a boat and teaches from the water, while the people stand along the shore. This is the third of Matthew's five blocks of teaching, and it is made up of parables, short stories drawn from farming, baking, fishing and trade.

The first is the parable of the sower, which Jesus later explains to his disciples. In between, they ask him why he speaks to the crowds in parables, and his answer, drawing on Isaiah, explains much about the division that has opened up in the previous chapters. More parables follow, each opening with the words, the kingdom of heaven is like. An enemy sows weeds among a farmer's wheat. A tiny mustard seed grows into a tree where the birds come to nest. A woman works leaven all through her flour. A man stumbles on treasure hidden in a field, a merchant finds one pearl of great price, and fishermen sort their catch on the shore. Several look to a final separation at the end of the age.

Then Jesus returns to his hometown, Nazareth, and teaches in the synagogue. The people there have known him since childhood. They name his mother and his brothers, James, Joseph, Simon and Judas, and they take offence at him.

=== 14
Matthew now brings Herod Antipas into the foreground. He is the son of Herod the Great, and he rules Galilee and Perea as tetrarch, a lesser title than king, though Matthew also calls him king. Hearing about Jesus, he tells his servants that this must be John the Baptist, risen from the dead.

That leads Matthew to tell, as a flashback, how John died. John had condemned Herod's marriage to Herodias, who had been the wife of his brother Philip. The setting is Herod's birthday banquet, and the scene is grim.

When Jesus hears the news, he withdraws by boat to a solitary place, but the crowds follow him around the shore. There follows the feeding of the five thousand, the only miracle apart from the resurrection that all four Gospels record. Then Jesus sends the disciples back across the lake while he goes up the mountain alone to pray. Between three and six in the morning, the fourth watch of the night, with the boat struggling against the wind, they see him coming to them on the water. By morning they have landed at Gennesaret, on the western shore.

=== 15
Jesus is back on the western shore of the lake, and a delegation of Pharisees and scribes arrives from Jerusalem, a sign that the authorities in the capital are now taking notice. Their complaint is that his disciples break the tradition of the elders by not washing their hands before eating, a ritual washing rather than a matter of hygiene. Jesus turns the charge back on them and speaks about where defilement really comes from.

Then he leaves Jewish territory altogether and goes north-west to the district of Tyre and Sidon, the old Phoenician cities on the Mediterranean coast. There a Canaanite woman comes to him, crying out for her daughter. Matthew uses the ancient name for Israel's neighbours, the people of the land before Joshua, and the exchange that follows is one of the most surprising conversations in the Gospels.

Jesus returns to the Sea of Galilee and climbs a hillside, and crowds bring their sick to him. Then comes a second feeding, this time of four thousand, in a remote place, after the people have been with him for three days.

=== 16
The Pharisees, joined now by the Sadducees, two groups who rarely agreed on anything, come to test Jesus by asking for a sign from heaven. He refuses, and as the disciples cross the lake he warns them about the leaven of these leaders, a warning they at first misunderstand.

Then Jesus takes them far to the north, to the region of Caesarea Philippi. The city lay at the foot of Mount Hermon, near one of the springs of the Jordan, and had been rebuilt by Herod's son Philip and named in honour of Caesar. It was a place of pagan shrines and imperial power. There, away from the crowds, Jesus asks his disciples who people say he is, and then who they say he is. Peter answers.

This is the turning point of the Gospel. Matthew marks it with the same words he used when Jesus began preaching back in chapter four: from that time on. From now on, Jesus begins to tell his disciples plainly that he must go to Jerusalem, suffer at the hands of the elders, chief priests and scribes, be killed, and be raised on the third day. Peter's reaction shows how unthinkable this is to them.

=== 17
Six days after Peter's confession, Jesus takes Peter, James and John up a high mountain. There the three see Jesus transfigured, his face shining like the sun, and they see Moses and Elijah, the great figures of the Law and the Prophets, talking with him. The voice from heaven heard at his baptism speaks again.

On the way down the disciples ask about Elijah, and Jesus links him once more with John the Baptist. At the foot of the mountain the scene changes sharply: a father pleads for his son, whom the other disciples have been unable to help.

Back in Galilee, Jesus repeats his warning about what awaits him in Jerusalem, and this time the disciples are filled with grief. In Capernaum there is a small episode about the temple tax, an annual payment of two drachmas expected of every Jewish man for the upkeep of the temple, and Peter is sent fishing.

=== 18
The disciples come to Jesus with a question about status: who is the greatest in the kingdom of heaven? His answer opens the fourth of Matthew's five blocks of teaching, and it is about how his followers are to live together.

He begins by placing a little child in the middle of them, in a world where children had little standing. From there he speaks of the little ones, the vulnerable among his followers, of the danger of causing them to stumble, and of a shepherd who leaves ninety-nine sheep to search for one that has gone astray.

The second half turns to what happens when one disciple sins against another, and Jesus describes a process of seeking reconciliation, first privately and then with others. Here, for the second time in Matthew, he uses the word church, the only Gospel in which the word appears. Then Peter asks how many times he must forgive, and suggests as many as seven. Jesus replies with a parable about a servant forgiven a debt of ten thousand talents, a sum beyond imagining, who will not forgive a fellow servant a hundred denarii.

=== 19
With his teaching on life together complete, Jesus leaves Galilee, and he will not return there before the cross. He goes south into the region of Judea beyond the Jordan, the territory east of the river known as Perea. Galilean pilgrims often travelled to Jerusalem this way, down the Jordan valley, and from here the road leads on toward the city.

Pharisees come to test him on divorce. It was a live question among the teachers of the day, some allowing divorce for almost any reason, others only for serious wrongdoing. Jesus takes them back beyond Moses to the beginning, to the creation of man and woman in Genesis.

Then little children are brought to him for a blessing, and the disciples try to turn them away. A rich young man asks what good thing he must do to gain eternal life, and he goes away grieving at the answer. Jesus' words about a camel and the eye of a needle leave the disciples astonished, asking who then can be saved. Peter reminds Jesus that they have left everything, and the chapter ends with a promise about the renewal of all things and a saying about the first and the last.

=== 20
Jesus has just said that many who are first will be last, and the last first. Now he tells a parable that ends with the same words, about a landowner hiring labourers for his vineyard at different hours of the day. Day labourers would gather in the marketplace each morning hoping for work, and a denarius was the ordinary wage for a day in the fields.

Then the road begins to climb. As they go up toward Jerusalem, Jesus takes the twelve aside and tells them for the third time what is going to happen. This time he is more specific than ever: he will be handed over to the Gentiles to be mocked, flogged and crucified. Immediately afterwards the mother of James and John comes asking for places of honour for her sons, and the other ten are indignant.

The chapter ends at Jericho, the last town before the steep climb of some fifteen miles up through the Judean wilderness to Jerusalem. Pilgrims for the Passover crowd the road. Two blind men by the wayside call out to Jesus as the Son of David, the royal title, and the crowd tries to silence them.

=== 21
The final week begins. Jesus and his followers reach Bethphage, a village on the Mount of Olives, the ridge that looks across the Kidron valley at Jerusalem and its temple. The city is filling with pilgrims for the Passover, swelling to several times its usual population.

Jesus sends for a donkey and a colt, and Matthew quotes the prophet Zechariah, who had pictured Zion's king coming to her humble and riding on a donkey. The crowds spread their cloaks and branches on the road and shout Hosanna, a Hebrew cry meaning save us, with words from the Psalms. The whole city is stirred.

In the temple courts Jesus overturns the tables of the money changers and the dealers in sacrificial doves. Pilgrims needed approved coins for the temple tax and animals for sacrifice, and the trade had filled the outer court. That night he lodges at Bethany, a village just over the hill. The next morning, after a fig tree withers at his word, the chief priests and elders confront him in the temple: by what authority is he doing these things? He answers with a question about John the Baptist, and then with two parables that his hearers understand are about them.

=== 22
Jesus is still teaching in the temple courts, and the parables aimed at the leaders continue with a story about a king's wedding banquet and the invited guests who refuse to come.

Then the leaders try a new approach. One after another, different groups come with questions designed to trap him. First, disciples of the Pharisees arrive together with the Herodians, supporters of the Herod family, an unlikely alliance. Their question is whether it is lawful to pay taxes to Caesar. The tax was resented, and it was paid with a silver denarius stamped with the emperor's image. Any answer seemed bound to offend either the people or Rome.

Next come the Sadducees, who deny the resurrection, with an elaborate case about a widow married in turn to seven brothers, based on the law that a man should marry his dead brother's widow. Then an expert in the law asks which commandment is the greatest. Finally Jesus asks a question of his own, about the Messiah as David's son, drawn from Psalm one hundred and ten. By the end of the day no one dares to ask him anything more.

=== 23
The questioning is over, and Jesus now speaks to the crowds and his disciples about the scribes and Pharisees. This is his last public address in the temple in Matthew's account, and it is the most severe.

He begins by granting that they sit in Moses' seat, as the recognised teachers of the law, but he warns against their example. He points to the trappings of religious status in his day: broad phylacteries, the small leather boxes containing verses of Scripture worn on the forehead and arm during prayer, long tassels on their garments, the best seats at banquets and in the synagogue, and the title Rabbi.

Then come seven woes, each beginning, woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, you hypocrites. The images are vivid and pointed: straining out a gnat and swallowing a camel, cleaning the outside of a cup, tombs whitewashed so that passers-by would not touch them by accident and become unclean. Yet the last word is not anger but lament, as Jesus grieves over Jerusalem, the city that kills the prophets, and speaks of her house left desolate.

=== 24
Jesus leaves the temple for the last time. As they walk away, his disciples point out its buildings. Herod's temple was one of the wonders of the Roman world, built of enormous gleaming stones, and work on it was still going on. Jesus tells them that not one stone will be left on another. About forty years later, Roman armies destroyed Jerusalem and burned the temple.

Later, sitting on the Mount of Olives, looking back across the valley at the temple, the disciples ask him privately when this will happen and what will be the sign of his coming and of the end of the age. His answer is the fifth and last of Matthew's great blocks of teaching, and it continues through chapter twenty-five.

He speaks of false messiahs, wars, persecution, and the abomination of desolation, a phrase from the book of Daniel. Some of his words fit closely what happened to Jerusalem, and some reach beyond it to his own return. Jesus closes this part of his answer with warnings to be ready, because no one knows the day or the hour.

=== 25
Jesus is still sitting on the Mount of Olives with his disciples, continuing his answer about the end of the age. The previous chapter ended with a servant waiting for his master's return. Now three more pictures follow, each about waiting and readiness.

The first is drawn from a village wedding. The bridegroom would come at night to fetch his bride, and young women with lamps would go out to meet the procession and escort it to the feast. In the story, the bridegroom is delayed. The second is about a master who goes away and entrusts his servants with talents, large sums of money, each talent worth many years of a labourer's wages, and who returns after a long time to settle accounts.

The third is not a parable in the usual sense. It is a scene of judgement, with the Son of Man seated on his glorious throne and all the nations gathered before him, separated as a shepherd separates the sheep from the goats. With it, Jesus' public teaching in Matthew's Gospel comes to an end.

=== 26
For the last time Matthew writes the phrase that has closed each block of teaching, and this time he adds a word: when Jesus had finished saying all these things. The Passover is two days away. In the courtyard of Caiaphas, the high priest, the chief priests and elders plot to arrest Jesus quietly, away from the festival crowds.

At Bethany, in the house of a man known as Simon the Leper, a woman pours expensive perfume on Jesus' head. Then Judas goes to the chief priests and agrees to hand Jesus over for thirty pieces of silver.

On the first day of the Feast of Unleavened Bread the disciples prepare the Passover meal, which recalled Israel's rescue from Egypt. During it Jesus speaks of betrayal, and he gives the bread and the cup new meaning. Then they go out to the Mount of Olives, to a place called Gethsemane, whose name means oil press. The night that follows brings prayer, arrest, a hearing before the Sanhedrin at Caiaphas's house, and in the courtyard outside, Peter's three denials.

=== 27
It is early on Friday morning. The Sanhedrin has condemned Jesus during the night, but under Roman rule it could not carry out a death sentence, so the leaders bind him and take him to Pontius Pilate. Pilate was the Roman governor of Judea for about ten years. He normally lived at Caesarea on the coast, but at Passover he came up to Jerusalem with his troops to keep order.

Matthew first follows Judas, who flings the silver into the temple and takes his own life, and the priests use the blood money to buy a potter's field. Then he returns to Pilate's judgement seat. It was the custom at the feast to release a prisoner, and the crowd is offered a choice between Jesus and a notorious prisoner named Barabbas. While Pilate sits in judgement, his wife sends him an urgent message about a dream, and before the trial is over he will call for water and wash his hands in front of the crowd.

Jesus is flogged, mocked by the soldiers and led out to Golgotha, outside the city walls. Darkness falls from noon until three in the afternoon. By evening Joseph of Arimathea has laid the body in his own tomb, and the next day the chief priests ask Pilate for a guard, because they remember what Jesus said about rising after three days.

=== 28
The Sabbath has passed. At dawn on the first day of the week, Sunday, Mary Magdalene and the other Mary, who watched the burial, come to see the tomb. The guard is still posted and the stone still sealed.

Matthew tells the events that follow briefly and with great energy: an earthquake, an angel, guards who shake with fear, and a message for the disciples. Then the women meet Jesus himself on the road. Meanwhile, the soldiers report to the chief priests, and Matthew explains the origin of a story still circulating in his own day, that the disciples had stolen the body.

The final scene takes place far from Jerusalem, back in Galilee, on a mountain where Jesus had told the eleven to meet him. Matthew does not hide that some of them doubt. Jesus speaks of all authority in heaven and on earth, and sends them to make disciples of all nations. The Gospel that began with a child named Immanuel, God with us, ends with his promise to be with them always, to the end of the age.
