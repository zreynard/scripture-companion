=== intro
The book of Lamentations stands in the ashes of Jerusalem. The Babylonian army has broken through the walls, burned the temple and the royal palace, and led the people away into exile, in the year five hundred and eighty-six before Christ. Jeremiah warned for forty years that this would happen. Lamentations is what grief sounds like once it has.

The book is made of five poems, one to each chapter, and they were written by someone who knew the city's last days at first hand: the famine of the siege, children fainting in the streets, the king captured as he fled, the neighbouring Edomites gloating. The poems do not name their author. An old tradition, found in the ancient Greek translation and widely followed since, attributes them to Jeremiah, and it is easy to see why. He lived through these events, he was a man of tears, and the second book of Chronicles remembers him composing laments. But the poems themselves never make the claim, and many scholars think they come from another poet, or more than one, among the survivors. Their tone and outlook also differ from Jeremiah's in places. Whoever wrote them, they belong to the same terrible years.

What a listener cannot easily hear in English is how carefully the poems are made. Four of the five are acrostics. In the first, second and fourth, each verse begins with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet, all twenty-two letters in order. The third poem is the most elaborate, with three verses for each letter, sixty-six in all. The fifth has twenty-two verses but no acrostic, as if the pattern itself has broken down. Many have suggested that the alphabet gives shape to grief that would otherwise have none, mourning from beginning to end, from A to Z. Much of the poetry also moves in a limping rhythm, a long line followed by a shorter one, which was used for funeral songs.

The Hebrew name of the book is its first word, Eikah, which means How!, the cry with which the first, second and fourth poems all begin. In the Hebrew Bible, Lamentations is not placed after Jeremiah but among the Writings, as one of five short scrolls read at the festivals of the year. It is still read in synagogues on the fast of the ninth of Av, which remembers the destruction of the temple. Christian Bibles follow the Greek order and set it beside the prophet traditionally linked with it.

The poems do not tell a story so much as circle a wound. Different voices speak: a narrator, the city herself pictured as a weeping widow, a single suffering man, and the community praying together. They confess that the disaster was deserved. They also protest, bluntly, at its cruelty, and ask God to look and see. At the very centre, in the third poem, there is a passage of hope, often quoted on its own. But the book does not end in triumph. It ends with a prayer, still waiting for an answer.

=== 1
The first poem opens with a single cry, How, and a picture of the city: lonely, once full of people, a princess become a slave, a widow weeping through the night. Jerusalem is spoken of as a woman, the Daughter of Zion, and the whole poem stays with her.

For the first half, a narrator describes her plight. Her friends have betrayed her, the roads to Zion mourn because no one comes to the feasts, and foreigners have entered the sanctuary where they were forbidden to go. The poem does not hide the reason. Jerusalem has sinned greatly. Then, partway through, the city herself begins to speak. Is it nothing to you, all you who pass by? Look and see whether there is any sorrow like mine.

One phrase comes back again and again, there is no one to comfort her. It sounds through the poem like a bell. Each of its twenty-two verses begins with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet, and the poem ends with Zion appealing to the Lord to see her groaning and to deal with her enemies as he has dealt with her.

=== 2
The second poem begins with the same cry, How, but its focus shifts. The first poem dwelt on Zion's loneliness and shame. This one looks at the hand that struck her. Again and again it says that the Lord has done this. He has covered the Daughter of Zion with the cloud of his anger, swallowed up her palaces, and torn down his own sanctuary like a garden shelter. The Lord has become like an enemy.

The details are those of a city just fallen. The walls and gates lie in ruins. The king and princes are in exile, and the prophets receive no vision. Elders sit silent on the ground with dust on their heads. Then the narrator's own composure breaks, and he describes children and infants fainting in the streets, asking their mothers for bread and wine.

He also names the prophets who failed the city, whose visions were empty and who never exposed her guilt to turn back her captivity. The poem ends by urging Zion to cry out to the Lord through the night, and with her anguished question: whom have you ever treated like this?

=== 3
The third poem is the longest and the most intricate. For each letter of the Hebrew alphabet there are three verses, all beginning with that letter, sixty-six in all. And here a new voice speaks, not the city but a single man. I am the man who has seen affliction under the rod of his wrath.

He does not say who he is. Some have heard Jeremiah's voice in him, and the description of being thrown alive into a pit has strengthened that tradition, but he may be a figure in whom the sufferings of the whole people are gathered. His opening complaint is relentless: walled in, weighed down with chains, prayers shut out, a target for God's arrows.

Then, at the very centre of the book, comes a turning. Yet this I call to mind, and therefore I have hope. What follows is one of the best-known passages in the Old Testament, the Lord's loving devotion and mercies that are new every morning. It is not the end of grief. The poem moves on into confession, more tears and a plea for justice. But the hope has been spoken, and it stands at the heart of the book.

=== 4
The fourth poem returns to the opening cry, How, and to the alphabetic pattern, now in shorter verses of two lines. It begins with gold that has lost its lustre and sacred stones scattered in the streets, and much of it is built on the contrast between before and after.

The young nobles of Zion were once worth their weight in gold, and now they are treated like clay pots. Those raised in purple lie on ash heaps. Princes whose faces once glowed with health are blackened and shrivelled, unrecognised in the streets. The poem speaks plainly of the famine of the siege and what it drove people to.

Then it looks for causes and moments. The sins of the prophets and priests are blamed. The people recall watching from their towers for a nation that could not save them, most likely Egypt, whose promised help came to nothing. They remember being hunted down in the mountains and the wilderness, and the capture of the Lord's anointed, the king under whose shadow they thought they would live, surely Zedekiah. The poem ends with a word to Edom, which rejoiced at Jerusalem's fall: the cup will pass to you as well.

=== 5
The last poem is different from the others. It is a prayer, spoken by the whole community, and it opens with an appeal: remember, O Lord, what has happened to us. It has twenty-two verses, one for each letter, but the acrostic pattern has gone.

Some time seems to have passed since the fall. The poem describes life under occupation. Ancestral land has passed to strangers. The people must pay for their own water and firewood. They risk their lives to gather bread with raiders in the wilderness. Princes have been hung up by their hands, young men are forced to grind at the millstones, and boys stagger under loads of wood. Mount Zion lies desolate, and foxes prowl across it.

Near the end, the prayer lifts its eyes to the Lord who reigns forever, and asks to be restored and to have its days renewed as of old. The final line leaves the question open, whether he has utterly rejected them. When the book is read in the synagogue, the reader repeats the plea for restoration after it, so that the book does not end on that note.
