=== intro
First John does not look much like a letter. There is no greeting, no named writer, no named readers, and no farewell. It begins with something closer to a solemn announcement and ends with a single line of warning. Yet it is plainly written by a pastor to people he knows and loves. He calls them my little children again and again, and he tells them more than once exactly why he is writing.

The writer never gives his name, but he writes as one of the first witnesses: one who heard, saw and touched the Word of life. He is John, the son of Zebedee, the fisherman whom Jesus called alongside his brother James, and the disciple whom the Gospel of John calls the one Jesus loved. John lived to a great age and spent his last years in Ephesus, the leading city of the Roman province of Asia, on the western coast of what is now Turkey, caring for the churches of the region. This letter and the Gospel of John are close kin. The vocabulary is the same: light and darkness, truth and lies, life, love, abiding, the world. So is the habit of returning to a few great words and seeing them from a new angle each time.

This is the voice of the last surviving apostle, writing near the end of the first century, some sixty years after the crucifixion. Jerusalem and its temple have been destroyed. Peter and Paul are long dead. The churches of Asia are now in their second and third generations, and the people John writes to have never seen Jesus for themselves.

Something painful has happened among them. A group has left the fellowship. They went out from us, John writes, but they did not belong to us. Behind them they have left a cloud of false claims. There are those who deny that Jesus is the Christ, and spirits that will not confess that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh. There are voices saying that a person can know God while ignoring his commands, can be without sin, can love God while hating his brother.

The people who remain are shaken. They wonder whether those who left were right, and whether they themselves truly know God. John writes to steady them. He gives several reasons in his own words: so that our joy may be complete, so that they will not sin, and, near the end, so that they may know that they have eternal life. He gives them ways to recognise the truth, and to recognise those who belong to God: what they believe about Jesus, whether they obey his commands, and whether they love one another.

The letter does not move in a straight line. It circles, like a man walking around a building and viewing it from every side. It begins with the eyewitness testimony of the apostles and the message that God is light. It speaks of sin and forgiveness, of the old command to love, and of the antichrists who have gone out into the world. It turns to the wonder of being called children of God, and to love shown in action rather than words. It calls the readers to test the spirits, and it contains the simple statement that God is love. It ends with faith that overcomes the world, assurance of eternal life, and a final warning to keep away from idols.

Two shorter letters follow, from a writer who calls himself the elder, and they reflect the same circle of churches and the same concerns. Later still comes the book of Revelation, written by John on Patmos, and also sent to churches in the province of Asia.

=== 1
The letter opens without a greeting, and its first words recall the opening of John's Gospel. That which was from the beginning is now described in the language of the senses: heard, seen with our own eyes, gazed upon, touched with our own hands. The writer stands among the eyewitnesses, and he insists that the life of God appeared in a real human body, a deliberate place to begin.

He writes so that his readers may share the fellowship the apostles have with the Father and the Son, and so that our joy may be complete. Then he states the message at the heart of what follows: God is light, and in him there is no darkness at all.

The rest of the chapter tests three claims, each beginning if we say: that it is possible to have fellowship with God while walking in darkness, that we have no sin, and that we have not sinned. John answers each one, and among them stands the promise that if we confess our sins, God is faithful to forgive.

=== 2
For the first time John calls his readers my little children, the voice of an old man speaking to people he loves. Having insisted that no one can claim to be without sin, he now says he writes so that they will not sin, and that if they do, Jesus Christ the Righteous One speaks for them before the Father.

He offers a test of knowing God: obeying his commands, above all the command to love one's brother. It is an old commandment, he says, that they have had from the beginning, and yet new, because the darkness is passing and the true light is already shining. A short, rhythmic passage follows, addressed in turn to children, fathers and young men, and then a warning not to love the world and its desires.

The second half of the chapter brings the crisis into view. It is the last hour, John says, and many antichrists have appeared. The word appears in the Bible only in these letters of John. Then comes the painful detail: they went out from us. Some have left the community, denying that Jesus is the Christ, and John reassures those who remain that they know the truth.

=== 3
Chapter two ended with the thought of being born of God. John now stops to marvel at it. See what love the Father has given us, he says, that we should be called children of God, and that is what we are. What they will be has not yet been revealed, but when Christ appears they will be like him.

From there he draws a sharp line between the children of God and the children of the devil, marked by how people live. Then he returns to the old message of loving one another, and reaches back to the first brothers in Genesis. Cain, who murdered Abel, is the pattern of hatred, and John is not surprised that the world hates those who belong to God.

The measure of love, by contrast, is Jesus laying down his life. John brings it down to earth with a believer who has possessions and sees a brother in need. The chapter ends with confidence before God, and with the Spirit he has given, which leads straight into the warning that opens chapter four.

=== 4
Chapter three ended with the Spirit God has given to his people. But others claim the Spirit too, and so John opens with a warning: do not believe every spirit. Many false prophets have gone out into the world.

The test he gives is precise. Every spirit that confesses that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is from God. Every spirit that does not confess Jesus is not from God, and John calls it the spirit of the antichrist, already in the world. He assures his little children that the one who is in them is greater than the one who is in the world.

Then he turns to love, and the rest of the chapter contains some of the most famous lines in the New Testament. Twice he says that God is love, words found nowhere else in Scripture. He explains that God's love was revealed in sending his one and only Son, not because we loved God, but because he loved us. Love drives out fear, John says, and the chapter ends with a simple logic: no one can love the unseen God while hating the brother he can see.

=== 5
The last chapter gathers together the threads that have run through the letter: believing that Jesus is the Christ, loving God and his children, and keeping his commands. Those born of God overcome the world, John says, and the victory is their faith.

He then speaks of Jesus Christ as the one who came by water and blood, not by water alone. John adds the witness of the Spirit, and speaks of God's own testimony about his Son.

Near the end he states his purpose plainly: I have written these things so that you may know that you have eternal life. It echoes the words near the end of John's Gospel, which say it was written so that its readers may believe. There are words on prayer, including a reference to a sin that leads to death, three statements of what we know, and a single closing line, with no farewell.
