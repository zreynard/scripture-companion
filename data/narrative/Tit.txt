=== intro
The letter to Titus is the third of the pastoral letters, and it belongs to the same late chapter of Paul's life as the first letter to Timothy. Once again Paul writes to a trusted colleague he has left behind to finish a task. This time the place is not a great city but an island: Crete, the long, mountainous island that lies across the southern edge of the Aegean Sea, south of mainland Greece.

Titus never appears by name in Acts, but Paul's other letters show how much he relied on him. Titus was a Greek, a Gentile convert. On one visit to Jerusalem he went up with Paul and Barnabas, and Paul later pointed out that the leaders there had not required him to be circumcised. Years afterwards Titus was Paul's envoy to the troubled church in Corinth, sent to learn how they had taken a painful letter, bringing back news that comforted Paul greatly, and helping to organise the collection for the poor in Jerusalem. He seems to have been the colleague Paul sent where firmness and tact were both needed.

How the gospel first reached Crete is not recorded. Cretans were among the crowd in Jerusalem on the day of Pentecost, and Paul's ship called briefly at a harbour on the island on the voyage to Rome that ends Acts, but neither moment describes the founding of churches. This letter simply tells us that Paul and Titus had worked in Crete together, that Paul had moved on, and that Titus had stayed to put in order what remained unfinished and to appoint elders in every town. As with the letters to Timothy, the visit is hard to fit into the story Acts tells, and many place it in the years after Paul's first imprisonment in Rome. And as with those letters, scholars differ over whether Paul wrote it himself or a later follower wrote in his name.

The churches in Crete were young and unsettled. There were rebellious talkers in them, many of them from among the Jewish believers, who were teaching for gain, upsetting whole households, and promoting myths, genealogies and quarrels about the law. Paul quotes a line from a Cretan poet, traditionally identified as Epimenides, about the island's people being liars, a jibe the ancient world knew well. Titus was to answer this by appointing sound leaders, by teaching clearly, and by showing what a well-ordered Christian life looked like in a place where reputations mattered.

The letter is short, and its three chapters move in a natural order. The first concerns elders and the false teachers they will have to resist. The second turns to the households of the church, older men and women, younger men and women, and slaves, and grounds their way of life in the grace of God that has appeared in Christ. The third widens to the believers' conduct as citizens and neighbours, recalls how God saved them, and ends with travel plans.

Those plans are a small window onto Paul's world in motion. He intends to send Artemas or Tychicus to take Titus's place, and asks Titus to join him at Nicopolis, probably the city of that name on the western coast of Greece, where Paul has decided to spend the winter. The second letter to Timothy will later mention, in passing, that Titus has gone on to Dalmatia, across the Adriatic, in what is now Croatia.

=== 1
The letter opens with one of the longest greetings in Paul's letters, a full sentence about his calling as a servant of God and an apostle, and about the hope of eternal life promised before time began. Only then does he name Titus, his true child in a common faith.

Paul moves at once to the task. He reminds Titus why he was left in Crete: to set in order what was unfinished and to appoint elders in every town. The qualities he describes are much like those in the first letter to Timothy, with the same weight on character, household and self-control, and on holding firmly to sound teaching.

That last point leads to the reason it matters. Many in the Cretan churches are rebellious and full of empty talk, especially some from among the Jewish believers, and they are upsetting whole households for the sake of money. Paul quotes the Cretan poet's harsh line about his own people, and tells Titus to rebuke such teachers sharply, so that the churches may be sound in the faith.

=== 2
Paul has described the false teachers in Crete, whose lives contradict what they claim to believe. Now he turns to Titus, and to what he should teach instead. The contrast is deliberate. Over against empty talk and corrupted households, Titus is to teach what fits sound doctrine, and to show it in ordinary life.

Paul goes through the church group by group: older men, older women, who are to train the younger women, younger men, Titus himself, and slaves. Throughout, he keeps one eye on outsiders. He wants nothing in the believers' lives to bring the message into disrepute, and he wants their conduct to make the teaching about God attractive. In a world that judged a new religion largely by the households it produced, that mattered.

Then the chapter rises to its centre. Paul explains why such a life is possible, and what it looks back to and forward to: the grace of God that has appeared, and the hope of the glorious appearing of Jesus Christ, who gave himself to redeem and purify a people for his own. He closes by telling Titus to teach these things with authority, and to let no one despise him.

=== 3
The previous chapter spoke of the household. Paul now widens the view to the believers' place in the wider community. He asks Titus to remind them to be subject to rulers and authorities, to be ready for every good work, and to be gentle and courteous to everyone. Crete was a Roman province, and Christians there, as elsewhere, were watched by neighbours who knew little about them.

To ground this, Paul recalls what he and his readers once were, foolish and enslaved to their desires, and then sets out in a few compact lines how God saved them, not because of their own righteous deeds, but by his mercy, through the washing of new birth and renewal by the Holy Spirit.

The letter then returns briefly to the troublemakers, with instructions on how to deal with a divisive person, before closing with the travel plans for Nicopolis and a request that Titus help two travellers on their way: Zenas the lawyer, and Apollos, the learned teacher from Alexandria whom Acts and the letters to Corinth remember well.
