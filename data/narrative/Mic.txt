=== intro
Micah, whose name means who is like the Lord, was a countryman. He came from Moresheth, a small town in the low hills of the Shephelah, about twenty-five miles south-west of Jerusalem, in the border country between Judah and the old Philistine cities. It was farming land, dotted with fortified towns that guarded the approaches to the capital, and it lay directly in the path of any army marching up from the coast. Micah's prophecy is full of that landscape and its people.

After the story of Jonah, set in the early years of the eighth century when Assyria was still distant, Micah brings us into the decades when it arrived on Judah's doorstep.

The book dates his ministry to the reigns of Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah, kings of Judah, roughly the last third of the eighth century before Christ. That makes him a younger contemporary of Hosea and Amos, and a contemporary of Isaiah. Isaiah moved among kings and courtiers in Jerusalem; Micah spoke for the small farmers and villagers. Yet the two prophets share a great deal, and one famous passage, about the nations streaming to the mountain of the Lord, appears in almost the same words in both books.

These were the years when Assyria swallowed the kingdoms of the west one by one. The listener knows the outline from the second book of Kings. Under Ahaz, Judah became a vassal of Assyria in return for help against Israel and Damascus, and Ahaz even had a copy made for the temple of an altar he had seen in Damascus, where he went to meet the Assyrian king. In about seven hundred and twenty-two before Christ, Samaria fell, as Micah foretold at the very start of his book. His son Hezekiah tore down the high places and led a reform of worship, and later he rebelled against Assyria, and in seven hundred and one Sennacherib invaded Judah. His own annals boast that he captured forty-six walled towns, and the reliefs from his palace at Nineveh show the siege of Lachish, only a few miles from Micah's home. Jerusalem itself was spared, as the books of Kings and Isaiah relate.

Micah's chief concern is what was happening inside Judah. Powerful men lay awake planning how to seize their neighbours' fields and houses. Judges and priests took bribes, prophets preached peace to anyone who fed them, and the rulers used their power to devour the people they were meant to protect. All the while, they assured themselves that the Lord was among them and no harm could come. Micah's answer was that Jerusalem would be ploughed like a field.

A century later that word was still remembered. When Jeremiah was on trial for his life for predicting the temple's destruction, some of the elders of Judah reminded the crowd that Micah of Moresheth had said the same thing in the days of Hezekiah. Hezekiah had not killed him, they said, but had sought the Lord, and the disaster was turned aside.

The book moves back and forth between judgement and hope, and its three main sections each open with a call to hear. Again and again, after the harshest warnings, comes a promise: a remnant gathered like sheep, Zion raised up as the place where the nations learn the Lord's ways, and a ruler born in Bethlehem, the little town of David's family. The book ends in hope, with a prayer spoken on behalf of the whole people.

=== 1
After a line naming the prophet and his times, the book opens on a courtroom of cosmic scale. All the peoples of the earth are summoned to hear, and the Lord comes down from his dwelling place, the mountains melting beneath his feet. The charge is against both of his people's capitals. Samaria is the sin of the north, and Jerusalem has become the high place of Judah, a name for the hilltop shrines of idolatry.

Samaria's fate comes first. It will become a heap of stones in an open field, fit only for planting vines, a word spoken before the city fell to Assyria. But the wound will not stop at the border. It has reached the very gate of Jerusalem.

Micah responds with personal grief, and then with a lament full of wordplay on the towns of his own district. Beth-leaphrah means house of dust, so its people should roll in the dust. Lachish, the great fortress, should harness its horses. The list traces an invader advancing through Micah's homeland towards the capital.

=== 2
Micah turns now from the coming invasion to its cause, beginning with the landowners who lie awake at night devising schemes and carry them out at first light, because they have the power to do it. They covet fields and seize them, and take away a man's house and his inheritance.

In Israel the land was a family's portion, given by God and meant to stay in the family for generations. The judgement fits the crime. Those who took other people's land will lose their own, and no one will be left to measure out their portion in the assembly of the Lord.

The prophet's hearers do not want to hear any of this. Do not preach, they tell him, disgrace will not overtake us. Micah replies with biting sarcasm: a man who came prophesying wine and strong drink would be just the preacher for them.

Then, abruptly, the tone changes, as it often does in this book. The Lord promises to gather the remnant of his people like sheep in a fold, and to go before them himself as their king.

=== 3
This chapter brings together three oracles aimed at the nation's leaders. The rulers come first. They should know justice, Micah says, yet they hate good and love evil, and he pictures them as butchers, stripping the people's flesh and chopping their bones for the pot.

Next he turns to the prophets who lead the people astray. They proclaim peace as long as they are fed, and declare war on anyone who will not pay. For them night is coming, with no vision and no answer from God. In contrast Micah describes his own calling: he is filled with power by the Spirit of the Lord, to declare to Jacob his transgression.

The final oracle gathers up the whole leadership, rulers, priests and prophets alike, all working for money while claiming that the Lord is among them. It ends with the most startling word in the book, the one still remembered in Jeremiah's day. Zion will be ploughed like a field, and the temple mount will become a wooded ridge.

=== 4
Immediately after the warning that the temple mount will become a wooded ridge, the book looks far ahead to a different future. In the last days, that same mountain will be raised above every other, and the nations will stream to it to learn the ways of the God of Jacob. They will beat their swords into ploughshares, and each man will sit under his own vine and fig tree. Almost the same words appear in the second chapter of Isaiah.

The rest of the chapter alternates between distress and deliverance in the near future. The Lord will gather the lame and the outcast and make them a strong nation. Zion is pictured as a woman in labour. Then a surprising name appears. You will go to Babylon, Micah says, and there you will be rescued. In Micah's day Babylon was a restless subject of Assyria, not yet the empire that would one day carry Judah into exile.

=== 5
The chapter opens with Jerusalem under siege and its ruler humiliated, struck on the cheek with a rod.

The next lines are among the best known in the Old Testament. The hope of Israel will come not from the capital but from Bethlehem Ephrathah, the small town where David was born. From there a ruler will come whose origins are from of old, and he will shepherd his flock in the strength of the Lord. When the wise men arrived in Jerusalem asking where the king of the Jews was to be born, the chief priests and scribes would quote this passage to Herod.

Assyria is then named, and called the land of Nimrod after the founder of its cities in Genesis. The remnant of Jacob will be among the nations like dew from the Lord, and like a lion among the flocks. In the last verses the Lord promises to remove everything his people have trusted instead of him, from chariots and fortresses to sorcery and idols.

=== 6
The third part of the book opens like the first, with a call to hear. The Lord brings a case against his people, with the mountains and the foundations of the earth as witnesses.

His charge begins with a question, my people, what have I done to you, and then recalls their story: the exodus from Egypt, the leadership of Moses, Aaron and Miriam, the time when Balak king of Moab hired Balaam to curse Israel and Balaam blessed them instead, and the crossing from Shittim, east of the Jordan, to Gilgal, the first camp in the promised land.

A worshipper's reply follows, asking what to bring the Lord: calves, rams, rivers of oil, even a firstborn child. Micah's answer has become one of the most quoted sayings in the Bible: to act justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God.

The chapter then turns to the city, Jerusalem, with its false weights and violent rich. It has kept the statutes of Omri and the practices of Ahab, the northern kings most remembered for their idolatry.

=== 7
The final chapter begins with the prophet's lament. He is like someone searching a vineyard after the harvest and finding nothing left. The godly have vanished, the prince and the judge demand bribes, and no one can be trusted, not even within the family. Jesus would draw on these lines when he warned that a man's enemies would be those of his own household.

Then the voice changes, and Micah, speaking for his people, turns to the Lord in hope. Zion speaks as a woman who has fallen and will rise again. She will bear the Lord's anger because she has sinned, but he will bring her into the light. The walls will be rebuilt, and people will come from Assyria and Egypt and from sea to sea.

The book closes with a hymn. Its opening words, who is a God like you, echo the prophet's own name, and it praises the God who pardons iniquity and delights in loving devotion. The final line reaches back to Abraham and Jacob, and to the promises the Lord swore to their fathers.
