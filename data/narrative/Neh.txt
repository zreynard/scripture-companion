=== intro
The book of Nehemiah opens far from Jerusalem, in the winter palace of the king of Persia at Susa, in what is now southwestern Iran. It is the middle of the fifth century before Christ, and a Jewish official at the royal court has just heard news from home that leaves him weeping.

The home he grieves for is a city with a long and painful history. Jerusalem had been the capital of King David and the site of Solomon's temple, the place where God had chosen to set his Name. But the kings who followed led the people into the worship of other gods, and the prophets warned that judgement would come. It came through Babylon. In five hundred and eighty-six before Christ the armies of Nebuchadnezzar broke through the walls of Jerusalem, burned the temple and the palace, pulled down the city's fortifications and carried much of the population away to Babylonia. For two generations the Jews lived as exiles beside the rivers of Babylon, while their city lay in ruins.

Then the world changed. Cyrus, king of Persia, conquered Babylon and allowed the exiled peoples of his empire to go home. The book of Ezra tells how the first Jews returned, rebuilt the altar and, after long delays, finished a new temple in the reign of Darius. Some decades later Ezra the scribe came from Babylon with royal authority to teach the Law of Moses. By the time Nehemiah begins, the Jews of Judah have lived under Persian rule for nearly a century. The Persian Empire stretches from Egypt and the Aegean Sea to the borders of India, and its kings govern through a network of provinces and officials. Judah is one of its smaller districts, a modest territory around Jerusalem in the great province the Persians called Beyond the River, meaning west of the Euphrates.

The temple stands, but the city around it is still a ruin. Its walls are broken and its gates burned, and earlier attempts to rebuild them had been halted by Persian order after complaints from Judah's neighbours. In the ancient world a city without walls was barely a city at all. It could not defend itself against raiders, it could not control who came and went, and few people would choose to live there. For the Jews it was also a disgrace, a visible sign before the surrounding peoples that God's city still lay under judgement.

Nehemiah is the man who changes that. He is the son of Hacaliah, one of the Jews still living in the east, and he serves as cupbearer to King Artaxerxes. It was a position of great trust. The cupbearer served the king his wine and guarded against poison, and he stood close enough to the throne to have the king's ear. When Nehemiah asks to go to Jerusalem, Artaxerxes appoints him governor of Judah and sends him with letters, timber and an armed escort.

Much of the book is Nehemiah's own account, written in the first person, frank and vivid, and punctuated by short prayers in which he asks God to remember him. The first half tells how he rebuilds the wall of Jerusalem in a matter of weeks, in the face of ridicule, threats and plots from three powerful neighbours: Sanballat, the governor of Samaria to the north, Tobiah, an official with connections in Ammon to the east, and Geshem, a leader of the Arab peoples to the south. At the same time he must deal with poverty and debt among his own people. The second half turns from stones to people. Ezra reappears to read the Law to the assembled city, the people confess their sins and bind themselves by a written covenant, Jerusalem is repopulated, and the finished wall is dedicated with two great choirs singing along its top. The book ends some years later, when Nehemiah returns from a visit to the king and finds many of the old failings creeping back.

In the Hebrew Bible, Ezra and Nehemiah form a single book, and the two men's work overlaps: Ezra arrived about thirteen years before Nehemiah, and they appear together at the great reading of the Law. Who compiled the final work is not known, though it drew directly on Nehemiah's memoir and on lists from the temple archives. The prophet Malachi, whose book closes the Old Testament in English Bibles, probably spoke to this same community at about this time, and he confronts many of the same problems.

Nehemiah is the last of the Old Testament's historical narratives. After it, the biblical record falls largely silent until the New Testament opens, some four centuries later, in a Jerusalem that has outlived the Persians and the Greeks and now lies under the power of Rome.

=== 1
The book begins in Susa, the Persian royal city, in the month of Chislev, early in the winter. It is the twentieth year of King Artaxerxes, and the man speaking is Nehemiah son of Hacaliah.

Hanani, one of Nehemiah's brothers, has arrived from Judah with a group of men. Nehemiah asks about the Jews who escaped the exile and about Jerusalem. Their answer is bleak. The people in the province are in great trouble and disgrace, the wall of Jerusalem is broken down, and its gates have been burned. Whether this is the old ruin left by Babylon or fresh damage after the halted rebuilding recorded in Ezra, the news strikes Nehemiah hard.

For days he mourns, fasts and prays. The prayer at the heart of the chapter draws on the words of Moses: God had warned that an unfaithful Israel would be scattered, and promised that a repentant Israel would be gathered again. Nehemiah confesses his people's sins as his own and asks for success before this man. Only in the last line do we learn who that man is, and what Nehemiah does: he is cupbearer to the king.

=== 2
Four months have passed since the news from Jerusalem. It is now the month of Nisan, in the spring, and Nehemiah is at his duties, serving wine to King Artaxerxes, with the queen seated beside him.

To appear sad before the king was dangerous, and Nehemiah has never done so before. But today his grief shows on his face, and the king notices. In the conversation that follows, Nehemiah prays silently and then makes his request: to be sent to Judah to rebuild the city where his fathers are buried. He has thought it through. He asks for letters of safe passage and for timber from the royal forest, and the king grants it all, adding army officers and cavalry for the journey.

His arrival does not go unnoticed. Two men are named who will oppose him throughout the book: Sanballat the Horonite, the governor of Samaria, and Tobiah, an Ammonite official. After three days in Jerusalem Nehemiah rides out by night, with only a few companions, to inspect the ruined walls before he tells anyone his plan. By the end of the chapter a third enemy, Geshem the Arab, has joined the mockery.

=== 3
The people have agreed to rebuild, and this chapter shows how they did it. It is a record of who built which section of the wall, a little like a list of names carved on a memorial.

The account traces the circuit of the walls, starting at the Sheep Gate, close to the temple at the north-eastern corner, then running west along the northern wall, down the western side and round the southern tip of the city, and back up the eastern side above the Kidron Valley. Gates and towers mark the way: the Fish Gate, the Valley Gate, the Dung Gate, the Fountain Gate, the Water Gate, the Horse Gate. Some of these cannot now be located with certainty.

What stands out is who is doing the work. The high priest and his fellow priests start at the Sheep Gate. Goldsmiths and perfume makers work alongside district rulers and men from towns across Judah, and one man's daughters build beside him. Many repair the stretch beside their own homes. One group, the nobles of Tekoa, refuse to put their shoulders to the work, and the record notes it. Nehemiah the governor does not appear in the list at all; the Nehemiah named partway through is another man.

=== 4
The work is going ahead along the whole circuit of the wall, and now the opposition grows louder. Sanballat, standing before his associates and the army of Samaria, pours scorn on the feeble Jews and their heaps of burnt stones, and Tobiah adds that a fox could knock their wall down. Nehemiah's response is to pray, and to keep building, until the whole wall is joined together to half its height.

As the gaps close, mockery turns to menace. Enemies on every side, from Samaria, Ammon, the Arabs and the coastal city of Ashdod, plot an attack. The builders are exhausted, the rubble seems endless, and Jews living near the enemy keep coming with warnings. Fear and fatigue are now as dangerous to the work as the enemy.

Nehemiah's answer is part prayer and part military planning. The rest of the chapter shows a city working under arms, with builders carrying swords as they work, a trumpeter beside Nehemiah ready to sound the alarm, and no one taking off his clothes at night.

=== 5
The wall is rising and the enemies outside have been held at bay. Now Nehemiah faces a crisis from inside the community, and it is a bitter one.

There has been a famine, and the poorer families are in desperate straits. Some have mortgaged their fields, vineyards and homes to buy grain. Others have borrowed money to pay the king's tax. Some have had to hand over their sons and daughters as slaves to fellow Jews, the nobles and officials who have lent to them at interest. The Law of Moses forbade Israelites to charge interest to their poor brothers, and Nehemiah is furious.

He calls a public assembly and confronts the lenders before the whole people. Then the chapter steps forward in time. Writing at the end of his twelve years as governor, Nehemiah describes how he conducted himself. Unlike earlier governors, he never claimed the food allowance his office entitled him to, though he fed a hundred and fifty officials and many guests at his table every day. The chapter closes with the first of several short prayers that he adds to his account, asking God to remember him.

=== 6
The wall is finished, with not a gap left in it, though the doors have not yet been hung in the gates. Sanballat, Tobiah and Geshem change their tactics. Open threats have failed, so now they try cunning.

First comes an invitation to a meeting in the plain of Ono, near Lod, on the edge of the coastal plain and well away from Jerusalem. Nehemiah suspects a trap. Then comes an open letter, the kind anyone could read along the way, accusing him of plotting rebellion and planning to make himself king. In the Persian Empire that was a dangerous charge, and the rumour alone could undo everything.

The last scheme comes through a man in Jerusalem, Shemaiah, who urges Nehemiah to hide from assassins inside the temple building itself, where only priests were allowed to go. Nehemiah sees through each of these, and the wall is completed in fifty-two days, in the month of Elul, at the end of summer. Even so, the chapter ends with a reminder that the struggle is not over: Tobiah has family ties with leading households in Judah, and a stream of letters keeps passing between them.

=== 7
The wall is complete and the doors are in place. Nehemiah now turns to keeping the city safe. He puts his brother Hanani in charge of Jerusalem, together with Hananiah, the commander of the citadel, a man more faithful and God-fearing than most, and gives strict instructions about when the gates may be opened and how the city is to be guarded.

But a wall alone does not make a city. Jerusalem is large and spacious, Nehemiah writes, but there are few people in it, and the houses have not been rebuilt. Moved by God to do something about it, he gathers the people to be registered by their families, and he finds the record of those who had first returned from Babylon nearly a century before.

That record fills most of the chapter. It is the same list found in the second chapter of Ezra, with small differences in names and figures, and it reminds the community of its roots. The chapter closes with the people settled in their towns as the seventh month approaches, and with that, the story moves into the great assembly that follows.

=== 8
The wall is finished. Now, on the first day of the seventh month, the opening of Israel's autumn season of feasts, the people gather as one in the square before the Water Gate, on the eastern side of the city. They ask for the Book of the Law of Moses.

The man they ask is Ezra the scribe, who has not appeared in Nehemiah's story until now. It is about thirteen years since he came from Babylon to teach the Law, and here, for the first time, the two leaders stand together.

Ezra stands on a wooden platform built for the occasion, with leading men on either side, and as he opens the scroll the whole crowd rises to its feet. He reads from daybreak until noon. Levites move among the people, explaining the reading so that everyone can understand; this may have included translating the Hebrew into Aramaic, the everyday language many now spoke. The people's first response is to weep, and they have to be told that this is a day for feasting. The next day, studying the Law further, the leaders discover something that will fill the rest of the month with celebration.

=== 9
The Feast of Tabernacles has been kept with great joy, and it ended only two days ago. Now, on the twenty-fourth day of the same month, the mood is very different. The people gather again, this time fasting, dressed in sackcloth, with dust on their heads.

Those of Israelite descent separate themselves from the foreigners among them, and the day is divided between reading the Law and confessing sin. Then the Levites lead the people in one of the longest prayers in the Bible.

It is a retelling of Israel's whole history, addressed to God. It begins with creation and the call of Abram, then moves through the slavery in Egypt, the crossing of the sea, the giving of the Law at Sinai, the wilderness years, the conquest of the land, the cycles of the judges and the long years of the prophets' warnings. Through it all runs a double refrain: the people's stubbornness, and God's patience and compassion. The prayer ends in the present, with a people back in their land but still subject to foreign kings, and with a resolution to make a binding written agreement.

=== 10
The prayer of the Levites ended with a decision to put a covenant in writing and seal it. This chapter is that document.

It begins with the names of those who set their seal to it. Nehemiah the governor heads the list, followed by the priests, the Levites and the leaders of the people. Many are family names already familiar from the lists of returning exiles.

Then come the terms, spoken by the whole people. They swear to keep the Law of Moses, and they spell out what that will mean in practice. They will not intermarry with the peoples of the land. They will not buy and sell on the Sabbath. They will let the land rest every seventh year and cancel debts. And they will provide for the house of God: a yearly contribution for its services, wood for the altar, firstfruits, the firstborn, and tithes for the priests and Levites. The promise that closes the chapter is simple: we will not neglect the house of our God. These are precisely the matters the book will return to at its end.

=== 11
The story now returns to the problem raised back in chapter seven, before the great assembly: Jerusalem has strong walls but too few people living inside them.

The leaders already live in the city. For the rest, lots are cast, and one family in every ten is chosen to move into Jerusalem, while the others stay in their towns and villages. Some volunteer, and the people bless them for it. Living in the capital meant leaving fields and family land, and it may also have carried some danger.

Most of the chapter is a record of who settled there: families from the tribes of Judah and Benjamin, priests, Levites, singers and gatekeepers, with the numbers of fighting men and temple workers. It ends with a list of the towns and villages where the rest of the people lived, from Beersheba in the far south to the Valley of Hinnom just outside Jerusalem, and through the towns of Benjamin to the north. It is a picture of the community as it now stood, a city at its heart and the old towns of Judah around it.

=== 12
The chapter opens with more lists, this time of the priests and Levites. They reach back to the first return under Zerubbabel and Jeshua the high priest, and they trace the line of high priests from Jeshua through several generations, some of them beyond Nehemiah's own lifetime. For a community rebuilt around its temple, an unbroken priestly succession mattered a great deal.

Then Nehemiah's own voice returns, and the scene changes to the event the whole book has been moving toward: the dedication of the wall of Jerusalem. The Levites and singers are gathered from the villages, and the priests purify themselves, the people, the gates and the wall.

Nehemiah stages the celebration on the wall itself. Two great choirs climb up and process along its top in opposite directions, one led by Ezra, the other followed by Nehemiah, until they meet at the house of God. Nearly a century earlier, when the temple's foundation was laid, the sound of joy had been mingled with weeping. This time the joy of Jerusalem is heard far away.

=== 13
The book's final chapter begins with one more public reading of the Law, and then it jumps forward. Nehemiah explains that after his twelve years as governor he went back to King Artaxerxes, in the king's thirty-second year. Some time later, how long is not said, he obtained leave to return to Jerusalem.

What he finds there is dispiriting. The first discovery is that Eliashib, the priest in charge of the temple storerooms, has given a room in the temple courts to Tobiah, the Ammonite who fought the rebuilding. It is not the last. One by one, the promises the people sealed in their covenant, about the support of the Levites, the Sabbath and marriage, turn out to have been quietly set aside, and the trouble reaches even into the high priest's own family.

Nehemiah's response to each of these is swift and forceful. Between the episodes come his brief prayers, asking God to remember him. The last of them is also the final sentence of the book.
