=== intro
When Numbers opens, Israel has been camped at the foot of Mount Sinai for almost a year. They arrived there in the third month after leaving Egypt, a crowd of freed slaves still astonished by the plagues and the crossing of the sea. At the mountain God gave them his covenant and his law. They built the tabernacle, the portable tent where his presence would dwell among them, and the book of Leviticus recorded the sacrifices, the priesthood and the laws of holiness that would govern life around it. Now, one month after the tabernacle was raised, the time has come to leave the mountain and march toward the land God promised to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob.

The English title comes from the old Greek translation of the Bible, which named the book after the two great counts of Israel's fighting men that stand near its beginning and its end. The Hebrew title is more evocative. It is taken from the book's opening line and means in the wilderness, and that is where almost the whole book takes place. It covers close to forty years, from the second year after the exodus to the fortieth, and it carries Israel from Sinai to the very edge of Canaan.

Like the books before it, Numbers does not name its author. Jewish and Christian tradition has linked it with Moses, and the book itself says that Moses recorded the stages of Israel's march. It belongs to the Torah, the five books that form the foundation of the Old Testament, and it reads as a continuation of Exodus and Leviticus, with the same leaders: Moses, the reluctant prophet who speaks with God face to face; his brother Aaron, the high priest; and their sister Miriam. Alongside them stand younger men who will matter later, especially Joshua son of Nun, Moses' assistant, and Caleb of the tribe of Judah.

The book moves through three settings. The first ten chapters take place at Sinai. The tribes are counted and arranged around the tabernacle, the Levites are given their duties, and the camp is prepared like an army about to march. Then the cloud lifts from the tabernacle and Israel sets out.

The middle of the book follows them through the wilderness, the dry, rugged country of the Sinai peninsula and the Negev, the arid south of Canaan. Almost at once the complaints begin: about the hardship, about the food, about Moses himself. They reach the oasis of Kadesh, on the southern edge of the promised land, and twelve men are sent to explore it. Ten return frightened, and the people refuse to go in. It is the great turning point of the book. God declares that the generation who came out of Egypt will die in the wilderness, and that only their children, with Caleb and Joshua, will enter the land. The years that follow are passed over almost in silence, broken only by rebellion, plague and the deaths of Miriam and Aaron.

The final section is set on the plains of Moab, east of the Jordan River, opposite Jericho. A new generation has grown up. Israel defeats the kings who stand in its way, and the king of Moab hires a famous seer named Balaam to curse them, with results he did not expect. A second census counts the people who will actually enter Canaan, and Joshua is appointed to succeed Moses. The book closes with arrangements for the land itself: how it will be divided, where its borders will run, and how its towns will be shared.

Numbers mixes story and law more freely than any book of the Torah. A narrative of rebellion is followed by instructions about offerings, as if to say that the promise still stands and the life of worship will go on. Its people are painfully recognisable: tired, frightened, nostalgic for Egypt, quick to blame their leaders. Even Moses fails at a critical moment. Yet through it all the cloud of God's presence goes before them, the manna falls each morning, and the God who brought them out of Egypt keeps leading them on.

Later Scripture returned to these chapters often. The psalms remember the wilderness years, and in the New Testament Jesus, Paul and the letter to the Hebrews all draw on events in this book. When Numbers ends, Israel is waiting on the plains of Moab, and the book of Deuteronomy records the words Moses speaks to them there, before they cross the Jordan without him.

=== 1
The book begins with a date and a place. It is the first day of the second month of the second year after the exodus, and the Lord speaks to Moses in the Tent of Meeting, in the Wilderness of Sinai. The tabernacle has stood in the middle of the camp for one month.

Moses receives an order that sounds military, because it is. He and Aaron are to count every man twenty years old or more who is able to serve in Israel's army, tribe by tribe. A leader from each tribe is named to help them, and these names will return in the chapters ahead.

The census itself is set out in a steady, formal pattern, the same words repeated for each of the twelve tribes, with Joseph's line counted as two, Ephraim and Manasseh. The total is large, and its meaning is plain: the family of seventy who went down into Egypt has become a nation. One tribe is deliberately left out. The Levites are not to be counted for war. Their task is to carry the tabernacle, set it up, take it down and camp around it as its guardians.

=== 2
The fighting men have been counted, and now they are arranged. This chapter gives the plan of the camp, and it is drawn with the precision of a military diagram.

At the centre stands the Tent of Meeting. Around it, at a distance, the twelve tribes camp in four groups of three, each under its own standard and family banners. Judah takes the place of honour on the east, toward the sunrise, where the entrance of the tabernacle faces, with Issachar and Zebulun beside it. Reuben, Simeon and Gad camp on the south, Ephraim, Manasseh and Benjamin on the west, and Dan, Asher and Naphtali on the north. The Levites travel in the middle, with the tabernacle.

The same order governs the march. Judah's camp sets out first and Dan's camp last. The tribal leaders and numbers from the census are repeated, group by group. The picture is of a people organised around the presence of God, with the Lord's dwelling quite literally at the heart of their life.

=== 3
With the twelve tribes in their places, attention turns to the tribe that was set apart: Levi. The chapter opens by recalling Aaron's four sons. Nadab and Abihu died in the wilderness of Sinai when they offered unauthorised fire before the Lord, an event told in Leviticus, and so only Eleazar and Ithamar now serve as priests beside their father.

The Levites are given to Aaron as assistants. They do not serve as priests themselves, but they guard and care for the tabernacle on behalf of the whole people. The reason given reaches back to the night of the exodus. When God struck down the firstborn of Egypt, he claimed every firstborn of Israel as his own. Now he takes the Levites in their place.

The Levites are counted, every male a month old or more, by their three clans, descended from Levi's sons Gershon, Kohath and Merari. Each clan is given a side of the tabernacle to camp on and a share of its care. Then the firstborn of Israel are counted too, and the two totals are compared. The few firstborn left over are redeemed with a payment of silver, given to Aaron and his sons.

=== 4
The Levite clans now receive their working orders. A second count is made, this time only of men between thirty and fifty years old, the age of those who will actually do the heavy work of moving the tabernacle.

The Kohathites come first, and their duty is the most sacred and the most dangerous. They carry the holiest objects: the ark of the Testimony, the table of the Presence, the lampstand and the altars. Before they may approach, Aaron and his sons must go in, take down the veil, and wrap each object in its coverings of blue, scarlet and purple cloth and fine leather. The Kohathites must not touch the holy things or even look at them, or they will die. Eleazar, Aaron's son, oversees the oil, the incense and the whole tabernacle.

The Gershonites carry the curtains, coverings and screens, and the Merarites the frames, posts, bases, pegs and ropes, both under the direction of Ithamar, Aaron's younger son. By the end of the chapter every part of the tabernacle has someone appointed to carry it.

=== 5
The camp has been ordered around the tabernacle. Now the Lord speaks about keeping that camp fit for his presence, because, as he says, he dwells among them.

The chapter gathers three sets of instructions. First, those who are ritually unclean, through a skin disease, a bodily discharge or contact with a dead body, are to live outside the camp for a time. Second, anyone who wrongs another person is to confess, repay in full, and add a fifth, because a wrong against a neighbour is also unfaithfulness toward the Lord.

The longest section deals with a husband who suspects his wife of adultery when there is no witness and no proof. Rather than leave the matter to rumour or to the husband's anger, the case is brought to the priest at the sanctuary, and the woman takes a solemn oath before the Lord, accompanied by an offering and a drink of holy water. To modern listeners the ceremony is strange, and it belongs to a world very different from our own. What it does make clear is that such a suspicion is to be settled before God, not by the husband alone.

=== 6
From the purity of the whole camp, the instructions turn to individuals who choose to give themselves to the Lord in a special way. This is the vow of the Nazirite, from a Hebrew word meaning separated or consecrated, and it is open to men and women alike.

For the length of the vow, a Nazirite avoids wine and anything from the grapevine, lets the hair grow uncut, and stays away from dead bodies, even those of close family. The chapter explains what happens if the vow is broken by accident, when someone dies suddenly nearby, and how it is brought to a close with offerings at the Tent of Meeting, when the consecrated hair is shaved and burned. Later in Israel's history, Samson and Samuel will be dedicated in a similar way from birth.

The chapter ends with some of the best-known words in the Bible. The Lord gives Aaron and his sons the words with which they are to bless the people of Israel, three short lines that ask the Lord to bless and keep them, to make his face shine on them, and to give them peace. Through this blessing, the Lord says, they will put his name on the Israelites.

=== 7
This chapter steps back in time. It returns to the day, a month before the census, when Moses finished setting up the tabernacle and anointed it and all its furnishings. On that occasion the twelve tribal leaders, the same men who helped with the count, came forward with gifts.

Their first gift is practical: six covered carts and twelve oxen, given to the Levites for moving the tabernacle. The Gershonites and Merarites, who carry the heavy curtains and frames, receive them. The Kohathites receive none, because the holiest objects are to be carried on their shoulders.

Then, for the dedication of the altar, the leaders bring their offerings one at a time, a tribe a day for twelve days, beginning with Nahshon of Judah. It is the longest chapter in the Torah, and the reason is that each leader's gift is identical and each is recorded in full: silver platter, silver bowl, gold dish of incense, and animals for sacrifice. In the ancient world such repetition was a way of honouring each tribe equally, so that none was a footnote. The chapter ends quietly, with Moses entering the Tent of Meeting and hearing the voice of the Lord speaking to him from above the ark.

=== 8
The dedication of the altar is complete, and the narrative turns first to the light inside the sanctuary. Aaron is instructed to set up the seven lamps of the golden lampstand so that they shine forward, and the chapter recalls that the lampstand was hammered from gold to the pattern God showed Moses on the mountain.

Then the Levites, who have been counted and given their duties, are formally set apart for service. They are sprinkled with the water of purification, shave their bodies and wash their clothes. The whole congregation gathers, and the Israelites lay their hands on the Levites, a sign that this tribe now stands before the Lord on behalf of all the others. Aaron presents them as a wave offering, a gesture usually performed with a sacrifice held up before the altar, here applied to a whole tribe of living people.

The reason is repeated from earlier: the Levites are taken in place of Israel's firstborn, whom God claimed on the night of the exodus. The chapter closes with the ages of service. The Levites begin work at twenty-five, retire from heavy duties at fifty, and afterwards may still assist their younger brothers.

=== 9
Like the dedication of the altar, the opening of this chapter looks back a little. It is the first month of the second year, a full year since the night Israel left Egypt, and the Lord tells Moses that the people are to keep the Passover at its appointed time, on the fourteenth day of the month. It is the first anniversary of their deliverance.

A practical problem arises. Some men have become unclean through contact with a dead body, and so they cannot take part. They come to Moses and Aaron and ask why they should be shut out. Moses does not answer on his own authority. He waits to hear from the Lord, and the answer establishes a second Passover, a month later, for anyone who is unclean or away on a journey. Foreigners living among Israel may also keep it on the same terms.

The second half of the chapter describes the cloud over the tabernacle, which looks like fire by night. When it lifts, Israel sets out. When it settles, they camp, whether for a night, a few days, a month or longer. Israel is about to leave Sinai, and there is no doubt who will be leading.

=== 10
Israel's long stay at Sinai is about to end. First Moses is told to make two silver trumpets. Their blasts will summon the congregation or its leaders, signal each camp to set out, sound the alarm in battle, and mark the feasts and the new moons.

Then, on the twentieth day of the second month of the second year, the cloud lifts from the tabernacle. Israel has been at the mountain for nearly eleven months. For the first time the camp marches in the order laid out in the opening chapters: Judah first, with Nahshon in command, the tabernacle and its carriers in the middle, Dan bringing up the rear. Their direction is the Wilderness of Paran, the vast, dry region to the north, toward Canaan.

Before they go, Moses appeals to Hobab, the son of his Midianite father-in-law, here called Reuel, to come with them as a guide who knows where to camp in the desert. The chapter does not record his final answer. It ends with the ark going ahead of the people, and with the words Moses spoke each time it set out and each time it came to rest.

=== 11
The march has begun, and the chapters of careful order give way to something else. Soon after leaving Sinai, the people start to complain. From here on, much of the story of Israel in the wilderness will be a story of complaint, rebellion and God's patience under strain.

The first outbreak is brief. Fire from the Lord burns the edge of the camp until Moses prays, and the place is named Taberah, burning. Then a larger crisis begins among the rabble, the mixed crowd that came out of Egypt with Israel. They crave meat, and soon the whole people are weeping in their tents, remembering the fish, cucumbers, melons, leeks, onions and garlic of Egypt, and despising the manna God sends each morning.

What follows shows Moses at breaking point. He tells the Lord the burden of this people is too heavy for him and asks to die. God answers in two ways. He shares the weight of leadership by placing his Spirit on seventy elders, and he promises the people meat until they are sick of it. The chapter introduces Joshua son of Nun, Moses' assistant since his youth, protective of his master's authority. And it ends with a name that will be remembered: Kibroth-hattaavah, the graves of craving.

=== 12
The people have moved on to Hazeroth, and now the challenge to Moses comes from inside his own family. His sister Miriam, the prophetess who led the women in song after the crossing of the sea, and his brother Aaron, the high priest, begin to criticise him. The spark is Moses' marriage to a Cushite woman. Cush usually means the land to the south of Egypt, and the text does not say whether this is Zipporah, the Midianite wife Moses married in Exodus, or a second wife.

The marriage, though, is only the occasion. The real complaint comes out at once: has the Lord spoken only through Moses? Has he not spoken through us too? The narrator adds a comment about Moses himself, that he was very humble, more than anyone on the face of the earth, as if to explain why he says nothing in his own defence.

Instead the Lord intervenes. He summons all three to the Tent of Meeting and speaks of what sets Moses apart from every other prophet. The consequences fall on Miriam, and the chapter ends with the whole camp waiting for her before they move on into the Wilderness of Paran.

=== 13
Israel is now camped in the Wilderness of Paran, at the southern edge of the land God promised, and the oasis of Kadesh will be their base.

At the Lord's command, Moses sends twelve men to explore Canaan, one leader from each tribe. Two names stand out from the list. Caleb son of Jephunneh represents Judah. Hoshea son of Nun represents Ephraim, and here Moses gives him the name by which he is better known, Joshua, meaning the Lord saves. They are to see whether the people are strong or weak, whether the towns are walled, whether the soil is rich, and bring back some fruit. It is the season of the first ripe grapes.

For forty days they travel the length of the land, from the Wilderness of Zin in the south to Rehob in the far north. They pass through Hebron, where Abraham once lived and where the patriarchs are buried, and which is now home to the descendants of Anak, a people remembered for their great size. The chapter ends with the spies back at Kadesh, carrying a cluster of grapes so heavy it hangs from a pole between two men, and with a report that begins well and ends in fear.

=== 14
The spies have given their report, and ten of them have convinced the people that Canaan cannot be taken. Caleb's attempt to quiet the crowd has failed. Now the whole congregation weeps through the night, and by morning there is talk of choosing a new leader and going back to Egypt.

This is the crisis to which the whole book has been moving. Moses and Aaron fall face down before the assembly. Joshua and Caleb tear their clothes and plead with the people not to rebel and not to fear, because the Lord is with them. The answer is a threat to stone them.

Then the glory of the Lord appears at the Tent of Meeting. What follows echoes the golden calf at Sinai, when God threatened to destroy the people and begin again with Moses, and Moses interceded for them. Here Moses prays again, appealing to God's reputation among the nations and to the words God used to describe himself on the mountain: slow to anger and abounding in loving devotion. The Lord pardons, but there will be a heavy consequence, measured out in years. The chapter ends with a doomed attempt to undo the damage, on the slopes of the hill country, near a place called Hormah.

=== 15
After the disaster at Kadesh, the book does something surprising. The narrative pauses, and the Lord gives Moses laws that begin with the words: after you enter the land that I am giving you. The generation that refused to go in has just been told they will die in the wilderness, yet the promise to their children still stands, and these instructions assume it.

The first laws concern the grain, oil and wine that are to accompany burnt offerings and sacrifices, products of a settled farming life in Canaan, and the first portion of dough from each baking. Foreigners living among Israel are to bring their offerings in the same way. Then come provisions for sins committed unintentionally, by the whole community or by one person, with a sharp contrast: the one who sins defiantly, with a high hand, despises the word of the Lord.

That contrast is followed by a case from the wilderness years, a man found gathering wood on the Sabbath, which shows how seriously such defiance was treated. The chapter ends with tassels, blue cords on the corners of every garment, so that Israel will look at them and remember the commandments.

=== 16
The book returns to the wilderness and to the most serious challenge yet to Moses and Aaron. It comes from two directions at once. Korah is a Levite of the Kohathite clan, the clan entrusted with carrying the most holy things, and he is a cousin of Moses and Aaron. Alongside him stand three men from the tribe of Reuben, Dathan, Abiram and On, and two hundred and fifty respected leaders of the congregation.

Their charge is that Moses and Aaron have raised themselves too high, since the whole community is holy. Korah's ambition, Moses says, is the priesthood itself. Dathan and Abiram refuse even to come when summoned, and their reply turns the story upside down: it was Egypt, they say, that was a land flowing with milk and honey.

Moses proposes a test. Korah and his men are to bring censers with fire and incense before the Lord, alongside Aaron, and the Lord will show whom he has chosen. What follows is one of the most fearful scenes in the Torah. The next day the whole congregation turns against Moses and Aaron, and it is Aaron, censer in hand, who runs into the crowd and stands between the living and the dead.

=== 17
The rebellion of Korah has been crushed, but the question behind it has not gone away: who has God chosen to serve as priest? This short chapter settles it in a way no one could dispute.

Each of the twelve tribal leaders brings a staff, a walking stick or rod that served as a sign of authority, with his name written on it. Aaron's name is written on the staff of Levi. The staffs are placed overnight in the Tent of Meeting before the ark of the Testimony, and the Lord says that the staff of the man he chooses will sprout. In the morning Moses goes in, and what he finds is more than a sprout.

Aaron's staff is then kept before the ark as a lasting sign for the rebellious. The chapter ends with the people overwhelmed with fear, crying out that anyone who comes near the tabernacle will die. It is a real question after so many deaths, and the next chapter gives the Lord's answer.

=== 18
The people's frightened question at the end of the last chapter was whether anyone could come near the tabernacle and live. The Lord now answers, and for once he speaks directly to Aaron.

The answer is a system of protection. Aaron and his sons, the priests, are to bear responsibility for the sanctuary and the altar, and to guard what lies behind the veil. Their fellow Levites will assist them and keep watch over the Tent, but they must not touch the holy furnishings or the altar. Ordinary Israelites will no longer approach. In this way the holiness of God's dwelling is guarded, so that wrath does not break out on the people again.

The second half of the chapter explains how this tribe will live, since the priests and Levites will receive no territory when the land is divided. The Lord himself is their portion. The priests receive their share of the holy offerings, the firstfruits and the redemption price of the firstborn, by what is called a covenant of salt, an image of something preserved and lasting. The Levites receive the tithes of Israel, and from those tithes they in turn give a tenth to the priests.

=== 19
The deaths of the recent chapters, at Kibroth-hattaavah, after the spies' report and in the rebellion of Korah, have left the camp full of the dead. Under the laws of purity, contact with a dead body made a person unclean, unable to come near the sanctuary. This chapter provides the remedy.

It begins with an unusual sacrifice. A red heifer, a young cow without defect that has never worn a yoke, is brought to Eleazar, Aaron's son, slaughtered outside the camp and burned completely, together with cedar wood, hyssop and scarlet wool. Its ashes are gathered and kept outside the camp. Mixed with fresh water, they make the water of purification.

The rest of the chapter applies it. Anyone who touches a corpse, a human bone or a grave, or who is in a tent when someone dies, is unclean for seven days and must be sprinkled with this water on the third and seventh days. One of the paradoxes of the ritual is that those who prepare the ashes and the water are themselves made unclean until evening. The letter to the Hebrews would later recall these ashes in its reflection on cleansing.

=== 20
Israel is back at Kadesh, in the Wilderness of Zin. The chapter names the first month but not the year, and the long years of wandering have passed almost without a word. Aaron's death, at the end of this chapter, is later dated to the fortieth year after the exodus, so most of the generation that refused to enter the land is now gone. The chapter opens with another death. Miriam, the sister of Moses and Aaron, dies at Kadesh and is buried there.

Then comes a familiar crisis. There is no water, and the people quarrel with Moses in words that echo their parents. This time the Lord tells Moses to take the staff and speak to the rock in front of the people. What Moses does instead costs him and Aaron the promised land.

The rest of the chapter turns toward the journey ahead. The direct route north is closed, so Moses asks the king of Edom, descended from Jacob's brother Esau, for permission to pass along the King's Highway, the main road east of the Jordan rift. Edom refuses. The chapter closes on Mount Hor, near Edom's border, where Aaron's priestly garments are placed on his son Eleazar.

=== 21
The new generation now begins to win battles. When the Canaanite king of Arad, in the Negev, attacks and takes captives, Israel calls on the Lord and defeats him. The place is named Hormah, the very name of the place where their parents were routed after Kadesh.

Edom's refusal forces a long detour around its territory, and the people grow impatient and speak against God and Moses, despising the manna once more. Venomous snakes appear in the camp. When the people confess, God tells Moses to make a snake and put it on a pole, so that anyone bitten may look at it and live. Centuries later, the bronze snake would be destroyed by King Hezekiah because Israel had begun to worship it, and in John's Gospel Jesus uses this scene to describe his own lifting up.

From here the journey moves north through the lands east of the Dead Sea, with a rapid run of place names, a fragment from an old collection called the Book of the Wars of the Lord, and a song about a well. Two kings stand in the way: Sihon, king of the Amorites, whose capital is Heshbon, and Og, king of Bashan, the fertile highland farther north. By the end of the chapter, both their lands are in Israel's hands.

=== 22
With Sihon and Og defeated, Israel moves to the place where it will stay for the rest of the book and all of Deuteronomy: the plains of Moab, on the eastern side of the Jordan River, opposite Jericho. The camp looks across the valley at the promised land.

The Moabites, descendants of Abraham's nephew Lot, watch with dread. Their king, Balak son of Zippor, has seen what happened to the Amorites and does not trust his army to do better. So he turns to the spiritual weapons of his world. He sends elders of Moab and of Midian, Moab's neighbours, with fees for divination, to summon Balaam son of Beor from Pethor, near the Euphrates, far to the north. Balaam is a famous seer, and Balak believes that those he curses are cursed. Remarkably, a plaster inscription found at Deir Alla in Jordan, written some centuries later, also speaks of a seer called Balaam son of Beor, which suggests how widely his name was known.

For the next three chapters Israel itself barely appears. The story is told from the hills of Moab, and it opens with Balaam's hesitation, God's warnings, and a journey interrupted by an angel with a drawn sword, which the seer cannot see but his donkey can.

=== 23
Balaam has arrived in Moab and been welcomed by King Balak, and from the heights of Bamoth-baal the two men look down on the outskirts of Israel's camp. Balak is a king paying for a curse. Balaam has already warned him that he can speak only the words God puts in his mouth.

The ritual is carefully prepared. Seven altars are built, and on each a bull and a ram are offered. Balaam goes off alone to a barren height to see whether the Lord will meet him, and then he returns and speaks. His words are poetry, the first of a series of oracles, and they are not what Balak paid for.

The pattern then repeats. Balak, unwilling to give up, takes Balaam to a second vantage point, the top of Pisgah, hoping that a different view of the camp will produce a different result. Again seven altars, again the sacrifices, again a message from the Lord. The second oracle contains one of the most quoted lines of the book: God is not a man, that he should lie. By the end of the chapter, Balak is ready to try a third place, the top of Peor.

=== 24
Balak and Balaam stand on the top of Peor, beside seven more altars. Something has changed in the seer. He no longer goes aside to look for omens, as he did before. He simply turns toward the wilderness, where Israel's tents are spread out tribe by tribe below him, and the Spirit of God comes on him.

His third oracle is a picture of Israel flourishing, its tents spread out like gardens beside a river, and it ends with an echo of God's promise to Abraham: blessed are those who bless you, and cursed are those who curse you. Balak strikes his hands together in fury and dismisses him without payment.

But Balaam is not finished. Before he leaves he speaks a fourth oracle, about what Israel will do to Moab in days to come, and it looks far beyond the present moment: a star will come out of Jacob, and a sceptre will rise from Israel. Its words find a first fulfilment in David, who would one day conquer Moab and Edom, and they point further still, to a greater king to come. Brief words follow about Amalek, the Kenites and distant powers, and then the two men go their separate ways.

=== 25
Balaam's oracles have blessed Israel from the hills above the camp. Now the scene shifts down to the camp itself, at Shittim on the plains of Moab, and what happens there is the opposite of the picture Balaam painted.

Israelite men begin to sleep with Moabite women, who invite them to the sacrifices for their gods. Soon Israel is eating the sacrifices and bowing down to Baal of Peor, the local form of the Canaanite storm god Baal, worshipped at the mountain where Balaam stood. Moab's king could not curse Israel. Israel is now bringing judgement on itself. Only later, in chapter thirty-one, does the book reveal who gave the advice behind it.

A plague breaks out in the camp. While the people weep at the entrance of the Tent of Meeting, an Israelite leader openly brings a Midianite woman into his family's tent. Phinehas, the grandson of Aaron and son of the new high priest Eleazar, acts at once. The Lord grants Phinehas a covenant of lasting priesthood, and commands Israel to treat the Midianites as enemies. This is the last great rebellion of the wilderness years, and with it the old generation passes from the story.

=== 26
The plague that followed the sin at Peor has ended, and the book reaches a new beginning. Almost forty years after the first census at Sinai, the Lord tells Moses and Eleazar, now high priest in Aaron's place, to count Israel's fighting men again. The setting is the plains of Moab, by the Jordan, across from Jericho.

The pattern deliberately mirrors the opening of the book. Tribe by tribe, the men of twenty and over are registered, this time with the clans of each tribe named. Along the way the list pauses to remember the past: Dathan and Abiram swallowed by the earth with Korah, though Korah's line did not die out; Judah's sons Er and Onan, who died in Canaan; the five daughters of a man named Zelophehad, who had no sons and who will speak for themselves in the next chapter. Some tribes have grown and others shrunk, Simeon most sharply, but the total is close to the first census.

The purpose of this count is no longer only the army. It is the inheritance, since the land is to be divided according to the size of each tribe. The chapter ends with a quiet, weighty sentence. Of all the men counted at Sinai, not one remains, except Caleb son of Jephunneh and Joshua son of Nun.

=== 27
The new census has been taken with the division of the land in view, and that raises a practical question. Five sisters, the daughters of Zelophehad of the tribe of Manasseh, come to the entrance of the Tent of Meeting and stand before Moses, Eleazar, the leaders and the whole congregation. Their names are Mahlah, Noah, Hoglah, Milcah and Tirzah. Their father died in the wilderness, they explain, not as one of Korah's rebels but for his own sin, and he had no sons. Must his name disappear from his clan simply because he had daughters?

Moses takes the case to the Lord, and the answer creates a new law of inheritance for all Israel.

Then the chapter turns to Moses himself. The Lord tells him to climb a mountain in the Abarim range, the heights east of the Dead Sea, and look at the land he will not enter, because of what happened at Meribah. Moses' response is not a plea for himself but a prayer for the people, that they will not be like sheep without a shepherd. The Lord names his successor, Joshua son of Nun, a man in whom is the Spirit, and Moses lays his hands on him before Eleazar and the whole congregation.

=== 28
A new leader has been commissioned, and the land is within sight. Before the conquest begins, the Lord sets out the regular offerings that Israel as a whole is to bring throughout the year. These two chapters read like a calendar of worship, a public timetable for the altar.

Many of the feasts were introduced in Exodus and Leviticus. What these chapters add is the precise list of animals, grain, oil and wine for each occasion. It begins with the daily offering, a lamb in the morning and another at twilight, the steady heartbeat of the sanctuary, first established at Sinai. Then come the extra offerings for each Sabbath, for the beginning of every month at the new moon, and for the great spring festivals: Passover, on the fourteenth day of the first month, followed by the seven days of the Feast of Unleavened Bread, and later the Feast of Weeks, when the first of the grain harvest is brought to the Lord.

The repetition of numbers and measures is part of the point. The new generation is being told exactly how the life of worship will go on, day after day and season after season, once they are in the land.

=== 29
The calendar of offerings continues with the seventh month, in the autumn, the busiest month of Israel's sacred year. It opens on the first day with a sacred assembly and the sounding of trumpets. On the tenth day comes the day on which the people are to humble themselves, the Day of Atonement described in Leviticus.

Then, from the fifteenth day, comes the great harvest festival, a feast of seven days that Leviticus calls the Feast of Tabernacles, when Israel lived in shelters to remember the wilderness. It receives more offerings than any other occasion in the year. On its first day, thirteen bulls are offered, and the number falls by one each day, until seven are offered on the seventh day. An eighth day of solemn assembly follows.

The chapter closes by reminding Israel that these are only the fixed public offerings, in addition to everything individuals bring to fulfil their own vows or as freewill gifts.

=== 30
The last chapter ended with vows, and this one takes up the subject in detail. Moses speaks to the heads of the tribes about promises made to the Lord.

The principle is stated first, simply and absolutely. When a man makes a vow or binds himself by an oath, he must not break his word. Then the chapter works through the situation of a woman, whose vow could affect the household she belonged to in that society. A young woman still living in her father's house, a wife, and a widow or divorced woman are each considered in turn. A father or husband who hears of a vow may cancel it on the day he hears it, and if he does, the Lord releases her. If he says nothing, it stands. A widow or divorced woman answers for her own vows.

The chapter is brief and legal in tone. Set between the commissioning of Joshua and the war against Midian, it reflects how seriously Israel was to treat words spoken before God.

=== 31
Back in chapter twenty-five, after the sin at Peor, the Lord told Moses to treat the Midianites as enemies. Now the order comes to carry it out, and with it a sobering note: after this, Moses will be gathered to his people. It is the last campaign he will oversee.

A thousand men are sent from each tribe, twelve thousand in all. They are accompanied not by Joshua but by Phinehas the priest, carrying holy articles and the trumpets for signalling, a sign that this is the Lord's battle. Among the dead are five kings of Midian and, unexpectedly, Balaam son of Beor. Only now does the book reveal that it was Balaam's counsel that led Israel astray at Peor. The seer who could not curse Israel found another way to harm them.

This is one of the hardest chapters of the Torah for modern readers, and Moses' orders on the army's return are severe. The rest of the chapter deals with the aftermath: purifying the soldiers and their spoils, dividing the plunder between the fighters and the whole community, with a share for the priests and Levites, and an offering of gold from commanders who find that not one of their men is missing.

=== 32
Israel now holds the lands east of the Jordan taken from Sihon and Og, and two tribes look at them with a herdsman's eye. The tribes of Reuben and Gad have very large herds and flocks, and the territory of Jazer and Gilead, the high pastureland east of the river, is ideal for livestock. They come to Moses, Eleazar and the leaders with a request: let us settle here, and do not make us cross the Jordan.

To Moses it sounds dangerously familiar. He hears in it an echo of Kadesh-barnea, when the spies discouraged the people and a whole generation was lost, and his reply is stern. Are your brothers to go to war while you sit here?

In the negotiation that follows, the two tribes offer a solemn promise, and Moses sets the terms, warning them that their sin will find them out if they fail. The arrangement is passed on to Eleazar and to Joshua, who will see it carried out. By the end of the chapter, half of the tribe of Manasseh has been included too, and these eastern lands will become the homeland of two and a half tribes of Israel.

=== 33
As the long march nears its end, the book pauses to look back over the whole route. At the Lord's command, the chapter says, Moses recorded the stages of Israel's journey, and here they are, from Rameses in Egypt to the plains of Moab.

It begins with the night of the exodus, when Israel marched out boldly while the Egyptians buried their firstborn. Then it lists the camps one by one: Succoth, the crossing of the sea, Marah and Elim, Sinai, and on through some forty stopping places. Many of these names appear nowhere else in Scripture. They fill in the long silent years between Sinai and the second arrival at Kadesh. The list pauses only once, for the death of Aaron on Mount Hor, in the fortieth year after the exodus, at the age of a hundred and twenty-three.

When the itinerary reaches the plains of Moab, the chapter turns from the past to the future. The Lord gives instructions for the land across the Jordan: drive out its inhabitants, destroy their idols and high places, and divide the land by lot. And there is a warning. Any who are allowed to remain will become barbs in Israel's eyes and thorns in their sides.

=== 34
The Lord has told Israel to take possession of the land and divide it by lot. Now he draws its borders, as if tracing a map for a people who have never lived there.

The southern border runs from the Wilderness of Zin along the edge of Edom, from the southern end of the Salt Sea, which we call the Dead Sea, past Kadesh-barnea and along the Brook of Egypt, a seasonal stream bed in the desert, to the Mediterranean, here called the Great Sea. The coast forms the western border. The northern line runs from the sea toward Lebo-hamath, in the region of modern Lebanon and Syria, and the eastern border comes down past the Sea of Chinnereth, later known as the Sea of Galilee, and along the Jordan back to the Salt Sea. The outline is clear. This is the land west of the Jordan, for the nine and a half tribes still waiting for their share.

The chapter ends with the men who will oversee the division: Eleazar the priest and Joshua son of Nun, with a leader from each tribe. Only one name on the list is familiar. Representing Judah, as he did among the spies almost forty years before, is Caleb son of Jephunneh.

=== 35
The land has been mapped, and each tribe will receive its share. But the Levites, as the book has already explained, will receive no territory of their own. Instead, this chapter provides them with towns to live in, forty-eight in all, scattered through the lands of the other tribes, each with pastureland around it for their herds and flocks.

Six of these Levite towns are to serve a special purpose. They will be cities of refuge, three east of the Jordan and three in Canaan. In the ancient world, when someone was killed, it fell to a close relative, called the avenger of blood, to take the life of the killer. These cities offer protection to a person who has killed someone by accident, until the community can hear the case.

The chapter draws careful distinctions between murder and accidental killing: the weapon used, whether there was hostility, whether it was planned. The one who killed unintentionally must stay in the city of refuge until the death of the high priest. Behind it all lies a conviction stated in the final lines, that bloodshed pollutes the land, and this is the land where the Lord himself will dwell among his people.

=== 36
The book ends by returning to the five daughters of Zelophehad, whose request for their father's land was granted in chapter twenty-seven. Now the leaders of their clan, the family of Gilead in the tribe of Manasseh, raise a further question.

If these women marry men from other tribes, the land they inherit will pass with their children to those tribes, and Manasseh's territory will shrink. Even the Jubilee, the fiftieth year when land returned to its original family, would not bring it back, because it would by then belong to another tribe by inheritance. The concern is for each tribe to keep the land God gives it.

Moses brings the answer from the Lord. The daughters may marry whoever they please, as long as they marry within their father's tribe. They do, marrying cousins on their father's side. It is a quiet ending to a book of turbulent events, and it looks forward with confidence. The land has not yet been entered, yet its future ownership is already being settled. The last sentence places these laws where the whole second half of the book has stood: on the plains of Moab, by the Jordan, across from Jericho.
